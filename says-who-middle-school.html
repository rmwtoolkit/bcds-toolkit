Says Who?

Some of what you see online is true, some is selling, and some is made up. Here's how to tell the difference in about a minute.

Part 1 · Understand it

Not everything on your feed is what it looks like

Sound familiar?"Wait, is that real?"
  • A video says a celebrity died, and an hour later they post a selfie.
  • Your favorite creator loves a product, and there's a discount code in their bio.
  • An image looks real, and someone says it was made by AI.
  • A family group chat forwards a health tip that sounds a little off.
  • You watched one video about something, and now your whole feed is about it.
  • You saw something violent or upsetting, and it stuck with you.

If that sounds familiar, you're seeing what everyone sees. Telling real from fake is a skill, and most adults are still learning it too.

🧠 Why it's hard to tellFakes are made to look real.

Researchers at Stanford asked middle schoolers to look at a news site. More than 80 percent thought an ad labeled "sponsored content" was a real news story. The ads were designed to look like news, and they worked.

Your feed also learns what you stop on. Watch a few videos about one thing and the app shows you more, so it can start to feel like everyone believes it.

Before you believe it or share it, ask: says who, and what do they want?

Four kinds of posts

Most of your feed is one of these.

📰Real newsWho, what, when, where, and a source you can check.
💰SellingAds, sponsors, discount codes, "gifted" products.
🎭Made upFake stories, edited photos, AI images.
📢OpinionSomeone's take. Fine, as long as you know it's a take.

Plenty of posts mix two or three of these.

How creators get paidWhen they love a product, it might be a job.

Creators earn money when brands pay them to post, when you use their discount code, or when you buy through their link. US rules say they should label paid posts with words like "ad" or "sponsored," but plenty skip it or hide it.

They might really like it. They also have a reason to say so. You get to decide if it's worth your money.

The one-minute checkDo what fact-checkers do: leave the page.

Professional fact-checkers don't stare at a post trying to decide if it looks real. They open a new tab and search for the claim, the person, or the site. Researchers call this reading sideways.

  • Search the claim plus the word "fact check."
  • Look for the same story from two news sources you know.
  • For a photo, ask: where did this first show up?
When something online sticks with youYou can close it. You can talk about it.

Violent, scary, or gross videos can show up in your feed without warning, and they can replay in your head for days. That's your brain's normal reaction, not a sign something's wrong with you.

You can close the app, tap "not interested," and tell someone. Watching more of it to get used to it doesn't help. Seeing a lot of it can make real hurt feel less real, which is a reason to be choosy about what you watch.

Family chats and other languagesRumors travel in every language.

Fake health tips and scary rumors spread fast in family group chats, sometimes in languages that fact-checking sites cover less.

If you're the one in your family who's good with phones, you can help kindly. "I looked it up and found something different. Want to see?" works better than "That's fake." Grown-ups get fooled too, and nobody likes being corrected in front of everyone.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Check before you share

What actually helps

Try these this week.

Tap one to see how.

🔎 Ask: says who?

Who made this, and how would they know?

💰 Ask: what do they want?

Your click, your money, your vote, or your anger?

🗂 Open a new tab.

Search the claim. If no real news source has it, wait.

😡 Notice when you're mad.

Posts that make you furious get shared fastest. That's a reason to slow down.

🤖 Look twice at perfect photos.

Check hands, text, backgrounds, and where it first appeared.

🚫 Tap "not interested."

Your feed learns from what you skip, too.

Try itThree moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd actually do.

A video says a new energy drink "helps kids focus in school." The creator has a discount code.

Your aunt forwards a message to the family chat: "Drink hot water every 15 minutes to stop a virus."

A shocking clip of a fight at another school is going around, and everyone's sharing it.

Nobody catches every fake. Slowing down and checking once catches most of them.

👥 In a groupPick a corner. Then see what the research says.

👥 For advisory, homeroom or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move or point to a corner of the room: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. No one has to share.
  3. After each one, the leader taps it to show what research says.
👥 Four corners · 1 of 2"If lots of people shared it, it's probably true."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

No. Researchers studying Twitter found false stories spread farther and faster than true ones, mostly because they're more surprising. Shares tell you a post is exciting. They don't tell you it's true.

👥 Four corners · 2 of 2"You can tell an AI image just by looking closely."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not anymore, at least not reliably. Older AI images had weird hands and blurry text, but the tools keep improving. Checking where an image came from works better than staring at it.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Where do you see the most ads that don't look like ads?
  • Have you ever believed something online that turned out to be fake? How did you find out?
  • What do you do when something upsetting shows up in your feed?
Tip for the person leading

Students use different apps and some aren't allowed social media at all. Use examples from news sites and video platforms everyone can picture. Avoid showing real violent clips, even as examples.

When it's more than a weird post

  • Something you saw online keeps replaying in your head, or makes it hard to sleep.
  • You're seeing content about hurting yourself or others.
  • Someone online is pushing you to believe or do something that scares you.
  • You feel hopeless, or like you want to hurt yourself.

Close the app and talk to a counselor or an adult you trust. You don't have to explain it perfectly. If you feel like hurting yourself, call or text 988, any time.

Other ways in

People were faking things long before the internet. Plenty of stories show how.

📖 Read

The Wonderful Wizard of Oz · L. Frank Baum, 1900 Throwback

The great and powerful wizard turns out to be a man behind a curtain with a lot of special effects. Who's behind the curtain on your feed?

🎬 Watch

The Truman Show · Paramount, 1998

Truman doesn't know his whole life is a TV show, complete with actors slipping in product ads. Watch for the moment he starts asking questions. Rated PG.

🎧 Listen

Everybody's Free (To Wear Sunscreen) · Baz Luhrmann, 1999 Throwback

The words went viral by email, credited to a famous novelist who never wrote them. A newspaper columnist named Mary Schmich did. Ask a grown-up to play it.

🖼️ Look

The Cottingley Fairies · Elsie Wright and Frances Griffiths, 1917

Two girls took photos of "fairies" in their garden and fooled famous adults for decades. The fairies were paper cutouts. What's today's version?

✏️ Make your own

Make a fake ad for a ridiculous product, then add every trick you've seen creators use. Swap with a friend and spot each other's tricks.
