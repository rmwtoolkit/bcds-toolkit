The Inner Critic

A voice, not a verdict.

What's going on

The critic runs on your threat system. It treats a mistake as a threat to your standing and attacks you before anyone else can. Self-critical people tend to underuse the soothing system that balances it.

A small mistake→The threat system fires→The critic attacks first→Praise slides off→"It keeps me sharp"

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "Everyone thinks I don't deserve this."
  • "Nobody actually likes me."
  • "Why even try?"

🫀 Body

  • awake at 1 a.m. replaying it
  • a hot rush of shame
  • criticism that sticks for weeks

🔁 Behaviors

  • remembering the one note
  • assuming friends just tolerate you
  • brushing off compliments

Where to step in

Turn the critic into a coach.

  1. Notice it: "I'm having the thought that I'm an idiot" is a thought, not a verdict.
  2. Use your name: "Maya, you've done this before." Then check the evidence.
  3. Coach, don't condemn. Be honest about the mistake and specific about the fix.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why the critic is so convincing

Sound familiar?You would never talk to a friend like this.
  • You replay one comment you made in class at 1 a.m., three days later.
  • A coach gives you one note and ten compliments. You remember the note.
  • You quietly assume your friends are tolerating you.
  • You think the harsh voice keeps you sharp, and that without it you'd slack off.
  • Praise slides right off. Criticism sticks for weeks.

You've probably heard that the critic isn't telling the truth. This goes further: why it argues so well, what it's trying to do for you, and why research says kindness toward yourself makes you work harder, not less.

Three systems that are supposed to balance

Threat: protect

Scans for danger and gets you ready to defend. The inner critic runs on this system. It treats a mistake as a threat to your standing and attacks you before anyone else can.

Drive: achieve

Pushes you toward goals, rewards, and status. Useful fuel. When it gets fused with fear of failing, it starts feeding the critic.

Soothing: calm and connect

Comes online when you feel safe and cared for, by someone else or by yourself. Gilbert argues that self-critical people tend to underuse it, so the threat system runs unchecked.

The researchSelf-criticism feels motivating. It mostly isn't.
Self-compassion makes people try harder.
After failing a hard test, college students who were prompted to treat themselves with compassion studied longer for a retest than students prompted to boost their self-esteem, or given no prompt (Breines & Chen, 2012).
It's linked to less distress in teens.
A review of 19 studies with more than 7,000 adolescents found a large link between higher self-compassion and lower anxiety, depression, and stress (Marsh, Chan & MacBeth, 2018). These are correlations: a strong connection, not proof of cause.
People notice you far less than you think.
Students who walked into a room in an embarrassing T-shirt guessed about half the room noticed. Fewer than a quarter did (Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, 2000). Psychologists call it the spotlight effect.
Where the voice learned its linesSome of the critic's words were someone else's first.

The critic often repeats people: a coach, a sibling, a teacher, comments about your body, or messages about your race, size, gender, sexuality, or background that you've absorbed from the world around you.

Some families show love through high expectations and blunt feedback. That can be loving, and it can still leave a harsh voice behind. You can respect where a voice came from and still decide it doesn't get the last word.

Ask yourself: whose voice does this sound like?

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Turn the critic into a coach

The core skill

Get some distance from the voice.

You can't delete the critic. You can change how much it gets to decide.

Notice it"I'm having the thought that I'm an idiot" is different from "I'm an idiot." One is a thought. The other is a verdict.
Use your namePeople who prepped for a stressful speech by talking to themselves by name or as "you" felt less anxious and gave better speeches than people who used "I" (Kross et al., 2014). "Maya, you've done this before."
Check the evidenceFact or opinion? Would it hold up if a friend said it about themselves?
Coach, don't condemnA good coach is honest about the mistake and specific about the fix: "That intro was rushed. Slow down next time."

More tools. Tap to see how.

1 Don't try to shut it up.

In the classic "white bear" study, people told not to think about a white bear ended up thinking about it more (Wegner et al., 1987). Banning a thought can make it louder. Notice it, then redirect.

2 Keep an evidence file.

Wins, kind words, hard things you handled. The critic has a selective memory. Give yours a counterweight.

3 Audit your feed.

Notice which accounts leave you feeling worse about yourself. Mute a few for a week and see what changes.

4 Say it out loud to someone.

The critic sounds more convincing inside your head than it does spoken to a person who likes you.

Try itRewrite the critic as a coach.

Write down something your critic says. Then rewrite it as someone who's honest and on your side.

Try itThree moments. Which voice is talking?

Your SAT score comes back 80 points below your goal.

At practice, you miss an easy shot and hear a teammate laugh.

A friend has a small birthday dinner, and you weren't invited.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually use.

Myths that keep you stuckWhat the research says instead.
MYTH If I'm nice to myself, I'll get lazy.

The research points the other way. Self-compassion after failure goes with more effort to improve, not less.

MYTH Positive affirmations will fix it.

Telling yourself "I'm amazing" when you don't believe it can make the gap feel bigger. Aim for fair and kind, not glowing.

MYTH Everyone saw it.

The spotlight effect says most people noticed less than you think, and forgot faster.

MYTH The critic is the real me.

It's a learned habit of thinking. Habits can be relearned.

When to get more help

  • The critic is loud most of the day, most days.
  • You feel worthless or hopeless.
  • The critic is mostly about your body, food, or weight.
  • You're having thoughts of hurting yourself.

These are signs to talk to someone soon. A counselor can help. See Get help below.
