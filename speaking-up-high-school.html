Speaking Up

Freezing is getting stuck at a gate.

What's going on

Between seeing something and doing something are five gates: notice it, read it as a problem, take it on yourself, know what to do, do it. Freezing usually isn't a character flaw.

You notice it→"It's just a joke"→"Someone else will"→No words ready→You freeze, then replay it

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "It's just a joke."
  • "Someone else will deal with it."
  • "What if I'm next?"

🫀 Body

  • you laugh before you think
  • your voice shakes
  • replaying it all night

📱 In the group chat

  • laughing reactions rolling in
  • a side chat about one person
  • screenshots passed around

Where to step in

You don't need a speech. One move.

  1. Have your line ready before you need it: "Not funny." "Yo, come with me for a sec."
  2. Distract or delegate: pull the person away, or bring in a coach, teacher, or host.
  3. Delay: check in afterward. "I saw what happened. You okay?" It's never too late.

Get an adult right away if someone is being physically hurt or threatened. If it's happening now, call 911.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why good people stay quiet

Sound familiar?You saw it. You froze. Then you replayed it all night.
  • Someone in the locker room made a comment about a teammate's body, and you laughed before you thought about it.
  • A classmate mocked someone's accent, quietly enough that the teacher didn't hear.
  • The group chat keeps "joking" about a new student's religion.
  • At a party, a guy wouldn't leave a girl alone, and she kept looking around the room.
  • A friend said something racist "ironically," and everyone moved on.
  • You did say something once. Your voice shook, and afterward you were glad.

You already know the word upstander. The harder question is why it's so hard in the moment, and what makes the difference.

Five gates between seeing it and doing something

In 1970, psychologists Bibb Latané and John Darley mapped the steps a bystander has to get through before helping. Freezing usually isn't a character flaw. It's getting stuck at one of these gates.

The classic finding: in a 1968 study, students heard another student over an intercom seem to have a seizure. When they thought they were the only one listening, 85% went for help before the call cut off. When they thought four others could hear, 31% did.

The researchThe bystander effect is real. So is the comeback.
Crowds slow people down, but not always.
A 2011 review of studies with more than 7,700 people confirmed the bystander effect, and found it gets weaker when a situation is clearly dangerous and when the bystanders know each other. Friends who notice together act more.
In real life, someone usually steps in.
Researchers analyzed security-camera footage of 219 real public fights in the UK, the Netherlands, and South Africa. In 9 of 10, at least one bystander, usually several, did something to help. More people around made it more likely, not less.
Peers can stop it fast.
On school playgrounds, peers were present for 88% of bullying episodes but stepped in during only 19%. When they did step in, most of the time (57%) it stopped. Those were younger kids, and high school is more complicated, but the lesson travels: the people watching hold a lot of power.
Peers set the normWhen the right students take a stand, the climate shifts.

In a study of 56 New Jersey middle schools and more than 24,000 students, small groups of students were invited to take a public stand against conflict at school, in their own words and their own style. Over the next year, disciplinary reports of conflict at those schools fell 25% compared with other schools.

The effect was strongest when the group included students other students paid attention to. If people watch what you do, your "not cool" travels further than a poster ever will.

OnlineA reaction is still joining in.

Pile-ons in comments, screenshots passed around, side chats that exist to talk about one person, AI-edited photos. Online, every gate gets harder: it's easy not to notice, easy to call it a joke, and easy to assume someone else will report it.

The worst part: a like or a laughing reaction feels like nothing to you. To the person on the other end, every one counts.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Pick your move

Six ways to step in

You don't need a speech. You need one move.

Tap each one.

1 Distract.

Interrupt without confronting anyone. Ask a random question, spill something, or pull the person away: "hey, your friend's looking for you."

2 Delegate.

Bring in someone with more power: a coach, a teacher, the host, a friend people listen to. That's using your resources, not snitching.

3 Direct.

Short and flat: "Not funny." "Leave her alone." "Bro, stop." Only if it feels safe. One sentence, then move on.

4 Delay.

Check in afterward: "I saw what happened. You okay?" It's never too late, and people remember who did this.

5 Document.

Screenshot or note what happened so an adult can act on it. Don't share it anywhere else, and let the person who was targeted decide what happens next when you can.

6 Include.

If someone keeps ending up on the edge, wait for them, save them a seat, add them to the plan. Prevention counts.

What's safe to say can depend on who you are. A comment one student makes with no cost might cost another more, because of race, gender, size, being new, or how people already treat them. Pick the move that's safe for you. All six count.

Try itHave your line ready before you need it.

Gate 4 is "know what to do." Pick or write lines you'd actually say. Nothing is saved.

Try itThree moments. What's your move?

Pick a move that could help.

In the locker room, a teammate makes a comment about another player's body. A few guys laugh. The player goes quiet.

At a party, a guy keeps following a girl from your chemistry class. She looks uncomfortable and keeps scanning the room.

In a 30-person chat, someone posts a voice clip mocking a classmate's accent. Reactions start rolling in.

More than one answer works. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't helpThese usually backfire.
Assuming someone else will.

That's gate 3, and everyone in the room is stuck at it with you.

Getting physical.

It can escalate fast and get you hurt or suspended. Get an adult.

Forwarding it to show it's wrong.

Sharing it spreads it, even with good intentions. Save it for an adult instead.

Making it about you.

A big speech can put the target in a bigger spotlight. Short, then check on them.

Beating yourself up for past silence.

Everyone has frozen. What matters is the next time, and a late check-in still counts.

Get an adult right away if

  • Someone is being physically hurt or threatened. If it's happening now, call 911.
  • The same person is being targeted over and over.
  • Private or sexual images of someone are being shared, real or AI-made.
  • Someone says they want to hurt themselves or others. A counselor, a trusted adult, or 988 can help.
