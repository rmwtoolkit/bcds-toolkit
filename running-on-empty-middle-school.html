Part 1 · Understand it

Sleep and the teenage brain

Sound familiar?Tired all day. Wide awake at 11.
  • You're exhausted all day, then wide awake at 11 at night.
  • Your alarm feels like a personal attack.
  • You fell asleep in class, or came close.
  • You stayed up late on your phone and regretted it in the morning.
  • You sleep until noon on weekends and still feel tired Monday.
  • Small things make you cry or snap when you haven't slept enough.
  • Your sleep gets squeezed by things you can't skip: a shared room, helping at home, or a family schedule that runs late.

Teen sleep problems are incredibly common, and a lot of it is biology, not bad choices.

What's going on with teen sleep

Tap each one.

🕰Your clock shifted

During puberty, your body starts releasing the sleep hormone melatonin later at night. That's why you're not sleepy at 9 anymore.

⏰School starts early

Your body wants to sleep later, but school doesn't move. The gap leaves a lot of teens short on sleep every night.

📱Screens keep you up

Phones are engaging by design, and late-night light can push sleepiness back even further.

Why it hits so hardLess sleep makes everything harder.
You need more sleep than you think.
Sleep experts recommend 8 to 10 hours a night for teens. Many students get much less.
Sleep affects everything.
Mood, memory, focus, patience, athletic performance, how often you get sick. Short sleep makes all of them harder.
You can't fully catch up on weekends.
Sleeping until noon shifts your clock later again, which makes Sunday night and Monday morning even harder. Some call it social jet lag.
Being tired feels like being sad or anxious.
Not enough sleep can make emotions bigger and harder to manage. Sometimes the fix for a bad mood is a good night's sleep.
📱 Your phone and your sleepThe group chat can wait till morning.

Late-night scrolling, group chats, and gaming keep your brain switched on. Notifications can wake you up even when you're asleep. And it's hard to put down a phone when your friends are still awake and talking.

The worst part: when everyone's up late in the group chat, logging off can feel like missing out. But sleep is what makes the next day feel okay.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

Small changes, better nights.

Tap one to see how.

📵 Phone out of the bedroom.

Or at least across the room, on Do Not Disturb. This one change helps a lot of people.

🕘 A consistent wake-up time.

Waking up at about the same time every day, even weekends (within an hour or two), helps your clock settle.

🌙 A wind-down routine.

Same few steps every night: shower, dim lights, read, music, a prayer, or whatever calms you. It tells your brain sleep is coming. Sharing a room or a loud home? Earplugs, a sleep mask, or a fan can help.

☀️ Morning light.

Getting outside or near a window soon after waking helps reset your clock earlier.

☕ Watch the caffeine.

Caffeine can stay in your system for hours. Energy drinks or coffee after lunch can make it harder to fall asleep.

😴 Nap smart.

If you nap, keep it short (about 20 minutes) and before late afternoon, so it doesn't steal your nighttime sleep.

Try itWork backward from your alarm.

Pick when you need to wake up. This shows when you'd need to be asleep to get 9 hours, and when to start winding down.

Try itLate night choices. What's your move?

Pick the best move.

It's 10:30. You're in bed but your friends are still texting in the group chat.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese quick fixes usually backfire.
🛌 Sleeping in until noon on weekends.

A little extra is okay. Way later shifts your clock and makes Monday rough.

⚡ Energy drinks to get through.

They borrow energy from later and can make sleep worse.

📱 Scrolling in bed until you're tired.

The phone usually keeps you awake longer, not shorter.

🌀 Lying awake stressing about not sleeping.

If you can't sleep after a while, get up, do something calm in dim light, and try again.

When to get more help

  • You have trouble falling or staying asleep most nights for weeks.
  • You're exhausted even after a full night's sleep.
  • Worry or sad thoughts keep you up at night.
  • You've felt drowsy while driving. Never drive drowsy: pull over or get a ride.

A counselor, school nurse, or doctor can help. See Get help below.

Want to write more?

A reminder

Being tired isn't a personal failing. Your body is asking for something it really needs. Listening to it is one of the best things you can do for your mood, your grades, and yourself.

Other ways in

People have been writing about sleep for a long time. A few of these are from bedtime when you were little.

📖 Read

Goodnight Moon · Margaret Wise Brown, 1947 Throwback

A bunny says goodnight to everything in the room, one thing at a time. That's a wind-down routine. You've known one since you were small.

🎬 Watch

Bluey: Sleepytime · Ludo Studio, 2020 Throwback

Bingo wants to sleep the whole night in her own bed, and dreams she's floating through space. Seven minutes long, and lovely.

Headspace Guide to Sleep · Netflix, 2021

Short animated episodes on sleep science: phones at bedtime, why you can't fall asleep, dreams. Each one ends with a wind-down exercise. Rated TV-G.

🎧 Listen

Somewhere Over the Rainbow / What a Wonderful World · Israel Kamakawiwoʻole, 1993

One voice and a ukulele. Quiet enough for a wind-down routine.

Weightless · Marconi Union, 2011

An eight-minute track made with sound therapists to help people relax. Try it with the phone across the room.

🖼️ Look

Little Nemo in Slumberland · Winsor McCay, 1905

A comic strip more than a hundred years old. Every week Nemo has a wild dream, and in the last panel he wakes up in bed, sometimes on the floor.

✏️ Make your own

Use "Work backward from your alarm" to find your wind-down time. Then draw your routine as a comic strip, like Little Nemo, one panel per step, ending in bed.

Ratings checked October 2026. Not sure a pick is right for you? Ask a parent, a teacher, or your counselor.

Your clock is running late, and maybe your brain is busy too. That's a really common teen combo.

What might help right now

  • 📵Phone across the room tonight.
  • 🌙Start a 20-minute wind-down: dim lights, something calm.
  • 📝Write tomorrow's to-do list before bed so your brain can let go.

Racing thoughts at night are hard. Sometimes the quiet makes worries louder.

What might help right now

  • ✍️Write the worries down and tell yourself you'll deal with them tomorrow.
  • 🌬Breathe in for 4, out for 6, ten times.
  • 🗣If this happens a lot, talk to a counselor. Worry is very treatable.

  • 🔌Charge it across the room, or outside it if you can.
  • 💬Tell your friends your phone bedtime. They might join you.
