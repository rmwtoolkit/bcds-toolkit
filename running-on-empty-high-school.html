Running on Empty

Your body clock moved later. School didn't.

What's going on

During puberty, melatonin starts later at night, so your clock moves later. School didn't. Short sleep fades the prefrontal cortex first and makes the amygdala louder. This is biology, not bad habits.

Melatonin comes later→Wide awake at midnight→The 7am alarm anyway→Shorter fuse, weaker memory→You stop noticing the damage

Part 1 · Understand it

What short sleep does to a teenage brain

Sound familiar?Wired at midnight. Wrecked at 7.
  • You get home from practice or a shift at 9, start homework at 10, and feel most awake around midnight.
  • You've caught yourself drifting on the drive to school.
  • Coffee or an energy drink is part of your morning, then part of your afternoon too.
  • You sleep until 1 on Saturday, and Monday still feels like jet lag.
  • Small things set you off, and later you can't say why.

You've heard "teens need more sleep" plenty of times. Here's what's actually going on inside your head, and what you can change even when your schedule isn't fully yours.

Your sleep runs on a clock inside your brain

Master clock (SCN): sets the time

A tiny cluster of cells in the hypothalamus that runs your daily rhythm. It sets itself mostly by light coming in through your eyes. Morning light pulls it earlier. Bright screens and rooms late at night push it later.

Pineal gland: sends the "night" signal

When the clock says evening, it releases melatonin, the hormone that makes you sleepy. During puberty that release starts later at night. That's a big reason you're not tired at 10 anymore.

Hippocampus: files what you learned

During sleep it replays the day and moves it into long-term memory. In a Harvard sleep lab study, people kept awake all night were up to 40% worse at forming new memories the next day, and their hippocampus was less active.

Amygdala: gets louder

Your threat detector. After a night without sleep, it reacted about 60% more strongly to upsetting images, and its connection to the prefrontal cortex weakened. Tired brains overreact.

Prefrontal cortex: first to fade

Judgment, focus, patience, and impulse control live here. It's one of the first systems to slip when you're short on sleep. That's why a tired day feels like a shorter fuse.

The researchThis is biology, not bad habits.
Your clock really did move.
In a 1998 study, sleep researcher Mary Carskadon's team tested 10th graders whose school started at 7:20. On morning nap tests, 12 of 25 fell straight into dream (REM) sleep, a pattern sleep doctors usually see in narcolepsy. Their brains thought it was still the middle of the night.
You need 8 to 10 hours. Most people your age get less.
That's the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's recommendation for ages 13 to 18. In the CDC's 2023 national survey, about 77% of high school students got less than 8 hours on a typical school night.
Later start times work.
When Seattle moved high school start times from 7:50 to 8:45, students got about 34 more minutes of sleep a night, and median grades rose 4.5%. It was one district and not a randomized trial, but it's one of the clearest real-world tests so far.
You stop noticing the damage.
In a classic lab study, adults who slept 6 hours a night for two weeks kept getting worse on attention and memory tests. Their sense of how sleepy they were leveled off after a few days. They felt "used to it." Their performance said otherwise.
Driving tiredFour hours of sleep, four times the crash rate.

A AAA Foundation study of more than 4,500 crashes found that drivers who'd slept 4 to 5 hours in the past day had about 4 times the crash rate of drivers who slept 7 or more. Under 4 hours, it was about 11 times.

Windows down and loud music don't fix drowsiness. If you're drifting, pull over somewhere safe, call someone, or take a ride. That's the adult call, not the dramatic one.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Run your own sleep system

The levers

What you control, even when your schedule doesn't bend.

LightGet bright light, outside if you can, in the first hour after you wake up. Dim screens and lights in the last hour before bed. This is how you move the clock.
A wake-up anchorKeep weekend wake-ups within about two hours of school days. Sleeping until 1pm is like flying a few time zones west every Saturday.
A caffeine cutoffIn a sleep lab study, caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still cost people about an hour of sleep, and they didn't notice. Set your cutoff by early afternoon.
The phone's bedtimeCharge it outside your room. If it's your alarm or you share a room, put it across the room on Do Not Disturb.

Not every household runs on the same clock. Late family dinners, a shared bedroom, someone working nights, younger siblings to get to bed: you may not control when the house goes quiet. Start with what you do control, and if something at home is costing you sleep, it's fair to talk about it with your family. What does a school night look like at your place?

More tools

For the nights that go sideways.

Tap to see how.

1 Protect the night before a test.

Sleep is part of studying. Your hippocampus does its filing overnight. Thirty minutes on the most important material, then sleep, usually beats three more hours of cramming.

2 Can't fall asleep? Get up.

If you've been lying awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up, do something boring in dim light, and go back when you're sleepy. Lying there trying teaches your brain that bed is for worrying.

3 Nap with a plan.

About 20 minutes, before mid-afternoon. Longer or later naps steal from tonight.

4 Ask for the schedule change.

Coaches, managers, and teachers adjust things more often than students expect. "I'm closing three school nights a week and it's wrecking my sleep. Could I end at 9 on Mondays?" is a reasonable ask.

Try itWork backward from your alarm.

Put in when you need to be up. You'll see when you'd need to be asleep, and when to start winding down.

Myths about sleepWhat the research says instead.
MYTH I've trained myself to need less.

You've trained yourself to feel used to it. In the lab, people on 6 hours a night stopped feeling sleepier after a few days while their test scores kept dropping.

MYTH Melatonin gummies fix it.

Melatonin is a timing signal, not a knockout pill. Supplements also aren't regulated like medicine: a 2023 JAMA study found gummies held anywhere from 74% to 347% of the dose on the label. Talk to a doctor before using it regularly.

MYTH I'll catch up on the weekend.

A little extra helps. Sleeping until the afternoon shifts your clock later and sets up a rough Sunday night.

MYTH Scrolling helps me wind down.

Feeds are built to keep you going, and bright light late at night pushes your clock back. A show on a TV across the room is a better bet than a phone in your hand.

When to get more help

  • Trouble falling or staying asleep most nights for weeks.
  • Exhausted even after a full night's sleep.
  • Worry, sadness, or racing thoughts keep you up.
  • Loud snoring or gasping in your sleep (someone else may notice first).
  • You've nodded off while driving. Don't drive drowsy: pull over or get a ride.

A counselor, the school nurse, or a doctor can help. Long-running insomnia has a well-tested treatment that doesn't involve medicine. See Get help below.

A reminder

Sleep isn't the thing you cut to make room for everything else. It's what makes everything else work. Guard it the way you'd guard anything that matters to you.

Other ways in

Sleep science is newer than you'd think. Artists and storytellers have been onto it for a while.

📖 Read

Why We Sleep · Matthew Walker, 2017

A sleep scientist on what a short night does to memory and mood. Some of the amygdala and hippocampus research in this lesson comes from his lab.

Heads up: Some scientists have criticized parts of the book for overstating the evidence.

Dr. Seuss's Sleep Book · Dr. Seuss, 1962 Throwback

One small bug yawns, and the yawn spreads until ninety-nine zillion creatures are asleep. Then it asks: what about you?

🎬 Watch

Inside Out · Pixar, 2015 Throwback

At the end of every day, while Riley sleeps, the day's memories get sent to long-term storage. That's the hippocampus job from the lesson, drawn as cartoons. Rated PG.

🎧 Listen

Fireflies · Owl City, 2009

Adam Young wrote it awake in the early morning during his insomnia. It's a cheerful song about not being able to sleep.

🖼️ Look

The Bedroom · Vincent van Gogh, 1888

Van Gogh wrote to his brother that the colors should suggest rest or sleep. Compare it with your room at midnight, lit by a phone.
