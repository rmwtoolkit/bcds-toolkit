Where Did the Afternoon Go?

"It'll take an hour." It did not take an hour.

What's going on

Almost everyone underestimates how long things take. You plan from the inside, picturing every step going smoothly, and you remember past tasks as shorter than they were. Experience alone doesn't fix this. Your own data does.

You picture it going smoothly→Interruptions aren't in the plan→One evening becomes three→You remember it as shorter→"I'll be faster next time"→The next plan is short again

Part 1 · Understand it

Why your estimates keep losing

Sound familiar?"It'll take an hour." It did not take an hour.
  • You budgeted one evening for the essay and it took three.
  • You leave for your shift at the exact time it takes to get there if every light is green.
  • Sunday's plan had seven things on it. You did two.
  • You're sure the last project only took a weekend. Your browser history says otherwise.
  • "Five more minutes" on your phone is a unit of time with no fixed length.

You may already know the trick "double your estimate." It helps. This lesson is about why the estimate is wrong in the first place, because once you see the mechanism you can fix it with better data instead of a guess times two.

The planning fallacy, in one chart

In a classic 1994 study, psychologist Roger Buehler and colleagues asked college seniors how long their thesis would take. They gave a realistic guess, a best case, and a worst case, if everything went as badly as it could.

The actual time beat even the worst case. These weren't careless students. They were seniors who had written plenty of papers before. That's the point: experience alone doesn't fix this. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named it the planning fallacy in 1979, and it shows up in students, engineers, and governments building bridges.

Why it happensWhy your brain lowballs time.
You plan from the inside.
When you estimate, you picture the steps going smoothly. Buehler's students rarely thought about how long past projects had actually taken, even though that history was the best predictor they had. Kahneman called this the inside view, as opposed to the outside view, which asks how long this kind of thing usually takes.
You remember past tasks as shorter than they were.
Michael Roy and colleagues found that people tend to underestimate how long past tasks took, and then base future guesses on that memory. A bad memory feeds a bad plan.
Interruptions don't make it into the plan.
Nobody schedules the text, the snack, the sibling who needs help, or the 20 minutes finding the right file. Those breaks are where much of the missing time goes.
Some brains feel time less.
Sensing time passing is an executive skill. Many people with ADHD describe "now" and "not now" as the only two times that exist. If that's you, outside tools matter even more.
The stakes go upNext year, fewer people will catch it for you.

In high school, teachers check in, and someone at home may notice you're up at midnight. In college or at a job, a professor posts a syllabus in September and the paper is due in December. A manager notices when you're late, more than why.

Learning to estimate time well is one of the most practical adult skills there is, and almost nobody is taught it on purpose.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Plan with real numbers

What the research supports

Three fixes that work on the mechanism.

Use your own dataTime a type of task three or four times. "Lab reports take me about 2.5 hours" beats any guess. This is the outside view, about you.
Unpack the taskList every piece before you estimate. In a 2004 study by Justin Kruger and Matt Evans, people who broke tasks into parts gave longer estimates, and in two of the four experiments those estimates were closer to the truth.
Decide where and whenIn a 2000 Dutch study, students who wrote down exactly when and where they'd do an assignment finished it more often and were interrupted less, which shrank the gap between plan and reality.
Putting it on the calendar

Blocks, buffers, and backward plans.

Tap one to see how.

1 Plan backward from the due date.

Write the due date, then the step before it, then the one before that. Put each step on a real day. If the first step should have started yesterday, you just learned something useful.

2 Add a buffer, on purpose.

Leave open time before anything that matters: the day before the deadline, ten minutes before the shift. Buffers aren't wasted. They absorb the interruptions you didn't plan for.

3 Schedule the transitions.

Getting from practice to dinner to the desk takes time. A calendar with no gaps between blocks is a plan that's already late.

4 Decide the end before you start.

Feeds and autoplay are built without stopping points. Set the stop time before you open the app, with an alarm, not a promise.

Try itStart building your own data.

Pick a task you do often. Unpack it, guess, then time it. Do this for the same kind of task three times and you'll have a number you can trust. Nothing here is saved, so jot your results somewhere you'll see them.

Nia is a senior. She figures her research paper will take "about three hours" because that's how long it would take if she knew exactly what to say. It's due Friday. It's Tuesday.

Jae's Saturday shift starts at 10. The drive takes 15 minutes, so he leaves at 9:45. He's been late three weeks in a row, and his manager has noticed.

Sofia has four free hours on Sunday and a list of seven things. She starts with whatever's on top and ends up with the two biggest ones untouched.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths about timeWhat the research says instead.
MYTH I'll be faster next time.

People predict that about every time. Buehler's seniors had plenty of experience and were still off by weeks. Use the number from last time, not the hope for next time.

MYTH A to-do list is a plan.

A list has no clock in it. Until tasks have times, you can't see that they don't fit.

MYTH Buffers are wasted time.

If nothing goes wrong, a buffer is free time. If something does, it's the reason you're on time.

MYTH Being late means I don't care.

Usually it means the plan assumed a perfect day. Fix the plan, and the caring shows up on time.

When to ask for more help

  • You're late or behind most days, even with timers, buffers, and a calendar.
  • Losing track of time is hurting your grades, your job, your sleep, or your relationships.
  • You've had trouble feeling time pass for as long as you can remember. That can be part of ADHD.

A counselor can help you sort out what's going on and, if needed, talk with teachers about deadlines. See Get help below.

A reminder

Everyone underestimates time, including the adults telling you to manage yours. The difference is using real numbers. Collect yours, plan with them, and leave room for the day to be a normal day.

Other ways in

The planning fallacy shows up in movies, songs and children's books. One of these is probably on a shelf at home.

📖 Read

Thinking, Fast and Slow · Daniel Kahneman, 2011

The chapter "The Outside View" tells how Kahneman's own team guessed two years for a textbook. It took eight. The lesson's inside view and outside view come from here.

Frog and Toad Together · Arnold Lobel, 1972 Throwback

In "A List," Toad writes down his whole day. Then the wind blows the list away. Reread it with Sofia's Sunday list in mind.

🎬 Watch

Zootopia · Disney, 2016 Throwback

Judy has 48 hours to solve the case, and she needs one plate number from the DMV, where everyone working is a sloth. Nobody plans for the interruptions. Rated PG.

Interstellar · Christopher Nolan, 2014

The crew plans a quick stop on Miller's planet, where time runs slow. One giant wave later, 23 years have passed back on the ship. A best-case plan meets reality.

Heads up: Rated PG-13 for some intense perilous action and brief strong language. Several characters die.

🎧 Listen

Time · Pink Floyd, 1973

Roger Waters wrote it after realizing in his late twenties that life wasn't practice for later. He was already living it.

Pick one task you do every week. Write down how long you remember it taking. Then time it for real three times and write those numbers next to your memory. That's your outside view.
