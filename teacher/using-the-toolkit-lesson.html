Session plan

A suggested plan. Change the timing, skip or reorder slides, or use any part on its own. You know your students.

Goal

Advisors leave knowing how the year works (Ms. Garcia, Dr. Wilson, another adult or an outside speaker gives the talk; advisors lead the conversation after it), where their grade's list and conversation guides live, and that the goal in advisory is engagement, not agreement.

Materials

  • The live toolkit open in a second browser tab, in case you want to click around
  • A projector with internet (each screenshot slide links to the real page)
  • Optional: each grade's advisor link in the meeting invite or chat

Flow

  1. 2 minOpening: an advisory conversation that took off
  2. 3 minHow it works: the talk, the conversation, the student page
  3. 4 minYour grade's list and the conversation guide (2 screenshots)
  4. 4 minEngagement, not agreement, and rolling with it
  5. 2 minEvery page points to a person
  6. 5 minTry it (two scenarios)

Short on time (15 minutes)

Twelve minutes: how it works (3), your grade's list and the guide (3), engagement, not agreement (2), roll with it (2), every page points to a person (2). Skip the opening and the scenarios.

Before you present
Say who gives the talks.

Advisors are not teaching these topics. Ms. Garcia, Dr. Wilson, another adult or an outside speaker gives the talk at a class meeting or Upper School meeting. Say it early; it takes a weight off the room.

Engagement, not agreement.

This is the heart of the talk. A group that pushes back on the talk is a group that listened. Say it more than once.

Show, don't tell.

Two slides are screenshots of the real pages. If the room has internet, click through and show the live page for a moment, then come back.

Keep it light.

Advisors don't need activities, scripts or charisma. One question and some curiosity carry most conversations.

Be clear about what it isn't.

It isn't therapy, a screening tool, or something counseling watches. A student who says something worrying gets the same response as anywhere else at Beaver: let counseling know that day.

Leave with one.

The ask is small: the next time your grade has a talk, open the guide and ask what they thought.

Every student in the room

Culture, family, identity, ability and money: what to keep in mind for this topic.

Every lesson has a page on this.

Point out the 'Every student in the room' page in each teacher lesson. It covers culture, family, identity, ability and money for that topic, so teachers don't have to guess.

Not every family talks about these things.

Some students come from homes where mental health, dating or substances aren't discussed, or are discussed very differently. The lessons are written to respect that and to avoid assuming one kind of family.

Students who learn differently.

Student pages are short, visual and readable on a phone or on paper. Students with a learning plan can keep the page and come back to it at their own pace.

Language at home.

A student's parents may not read English comfortably. The student page is the student's to keep; if a family wants something in another language, loop in counseling.

Teachers differ too.

Faculty bring their own cultures and comfort levels. It's fine for a teacher to say 'I'm still learning about this' in front of students. That models the lesson better than a perfect script.

Privacy for everyone.

Nothing students tap or type on a page is saved or sent. Nobody, including counseling, can see what a student wrote. Say this plainly, because students and families will ask.

What the research says

A meta-analysis of 213 school-based social and emotional learning programs (about 270,000 students) found better social skills, attitudes and behavior, and an 11 percentile point gain in academic achievement.

Durlak et al., 2011, Child Development

Across 99 studies, warmer teacher-student relationships were linked to higher student engagement and achievement, with the strongest links for older students and students at risk.

Roorda et al., 2011, Review of Educational Research

Students who felt connected at school and at home as teens had lower odds of poor mental health, substance use and risky behavior as adults.

Steiner et al., 2019, Pediatrics

Single-session interventions for youth mental health showed small but real benefits across 50 trials. Short, well-built lessons can help; they don't replace ongoing care.

Schleider & Weisz, 2017, Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry

Slides and notes

Tap any slide to present from there.

Slide 1 · Notes

Welcome everyone. Something like: "This will take about twenty minutes. Here's the short version: you won't be teaching any of these topics. Someone else gives the talk: Ms. Garcia, Dr. Wilson, another adult or an outside speaker. Your part is the conversation in advisory afterward, and you're already good at that."

Slide 2 · Notes

2 MINUTES. Thirty quiet seconds, then take two or three answers. You'll hear: a kid brought it up, it was about something they cared about, I asked what they thought, I didn't have an agenda. Say: "That's the whole model."

Slide 3 · Notes

3 MINUTES. Counseling and learning support set the topics and the order for each grade; you don't have to pick anything. If you miss a talk, the guide has a three-line recap so you can still lead the conversation.

Slide 4 · Notes

2 MINUTES. Counseling sends each grade's advisors one link. It shows only that grade's list, in order. If you're live, open it and click a conversation guide.

Slide 5 · Notes

2 MINUTES. Two minutes of reading is plenty. There are no slides to present and nothing to cover. Pick one of the zero-in questions, or none; follow the group.

Slide 6 · Notes

2 MINUTES. Say this plainly: you don't need to defend the talk or the speaker. Students who disagree were listening. Ask what they'd have said instead, or what the talk missed. Teens tune out being told; they lean in when an adult wants to know what they think.

Slide 7 · Notes

2 MINUTES. Long silences rarely work with high schoolers, so lower the stakes and keep it moving. Ask the room: "What do you already do when a group goes flat?"

Slide 8 · Notes

1 MINUTE. This answers the question people are too polite to ask: do I have to know about vaping, or consent, or anxiety? No. That's what the talk was for.

Slide 9 · Notes

2 MINUTES. The toolkit supplements people; it doesn't replace them. Every student page names who to talk to, including you, and says plainly that nobody is watching it. If something heavy comes up in advisory: thank them, keep it short in the group, check in privately after, and walk them to counseling that day.

Slide 10 · Notes

5 MINUTES for both. Pairs, a minute each, then a quick share. Good answers: "Fair. What would have made it less preachy?" or "What did they get wrong?" The pushback is the opening.

Slide 11 · Notes

Let it run for a bit; tangents are usually about the topic anyway. If you want to bring it back: "How does that connect to what the talk said?" There's no wrong answer here; the point is that they're talking.

Slide 12 · Notes

1 MINUTE. Say when the next talk for each grade is, if you know. Invite feedback: "Tell us what worked and what fell flat, especially the guides." Put each grade's advisor link in the meeting chat. Thank them.
