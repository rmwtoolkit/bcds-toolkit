Part 1 · Understand it

What the mirror, and the algorithm, leave out

Sound familiar?Your feed found your insecurities fast. That's its job.
  • Your feed is full of guys rating faces, explaining "canthal tilt," and telling you what's wrong with yours.
  • You've checked your side profile in the mirror or in photos more times than you'd admit.
  • Missing a lift, or eating something off plan, puts you in a bad mood for the whole day.
  • A teammate is talking about "running a cycle," or a friend just ordered something online that isn't really legal.
  • You've asked yourself "am I ugly?" and an app or a comment section was happy to answer.

This page focuses on the pressure aimed at guys, because a lot of it is. Anyone of any gender can feel it. The mechanics underneath are the same: find a flaw, then sell the fix.

Your body runs on its own clock

The height spurt

For boys, the fastest growth usually comes somewhere between 12 and 14, at roughly 9 to 10 cm a year at the peak. Recent US data suggests it's arriving a little earlier than it did a generation ago. Some guys keep filling out into their late teens and early 20s.

Strength comes during and after

Studies that track boys over time find the biggest jumps in strength happen during the spurt or within about a year after it. If the guy next to you is gaining faster, timing explains a lot of it.

Genes set the frame

Your bone structure, jaw shape and height range are mostly set by genetics and growth. Training, food and sleep change what you build on that frame. Tongue tricks and apps don't change the frame.

The words you keep hearingNew vocabulary, same move: find a flaw.
Looksmaxing
Maximizing how you look. It can mean a haircut and better sleep. In a lot of online spaces, it means scoring every part of your face.
Mogging
Looking so much better than someone that they "lose." It turns every group photo into a contest.
Mewing
Pressing your tongue to the roof of your mouth to reshape your jaw. There's no good evidence it changes bone structure in teens or adults.
Bone smashing
Hitting your face with hard objects to "build" bone. It can cause fractures and nerve damage. Doctors are clear: don't.
PSL and face ratings
Scoring faces on a scale invented by forums, not science. The "rules" shift depending on who's posting.
"It's over" thinking
Some looksmax spaces push the idea that your face decides your whole future, so there's no point trying. That's a worldview being sold to you, not a finding. Relationships, friendships and careers are built on far more than bone structure.
The researchHow common this is.
Muscle-building behavior is widespread.
A University of Minnesota team surveyed 2,793 students at 20 urban middle and high schools. About 35% had used protein powders or shakes, and about 5% said they had used steroids. Most of the five muscle-building behaviors they asked about were more common in boys.
Filters feed it.
A 2024 Canadian study of 912 teens and young adults found that guys who often used photo filters scored higher on drive for size and on how much muscle concerns interfered with daily life.
Guys get eating disorders.
In a national US survey, about 2% of men had binge eating disorder at some point in their lives. Men with eating disorders often get missed, because restricting, bulking and over-training can look like discipline.
What's actually in the productLabels on muscle products are often wrong.

Supplements. The FDA doesn't approve supplements before they're sold. A 2018 analysis of FDA warnings found 776 supplements that contained hidden drug ingredients. About 12% were sold for muscle building, and those most often contained synthetic steroids.

SARMs. Researchers bought 44 products sold online as SARMs and tested them. Only 52% actually contained a SARM. 39% contained a different unapproved drug, and only 41% had the amount the label claimed. The FDA has warned that SARMs are linked to liver damage, heart attack and stroke.

Steroids. Anabolic steroids are illegal without a prescription. In teens they can close growth plates early, which stops height growth for good. They're also linked to acne, mood swings, depression when stopping, and shrinking testicles.

Many physiques online are lit, posed, pumped up right before the photo, filtered, or built with drugs nobody mentions.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Take your mirror back

What actually builds a body

The boring basics beat every secret.

The American Academy of Pediatrics supports strength training for teens when it's supervised and focused on technique. Here's what that looks like.

Train for performanceA faster mile, a first pull-up, making varsity. Goals about what your body does hold up better than goals about how it looks.
Sleep like it mattersTeens need 8 to 10 hours. Recovery happens there, not in a shaker bottle.
Food firstMost teens get enough protein from regular meals. Powder is a convenience, not a requirement.
Real coachingAn athletic trainer, coach, or doctor who knows you beats a stranger with a shirtless profile picture.
Protect your head

Moves for the feed and the mirror.

Tap one to see how.

1 Ask "what are they selling?"

Check the link in bio before you believe a creator. If they profit from you feeling ugly, weigh their advice that way.

2 Reset the algorithm.

Tap "not interested" on rating and "fix your face" videos, or reset your recommendations in settings. Follow athletes for skills, not physiques.

3 Cap the body checks.

Checking a "flaw" again and again makes it look bigger. Try one look before you leave, then done.

4 Let rest days be rest days.

Muscle grows during recovery. If skipping a session makes you panic, that panic is worth paying attention to.

Try itSARMs, supplements, mewing. Myth or fact?

Tap your answer. No score, just checking.

Mewing can reshape your jaw if you do it long enough.

Supervised strength training is safe for high schoolers.

If a supplement is sold at a big store, the FDA has approved it.

SARMs are a safer alternative to steroids.

Guys don't really get eating disorders.

In a groupUseful advice or sales pitch? Vote first.

For advisory or class, about 10 minutes, no materials. The leader reads each claim like it's from a video. Everyone votes: thumbs up for useful, thumbs down for sales pitch, flat hand for not sure. Then tap to reveal.

1 "This jawline routine fixed my face in 30 days. Link in bio."

Sales pitch. Bone structure doesn't change in a month, and the link is the business model.

2 "Sleep 8 to 10 hours. Recovery is where you grow."

Useful. Sleep is when muscle repairs, and teens need more of it than adults do.

3 "You're a 4 out of 10. Here's how to get to a 7."

Sales pitch. The scale is made up, and the fix is usually something for sale.

4 "Start light, learn form, add weight slowly."

Useful. It's what coaches, athletic trainers and pediatricians recommend.

5 "Natty or not? I'll never tell."

The answer is "not sure," which is the point. If someone won't say whether they use steroids, don't use their body as your target.

Talk about it. Why do rating videos get so many views? If a younger kid you care about started watching this stuff, what would you tell them? What would a healthier gym culture at our school look like?

Tip for the person leading

Keep the talk on the content, not anyone's body. Nobody should be asked to rate, describe, or compare themselves or anyone in the room.

Try itThree real situations. What's your move?

A teammate says he ordered SARMs online and offers to split them with you before the season.

A group chat starts ranking everyone's faces from the formal photos, one to ten.

You've been training every day. You get sick, and the idea of skipping makes you anxious.

There's usually more than one decent answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

If it gets heavy

  • You're skipping meals, making yourself throw up, or exercising to make up for eating.
  • You or a friend are using steroids, SARMs, or anything you'd hide from a doctor.
  • You hate how you look so much it's hard to go to school or see friends.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here.

Tell a counselor, the health office, or a trusted adult. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night.
