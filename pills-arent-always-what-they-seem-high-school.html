Part 1 · Understand it

Fake pills, real fentanyl

Sound familiar?A pill feels safer than a powder. That's the trap.
  • Someone mentions "Percs" or "bars," and it sounds more like medicine than drugs.
  • A plug on Snapchat or Telegram posts a menu with prices and delivery.
  • A friend takes Xanax for anxiety before exams, but it isn't prescribed to them.
  • Someone has leftover pills from a surgery and offers to share.
  • You heard about an overdose at another school, and nobody explained what actually happened.

Most of this you may already know: fake pills exist, and fentanyl is in them. This goes further: how an opioid overdose stops breathing, what the death data shows, and exactly what to do if it happens in front of you.

22U.S. teens aged 14 to 18 died of a drug overdose each week in 2022, even though teen drug use has been falling. Most of those deaths involved fentanyl, often in counterfeit pills.Friedman and Hadland, New England Journal of Medicine, 2024

How an opioid overdose stops breathing

Nucleus accumbens: the rush

Opioids, including fentanyl, oxycodone, and heroin, activate mu-opioid receptors in the reward circuit. The rush and the calm are why they hook people quickly, and tolerance builds fast.

Medulla: the breathing rhythm

A small cluster of neurons here, the preBötzinger complex, sets the rhythm of every breath without you thinking about it. Opioids slow it. Enough of them and the rhythm stalls.

Pons: the breathing pace-setter

Another brainstem area that helps shape each breath. In a 2020 study in mice, researchers found that opioid receptors in these two small brainstem sites account for the slowed breathing that kills in an overdose.

As breathing slows, oxygen drops. After a few minutes without enough oxygen, the brain starts to be damaged, and then the heart stops. Naloxone (Narcan) binds the same receptors more tightly than opioids do, knocks them off, and restarts breathing, usually within minutes.

The dataMost teens who died weren't alone.
TANZ AND COLLEAGUES, CDC, 2022

In a review of U.S. overdose deaths among 10 to 19 year olds from mid-2019 to 2021, 84% involved illicit fentanyl, and about 1 in 4 had evidence of counterfeit pills. Two out of three had a potential bystander nearby. Most of the time, there was no record of anyone responding.

DEA LAB TESTING, 2024

Half of the fake pills the DEA tested contained a potentially deadly dose of fentanyl, which is 2 milligrams, a speck small enough to fit on the tip of a pencil. That is down from 7 in 10 in 2023. It is still a coin flip.

Fentanyl isn't spread evenly when pills are pressed, so two pills from the same batch can be completely different. The person selling them can't tell either.

What else is in the supplyFentanyl isn't the only thing showing up.
Xylazine
An animal sedative, not an opioid, often mixed with fentanyl. Naloxone doesn't reverse xylazine itself, but it still reverses the fentanyl mixed with it. Always give naloxone in a suspected overdose.
Nitazenes
A newer group of synthetic opioids, some stronger than fentanyl, found pressed into fake oxycodone, Xanax, and Adderall. Naloxone works on them, but it may take more than one dose.
Fake Adderall and Xanax
Counterfeit pills aren't only painkillers. Fake study drugs and anxiety pills have contained fentanyl and meth.
Test stripsA negative result isn't a green light.

Fentanyl test strips can show whether fentanyl is in a sample. They can't tell you how much, they don't detect xylazine, and because fentanyl clumps, testing one part of a pill doesn't clear the rest.

The only pills you can trust come from a pharmacy with your name on the label.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

The minutes that matter

What actually helps

Recognize. Call. Narcan. Breathe. Stay.

Learn this sequence once and it stays with you. It works for a friend, a stranger, or a family member.

Tap each step.

1 Recognize it.

Won't respond when you shout their name or rub their breastbone hard. Slow, shallow, or no breathing. Gurgling or snoring sounds. Blue or gray lips and fingertips. Tiny pupils.

2 Call 911.

Say "someone isn't breathing" and give the address. In Massachusetts, the Good Samaritan law means neither you nor the person overdosing can be charged with drug possession because you called for help. It covers possession, not every possible charge, but it was written so people would call.

3 Give naloxone.

One spray in one nostril. No response in 2 to 3 minutes: second dose in the other nostril. Narcan nasal spray has been sold over the counter since 2023, no prescription needed. It can't hurt someone who isn't on opioids, so when in doubt, give it.

4 Help them breathe.

Roll them onto their side so they don't choke. If the 911 dispatcher walks you through rescue breaths, follow along.

5 Stay.

Naloxone wears off in 30 to 90 minutes, and some opioids last longer, so the overdose can come back. Expect them to wake up sick, shaky, or upset. That's withdrawal, not ingratitude. Keep them with you until EMS arrives.

6 Prevent the next one.

Pharmacy, your name, or nothing. Know where naloxone is kept at school and at home, and consider carrying it. If you ever take a pill that didn't come from a pharmacy, don't take it alone.

Try itThree real moments. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

Sofia buys "bars" from a Snapchat plug to sleep before big tests. "He only sells pressed-from-pharma," she says.

At a party, Devin is slumped on the bathroom floor, snoring loudly. His lips look gray. Someone says, "He's fine, he just took a Perc and he's sleeping."

You gave Devin naloxone. He wakes up, throws up, and is angry and shaky. He wants to leave before the ambulance gets there.

The best answer is the one you'll actually do.

In a groupRun the drill. Then write the text.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. In pairs, one person says the five steps out loud, from memory, while the other checks: Recognize, Call, Narcan, Breathe, Stay. Switch.
  2. Read this text from a friend: "my cousin can get us percs for saturday, u in?" Each pair writes the reply they'd actually send.
  3. A few pairs share. The group picks the replies that sound most like a real friend and least like a parent.
Talk about itTalk it over.
  • In the CDC data, most teens who died had someone nearby. Why might people not respond?
  • What would make it easier for students to carry or find naloxone?
  • How do families and communities you know talk about drugs and overdose? What gets left unsaid?
  • Who is an adult at school you'd go to if you were worried about a friend?
Tip for the person leading

Keep it about helping a friend, never about anyone's own use. Some students may have lost someone to an overdose; let people pass, and know where the counselor is.

What doesn't helpThese usually backfire.
MYTH I trust my source.

The source can't see fentanyl either. Trust doesn't show up on a lab test.

MYTH Half a pill is safer.

Fentanyl clumps. Half a pill can hold all of it.

MYTH Narcan could hurt them if it's not opioids.

It can't. If it isn't an opioid overdose, naloxone does nothing. If it is, it can save their life.

MYTH They woke up, so the danger's over.

Naloxone can wear off before the opioid does. Stay until EMS takes over.

MYTH Calling 911 means my friend gets arrested.

In Massachusetts, the Good Samaritan law protects both of you from drug-possession charges when you call for help.

Call 911 right away if someone

  • Won't wake up, or can't talk.
  • Has slow, shallow, or no breathing, or is making gurgling or snoring sounds.
  • Has blue or gray lips or fingertips.
  • Took a pill that didn't come from a pharmacy and is acting very sleepy or strange.

Give naloxone (Narcan) if you have it, and stay with them. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222. See Get help below.
