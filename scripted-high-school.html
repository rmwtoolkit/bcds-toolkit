Part 1 · Understand it

The show and the real thing

Sound familiar?Most people didn't plan the first time they saw it.
  • Something popped up that you never searched for, and you couldn't unsee it.
  • A friend pulled up a video at lunch and a crowd formed around his phone.
  • The guys on your team joke about it like everyone watches, all the time.
  • You've watched more than you meant to, usually late at night, and felt off afterward.
  • You've wondered whether your body, or how you'd be with someone, measures up to what you've seen.
  • Someone you're seeing brought up something from a video and expected you to want it too.
  • You were raised to believe porn is wrong, and the guilt sits heavy.

And if you've never seen any? That's fine too. Most of your classmates have, so it still helps to know how it works on people.

How common it is

In a 2022 national survey, nearly three out of four teens had seen porn online. The average age for the first time was 12. For a lot of them, it wasn't a choice: more had seen it by accident than on purpose.

When did it start? For more than half of teens, before high school. Many first saw it in elementary or middle school, long before anyone had talked with them about sex.

Why it pulls

Let's be straight about why it's hard to look away. None of these make you gross or broken. They make you a person with a phone.

CuriosityBodies and sex are on your mind at this age. That's normal growing up, and porn is the fastest answer on the internet.
Your reward systemYour brain responds strongly to anything new and exciting. An endless stream of new clips keeps that response going.
Quick reliefBored, stressed, lonely, can't sleep. It works fast as an escape, which is part of how it turns into a habit.
Free and privateIt's in your pocket, it costs nothing, and nobody has to know. Very little else is that easy.

It's pro wrestling

Ever watched pro wrestling? The athletes are real. The strength is real, and some of the bruises are real. The match is scripted. Who wins got decided backstage, the moves are rehearsed, and the two wrestlers are working together the whole time so nobody gets hurt. Fans in the arena know it's a show. That's why WWE has told kids for years: please don't try this at home.

Porn works the same way. The bodies are real. What happens is a performance staged for a camera, with a director, retakes, lighting, and editing. The performers act out what sells.

What got cut from the tape:

  • talking first
  • asking
  • checking in
  • nerves
  • awkward moments
  • laughing
  • condoms and birth control
  • stopping when someone's not into it
  • how they treat each other afterward

Most teens already sense it's fake. In the same survey, only 27% of teens who had seen porn said it shows how most people have sex. The trouble is that it can still become the script.

The script problemIf nobody else hands you a playbook, porn will.

Researchers call them sexual scripts: the playbook in your head for what happens, in what order, and who does what. People build scripts from whatever they see most. Media researcher Paul Wright has argued that sexual media shapes young people's scripts, especially when nothing else is filling them in.

Teens say it's happening. Most who had seen porn said it taught them at least something about how sex works, and fewer than half said it taught them anything about talking with a partner.

Here's the encouraging part. Teens in the 2022 survey were more likely to say they'd learned a lot about sex from a parent, caregiver, or trusted adult (47%) than from porn (27%). In another national study of 14- to 17-year-olds, parents and friends topped the list of helpful sources, and only about 8% named porn. That changes by the early twenties, when porn became the most-named source (Rothman and colleagues, 2021).

So the people around you still get a say in your script, if you let them. That window is open now.

What a lot of it showsRough is common on screen. Asking is rare.

Choking shows up so often that it has spread into real life. In a national survey of adults, about 1 in 5 women said a partner had choked them during sex, and people who watched porn more often were more likely to report it (Herbenick and colleagues, 2020). Some teens have absorbed the idea that it's harmless: among teens who had seen violent porn, 20% said it's safe to put a hand around someone's neck during sex.

It isn't. Pressure on the neck can cut off blood to the brain within seconds, and there is no safe way to do it. If someone does it to you without asking, that's not okay, and you can tell someone.

Who gets typecastPorn sells stereotypes about bodies, race, and identity.

Over half of teens who had seen porn said they often or sometimes saw Black people shown in stereotyped ways, and around half saw the same for Latino and Asian people. Black teens were the most likely to say it left them feeling disgusted or self-conscious. Porn also typecasts women, gay and trans people, and bodies of every kind.

If you've felt reduced to a stereotype by it, that comes from the script, and it says nothing true about you. Same with bodies: performers are cast, lit, and edited. Comparing your body to theirs means comparing yourself to a casting call.

Consent behind the cameraYou can't always know if everyone agreed.

Some videos on free sites were filmed or posted without everyone's agreement. In one well-known case, a court awarded 22 women $12.75 million after a company lied to them and pressured them into filming. Their videos ended up spread across major sites, and the people behind the company faced federal charges.

When you can't tell whether the people on screen agreed, you can't know what you're watching. For a lesson about consent, that matters.

What the research says, and doesn'tSome of this is solid. Some is still argued about.
Attitudes: a consistent link.
A review of 20 years of studies found teen porn use linked to more stereotyped beliefs about gender and more permissive attitudes about sex. Most of those studies were snapshots in time, so they can't prove porn caused the change.
Aggression: researchers disagree.
One meta-analysis of 22 studies found porn use linked to sexual aggression, more for verbal pressure than physical force. Another found the link showed up mostly in weaker studies and not for nonviolent porn. Violent content is where researchers worry most.
"Porn addiction": the word is debated.
The World Health Organization added compulsive sexual behavior disorder in 2019, and it can involve porn. Researchers still argue about calling it addiction. One finding stands out: people who believe porn is wrong often feel addicted even when they watch fairly little, because the conflict itself feels like losing control.
Erections: less proven than you've heard.
You may have heard porn ruins erections. Large surveys of young men haven't found a clear link, though some men in therapy describe it. Nerves are a much more common reason things don't work the first few times, and that's normal.

Either way, if it's costing you sleep, schoolwork, or relationships, or you've tried to cut back and can't, that's worth talking over with someone.

For guys especiallyThe script casts you as the guy who never asks.

Guys start earlier and watch on purpose more, so the script lands on them hardest. In porn, the guy is always ready, always in charge, never nervous, and never has to ask. Real life doesn't run that way, and trying to play that part leaves you performing when you could be connecting.

Asking "is this okay?" is confidence. So is saying "I'm nervous," or "I'm not ready." And guys get pressured too. If someone pushes you to do something they saw, you can say no.

That goes for every guy: straight, gay, bi, trans, or still figuring it out.

What real intimacy takesNo crowd, no script, nobody keeping score.
Two people who both want to be there
A clear yes from both of you, and either of you can stop at any point. Consent keeps going the whole time.
Talking
Before, during, and after. Across 93 studies, couples who talk about sex well were more satisfied with their sex lives and their relationships.
Trust
What happens between you stays between you. No screenshots, no stories in the locker room.
Paying attention to them
Noticing how the other person is doing, instead of performing for an audience that isn't there.
Room for awkward
Real people laugh, get nervous, and figure it out together.
Taking care of each other
Protection, respect, and how you treat them the next day at school.
Time
Real closeness gets built out of friendship, honesty, and trust. Nobody gets there in a few edited minutes.

This is true for any couple, whoever they are. And there's no deadline. Plenty of people wait, for all kinds of reasons, and that's a real choice too.

Your family, your cultureEvery family talks about sex differently, or doesn't.

Some families talk about sex openly. Some never bring it up. Some have clear rules from their faith or culture about sex, marriage, and porn. When home stays quiet, porn often ends up as the teacher by default, without anyone choosing that.

  • What did you learn at home about sex and relationships, out loud or by the silence?
  • What do you want to keep from it?
  • Which adult could you ask a real question, without it getting weird?

If your faith teaches that porn is wrong and you've watched it, guilt can pile up fast. Shame tends to make a habit harder to change. A counselor, or a faith leader you trust, can help you sort it out without treating you like a bad person.

If you're LGBTQ+ or still figuring it out, porn may have been one of the few places you saw anyone like you. That makes sense. It's still a performance, and real queer relationships take the same things everyone's do. If home isn't a safe place to talk about this, a counselor can help you find someone who is.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Writing your own script

What actually helps

Small moves, made on purpose.

Tap each one.

1 Close it, no drama.

Seeing something doesn't say anything bad about you. You can close it and move on with your day.

2 Ask who made it, and why.

It was made to keep you watching and clicking. Ask what was staged, and what got cut.

3 Notice when it shows up.

Bored? Stressed? Lonely? Phone in bed after midnight? Most habits have a time and a mood attached.

4 Change the setup.

Charge your phone outside your room. Turn on a filter if you want a speed bump. It's a tool you pick for yourself, not a punishment.

5 Ride the wave.

Urges rise, peak, and fade. Get up, move however your body likes, text someone, take a shower. You don't have to win a fight with it, only outlast it.

6 Talk to a real person.

A counselor, a parent or caregiver, a doctor, an older cousin. Teens who talked with a trusted adult about porn mostly said it helped, and few said it made them feel bad.

7 Ask before, and keep asking.

With a partner, "Is this okay?" and "Do you want to keep going?" are the moves porn leaves out.

Try itFour moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd do.

At lunch, a friend pulls up a porn clip on his phone and people crowd around.

Someone you're seeing says, "I saw this in a video. Want to try it?" You're not into it.

You've been up late watching most nights this week. You're tired and kind of down about it.

Someone drops a nude photo of a girl from your grade into the group chat.

You won't always get it right. Noticing the script is how you start writing your own.

In a groupPick a corner. Then see if your guess was right.

For advisory or health class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move to or point at a corner: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. Nobody shares anything personal.
  3. After each one, the leader taps to show what the research says.

What the research says

No. Only 27% of teens who had seen porn said it shows how most people have sex, and 52% said it doesn't.

Four corners · 2 of 4"Teens learn more about sex from porn than from adults."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not in a 2022 national survey. More teens said they'd learned a lot from a parent, caregiver, or trusted adult (47%) than from porn (27%). Friends (41%) and sex ed (32%) also beat porn.

Four corners · 3 of 4"Porn usually shows people asking for consent."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

The opposite. Only 33% of teens who had seen porn had ever seen anyone ask. 52% had seen what looked like rape, choking, or someone in pain.

Four corners · 4 of 4"Scientists agree that porn addiction is real."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

They don't agree yet. The World Health Organization recognizes compulsive sexual behavior disorder, but researchers argue about the word "addiction." Feeling stuck is real either way, and it's worth getting help with.

Talk about itThree questions for the group.
  • Where do people our age learn about sex and relationships? Which of those sources do you trust?
  • How is porn like pro wrestling? Where does the comparison break down?
  • What would make it easier to ask a partner "is this okay?"
Tip for the person leading

Keep it general: nobody shares what they watch. Expect nervous laughter, and let it pass. Stay shame-free and skip lectures. If a student tells you an adult showed them porn or that someone hurt them, follow your school's reporting steps; you're a mandated reporter.

When it crosses a line

  • Someone shares or asks for sexual images of anyone under 18, real or AI-made.
  • An adult or much older person shows you porn, sends you sexual content, or asks you to keep it secret.
  • Someone pressured or forced you, or did something you didn't agree to, on a date or hookup.
  • You've tried to stop and can't, and it's hurting your sleep, school, or relationships.
  • You feel hopeless, or have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult today. You can call or text 988 any time, or call RAINN at 800-656-4673 about sexual assault. Take It Down, a free service from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, helps remove nude images taken when someone was under 18. If someone is in danger right now, call 911.
