What Makes You Happy

Wanting something isn't the same as liking it.

What's going on

Your brain has a big wanting system and small liking hotspots. You can want something intensely and barely enjoy it. And your brain overestimates how good a win will feel, and for how long.

You want the next thing→Dopamine drives the chase→You get it→A real lift→A week later, normal→"I'll be happy when..."

Part 1 · Understand it

Wanting something isn't the same as liking it

Sound familiar?You got it. It faded. Now you want the next thing.
  • You got the license, the acceptance, or the starting spot, and a week later it was just normal.
  • You've told yourself this year's stress will be worth it once you're in college.
  • A post did better than anything you've ever put up. You checked it twelve times and felt oddly flat.
  • You're busier than you've ever been, and you talk to fewer people for real.

You've probably heard that relationships matter more than stuff. That's still true. This is the next layer: why your brain keeps chasing anyway, and how to make better bets with your time.

Two systems: one wants, one enjoys

VTA: where wanting starts

A small area in the midbrain that sends out dopamine. Dopamine is about anticipation and pursuit more than pleasure. It surges for what's next and what's uncertain: the reply, the result, the refresh.

Nucleus accumbens: the pull

Dopamine here creates the drive to go get something. It also holds a few tiny "hotspots" that produce the actual feeling of enjoying it. The drive is big. The hotspots are small.

Ventral pallidum: a liking hotspot

Neuroscientist Kent Berridge's work suggests pleasure comes from a few small spots like this one, while wanting runs through a much larger network. That's how you can want something intensely and barely enjoy it.

Orbitofrontal cortex: keeps score

Tracks how rewarding things actually feel, and its response drops as you get your fill. One reason the tenth time is never as good as the first.

The researchYour brain is a bad fortune teller.
You overestimate how good, and for how long.
Psychologists Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert call it the impact bias. When you imagine getting into a school, you picture the moment you find out. You don't picture the ordinary Tuesday three months later, with laundry and a lab report.
You adapt, but it isn't hopeless.
The "hedonic treadmill" idea said everyone drifts back to the same baseline no matter what. Later research by Ed Diener and colleagues revised it: most people's baseline is mildly positive, it differs from person to person, and it can shift. What you do with your time can move it.
Money is more complicated than "it can't buy happiness."
A 2023 study run jointly by researchers who had disagreed found that for most people, happiness kept rising with income. It leveled off around $100,000 only for the least happy group. The gains also shrink per dollar as income rises, so it's real, but it's not the whole story.
People still matter most.
The Harvard Study of Adult Development, running for more than 85 years, found that how satisfied people were in their relationships at 50 predicted their health at 80 better than their cholesterol did.
Your phoneBuilt for wanting.

Likes, streaks, and feeds run on unpredictable rewards: maybe this refresh, maybe the next one. That's exactly what the wanting system responds to. It's how you can scroll for an hour you didn't enjoy. Wanting kept you there. Liking never showed up.

A more useful question than "is this bad for me?": did I like that, or did I just want more of it?

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Make better bets with your time

What the research says lasts

People pay off more than you predict.

Start the conversationChicago commuters predicted that talking to a stranger would be the worst way to spend the ride. The ones asked to do it had the most pleasant commutes.
Assume you're likedAfter talking with someone new, people consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them. In one study the gap lasted most of a year between college roommates.
Put in the hoursOne study estimated it takes about 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 to friend, and more than 200 to close friend. Friendship is a time investment.

More tools. Tap to see how.

1 Catch "I'll be happy when."

Your goals are worth chasing. Just notice when you've put your whole life on hold until you reach one. Ask what you could enjoy about this stretch, now.

2 Score want versus like.

For a week, after something you were looking forward to, rate how much you wanted it and how much you liked it, 1 to 10. Patterns show up fast.

3 Do things with people, not just near them.

Sitting in the same room on separate phones barely counts. Cooking, driving somewhere, playing, building, or working on something together does.

4 Care about something beyond you.

Tutoring, coaching younger kids, faith, a cause, your family, your community. Meaning holds up on the days pleasure doesn't.

What "a good life" looks like isn't the same everywhere. Psychologist Jeanne Tsai's research finds cultures differ in the kind of good feeling people aim for: some lean toward excitement, others toward calm and contentment. Neither one is the right answer. What does your family hope your life will feel like, and where do you agree?

Try itWho do you miss? One text is enough.

Think of someone you've drifted from: an old teammate, a cousin, a friend from your last school or from summer. Nothing here is saved.

Remember the liking gap. They're more likely to be glad you did than you'd guess.

Try it"I'll be happy when..." Ask a better question.
"I'll be happy when I get into that school."
What do I want a regular Tuesday there to feel like? Can I build any of that now?
"I'll be happy when I'm in a relationship."
Who do I already feel most like myself around?
"I'll be happy when I'm making my own money."
What do I want that money to make room for?
Try itYou don't need a crowd. You need one solid person.

Think of one person. Check anything that's true about them.

If no name came to mind, that's useful information, not a verdict on you. Plenty of people are between friend groups at some point, especially after a move or a big change. A counselor is a good person to think it through with.

Try itThree choices. Which bet pays off?

Sofia has 900 followers and posts almost every day. At lunch she usually eats alone with her phone.

Jamal moved for senior year. He figures there's no point making friends with nine months left.

Mei-Lin can take a paid summer job at a camp where her friends work, or an unpaid lab internship that "looks better."

Myths that keep you on the treadmillWhat the research says instead.
MYTH Once I get in, I'll be set.

You'll get a real lift, and then you'll adapt. Build a life you like on the way there, not only after.

MYTH People don't want to talk to me.

The commuter studies found people were more open than expected, and the liking gap says you come across better than you think.

MYTH Money doesn't matter.

It does, especially when there isn't enough. It's just one ingredient, and it won't replace time with people.

MYTH A good life feels good all the time.

Meaningful things include hard parts: training, caring for someone, building something. Happy and easy aren't the same.

If it gets heavy

  • Loneliness or low mood has been around most days for weeks.
  • Nothing feels good anymore, even things you used to love.
  • You feel hopeless, or like nobody would notice if you weren't around.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult. You can also call or text 988 any time. See Get help below.

A reminder

Your brain will keep chasing the next thing. That's how it's built. You still decide where your hours go, and the research keeps pointing the same way: people are the best bet.

Other ways in

Writers and artists have been tracking the gap between wanting and liking for a long time. These get it.

📖 Read

The Alchemist · Paulo Coelho, 1988

Santiago crosses a desert for a treasure, and finds out it was buried back where he started. A good book to read with "I'll be happy when..." in mind.

If You Give a Mouse a Cookie · Laura Numeroff, illustrated by Felicia Bond, 1985 Throwback

Every time the mouse gets something, he wants the next thing, and it ends with him wanting another cookie. The wanting system, drawn for preschoolers.

🎬 Watch

Soul · Pete Docter, 2020

Joe gets the gig he has wanted his whole life. Afterward, standing outside, he asks "now what?" Dorothea answers with a story about a fish looking for the ocean. The impact bias, in one scene.

Heads up: Rated PG for thematic elements and some language. Joe spends much of the movie between life and death after an accident.

🎧 Listen

The Happiness Lab · Laurie Santos, 2019

A podcast from the Yale professor who teaches the school's most popular class. She walks through why our guesses about happiness go wrong, and what to do instead.

🖼️ Look

Nighthawks · Edward Hopper, 1942

Four people in a diner late at night, close together and not talking. Hopper said he may have been painting the loneliness of a big city without meaning to. Compare it to the last real conversation you had.

✏️ Make your own

Make two columns. On the left, three things you wanted badly in the past year. On the right, how much you still think about each one now. Then circle a person you saw that week. Which side would they go on?
