Study Drugs and Your Brain

What Adderall and Ritalin do in a brain with and without ADHD, and why the research keeps finding that feeling sharper isn't scoring higher.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why a borrowed pill feels like it's working

Sound familiar?The pressure is real. So is the data.
  • It's finals week and someone in a group chat is selling "addy."
  • You have a prescription, and a teammate asked for two before the SAT.
  • You're taking three APs, working weekends, and sleeping six hours. A shortcut sounds reasonable.
  • You've wondered whether your focus problems are ADHD or just too much on your plate.

Those are fair pressures. Here's what these pills do in the brain, and what the research says happens to grades.

The circuit stimulants push on

Adderall (amphetamine) and Ritalin or Concerta (methylphenidate) raise two chemical signals, dopamine and norepinephrine, by increasing how much is released or slowing how fast it gets cleared away. Here is where those signals start and where they go.

Prefrontal cortex: holds the plan

Keeps your goal and the instructions in mind and blocks distractions. It works best in a middle zone of dopamine and norepinephrine. Too little or too much, and it gets worse.

Striatum: makes it feel worth doing

Part of the reward system. Stimulants turn it up, so dull work feels more interesting. That's the "I could study for hours" feeling. It's real even when the studying isn't any better.

VTA: the dopamine source

The ventral tegmental area, a small cluster in the midbrain that sends dopamine forward to the striatum and the prefrontal cortex.

Locus coeruleus: the alertness source

A tiny spot in the brainstem that supplies norepinephrine. Stimulants push it up, so you feel wired. The same chemical acts on your body, which is why your heart rate climbs.

Why more isn't better

The researchFeels smarter. Scores the same, or worse.
You feel enhanced. You mostly aren't.
In a 2013 double-blind study, healthy young adults took Adderall or a placebo and did 13 tests of memory, reasoning, and creativity. Overall, the drug didn't improve any of them. People still believed they had done better on it.
More effort, lower quality.
In a 2023 study in Science Advances, 40 adults without ADHD worked on a hard planning puzzle after taking methylphenidate, amphetamine, modafinil, or a placebo, without knowing which. On the drugs they worked longer and made more moves, and the quality of their answers dropped. Many people who did best on placebo fell below average on a stimulant.
Students who use them don't come out ahead.
A 2008 study of 1,253 college first-years found students using stimulants without a prescription skipped more classes, studied less, and had lower GPAs by the end of the year. The pill tends to be a patch on a bigger problem.
Fewer people do this than you'd guess.
Lifetime nonmedical stimulant use among 8th, 10th, and 12th graders fell from about 10% in 2005 to under 5% in 2023, in a 2025 analysis of the Monitoring the Future survey.
What it costsThe body pays even when the grade doesn't move.
Sleep.
Stimulants last for hours. Sleep is when your brain stores what you studied, so a pill that keeps you up until 3am works against the test it was meant to help.
Heart and nerves.
Racing heart, higher blood pressure, shaky hands, no appetite, anxiety, irritability, and a crash when it wears off. A prescriber checks for heart problems first. A friend can't.
Dependence.
Misuse, especially high doses or snorting, can lead to addiction. Mixed with alcohol, stimulants hide how drunk you are.
The law.
Adderall, Vyvanse, and Ritalin are controlled substances. Selling or giving away even one pill counts as distribution.
Fake pillsA pill that looks like Adderall may be meth or fentanyl.

The DEA has warned that counterfeit pills are pressed to look like Adderall and other prescriptions, and that they increasingly contain methamphetamine or fentanyl. They are often sold through social media. In DEA lab testing, two in five fake pills with fentanyl held a potentially lethal dose.

If it didn't come from a pharmacy with your name on the label, nobody in the chain knows what's in it, including the person handing it to you.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

What actually raises scores

The real performance stack

Four things with strong evidence and no side effects.

Quiz yourselfIn a classic 2006 study, students who practiced recalling a passage remembered more a week later than students who reread it, even though rereading made them more confident.
Spread it outSeveral short sessions across a week beat one long cram of the same total time. Forgetting a little between sessions makes the next recall stick harder.
Protect sleepSleep after studying is when memories get locked in. An all-nighter trades the storing for more reading.
Ask earlyExtensions, extra help, and learning support are easier to get a week out than the night before.
If you have a prescription

Your medicine is yours.

Tap each one.

1 Keep it private.

You don't owe anyone a reason you take it, or proof that you do.

2 Have a line ready.

"They're counted." "It's dosed for me, not you." "Not happening, but I'll quiz you." Short works.

3 Store it out of reach.

Not in your backpack or locker. If you take a dose during the day, the school nurse can hold it.

4 Tell someone if it doesn't stop.

If people keep pressuring you, or you hear pills are being sold, a counselor can help without making you the story.

Wondering about ADHD?Years, not just finals week.

ADHD is diagnosed when attention or impulse problems started in childhood, show up in more than one setting, and get in the way of life. A stressful semester, too little sleep, or a phone next to you can look similar, and they're worth ruling out.

A pill working doesn't diagnose anything. Stimulants make most people feel more focused, ADHD or not. A real evaluation finds the actual reason and the right support, which might be medication, accommodations, coaching, or a mix.

Families see ADHD and medication differently. Some see a medical question, some see a matter of effort or faith, some worry about labels. If you want an evaluation, a counselor can help you raise it at home in a way that fits your family.

Try itThree situations. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one reasonable one.

At 11pm, Nadia's friend texts: "I have an extra Vyvanse. Want it for the chem test tomorrow?" Nadia hasn't finished the review.

Theo has a prescription. A teammate offers him $20 for five pills before the SAT.

Since middle school, Jae has lost assignments, started projects he doesn't finish, and zoned out in class. His grades swing a lot.

MythsWhat the research says instead.
MYTH It's a prescription drug, so it's safe.

It's safe for the person it was prescribed to, at their dose, after a doctor checked their heart and history. That's the part a borrowed pill skips.

MYTH Everyone's doing it during finals.

Fewer than 1 in 20 teens have ever used one without a prescription, and that number has been falling for almost 20 years.

MYTH If I feel focused, it's working.

In one blinded study, feeling enhanced and actually scoring higher came apart. In another, more effort produced worse answers. The feeling comes from the drug. The score comes from you.

MYTH One pill is no big deal.

One fake pill can contain fentanyl. One real pill can still cost you a night of sleep.

For advisory or classHelps, hurts, or meh?

About 15 minutes. Read each finals-week habit, vote with fingers (1 helps, 2 hurts, 3 meh), then open the research.

Rereading your notes three times

Meh. Familiar words feel like knowledge. Self-testing works much better.

Taking a friend's Adderall

Hurts, or meh at best. You feel sharper, scores barely move, sleep drops, and it's illegal.

Practice tests spread over a week

Helps, a lot. Retrieval practice and spacing are two of the best-studied methods there are.

An all-nighter before the test

Hurts. You walk in having stored less.

  • Why do the habits that feel productive so often lose to the ones that don't?
  • What could our school change to make finals week less of a pressure cooker?
Tip for the person leading

Students with ADHD prescriptions are in the room. Be clear that prescribed medication for ADHD is legitimate treatment. The point is that borrowed pills don't do what people think.

Get help right away if someone

  • Has chest pain, a pounding heart that won't slow down, or faints.
  • Has a seizure, a very high temperature, or is confused.
  • Is extremely panicky or paranoid after taking a stimulant.
  • Took a pill that didn't come from a pharmacy and is very sleepy or hard to wake. That could be fentanyl. Give naloxone (Narcan) if you have it.

Call 911 in an emergency. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222, any time. If school stress has you feeling hopeless, call or text 988. See Get help below.
