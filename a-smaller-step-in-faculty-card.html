A Smaller Step In

Helping an anxious student in the moment. Anxiety asks for a way out. Offer a smaller step in.

Notes from a 45-minute faculty talk · Postpone, skip, leave, check again

What anxiety wantsSay it in two partsWhen a student asksFor everyoneEvery student

The pattern

What anxiety wants

Certainty and comfort. Postponing, skipping, leaving and checking again all deliver both, right away. The relief is real, and it teaches the worry that the task was dangerous, so it asks louder next time.

Offer this

A smaller step in

  • Shrinks the task, keeps the student in it
  • Sets a time to come back
  • Says the worry makes sense
  • Shows you think they can do it

Instead of this

A way out

  • Removes the task or the deadline
  • Answers the same worry again and again
  • Lets the student leave and not return
  • Says “don’t worry” or “just do it”

The words

Say it in two parts

“This feels really hard right now. And I know you can handle it.”

Accept the feeling, then show confidence. Skip “don’t worry,” which argues with the feeling, and “just do it,” which skips it.

In the moment

When a student asks

  1. “Can I take the test another day?”

    “Of course you’re nervous. It’s a test. Start with the questions you know, and I’ll check in after the first page.”

  2. “Can I skip this assignment?”

    Keep the work, shrink today’s piece. “Do the first two problems, then show me.”

  3. “Can I go to the nurse?” (right before the hard part)

    “Yes, after you write your first sentence,” or “Go, and come back in five minutes. I’ll hold your spot.” A real illness is always a yes.

  4. “I can’t present in front of everyone.”

    Lower the step: present to you at lunch, then to a small group, then seated with notes. Each time, one step closer.

  5. “Is this right? Can you check again?”

    Answer once. Then: “You’ve asked, and I think you know. Make your best guess and keep going.”

  6. Freezes, tears, or a racing heart

    “Your body’s alarm went off. It will pass. Breathe out slowly. Stay here with me or take two minutes in the hall, then come back.”

Whole class

In the room, for everyone

  • Make it predictable. Post the plan. Warn before you call on someone: “I’ll come to you for number 3.”
  • Name nerves as normal. “Most people feel nervous before a test. That’s your body getting ready to work.”
  • Practice the hard thing small. Low-stakes quizzes, partner run-throughs, one question out loud before a full presentation.
  • Talk about worry as a thing, not a trait. “What’s the worry telling you?” gives the student some distance from it.
  • Ten minutes of writing before a big test. Writing the worries down before an exam helped anxious students score higher.

Every student in the room

Not every worry looks the same

Anxiety doesn’t always look nervous. It can show up as stomachaches, anger, jokes, perfectionism or a quiet student who never asks. Some families see worry as private or as weakness, and some students are anxious about real things at home, like money, immigration or discrimination. Name what’s real, and keep the step small.

Department theme

Don’t carry it alone.

Bring in the counseling team and learning support when the avoidance is spreading to other classes, a student misses school to avoid a test, panic keeps coming back, or a student seems low for weeks.

Accommodations in a learning plan stay as written. This card is about the extra requests.
