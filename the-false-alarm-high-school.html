The False Alarm

The alarm feels true. It's just loud.

What's going on

Anxiety is a forecast: your brain predicts danger. Avoiding means the forecast never gets checked, so it sticks around. Staying, and seeing the disaster not happen, is how the alarm recalibrates.

A call, a game, a party→Your brain predicts danger→Your body revs→You leave, check, or ask→Relief for a minute→The prediction never gets tested

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "I'll freeze, and they'll think I'm an idiot."
  • "Are you sure it's fine?"
  • "I said something wrong."

🫀 Body

  • tight chest in the parking lot
  • pounding heart, short breath
  • shaking hands

🔁 Behaviors

  • asking a parent to make the call
  • rechecking the portal or chat
  • turning down the job or audition

Where to step in

Run the experiment.

  1. Name the prediction, specifically, and rate how sure you are from 0 to 100%.
  2. Drop the safety moves: the script, the excuse, the friend who talks for you.
  3. Compare: what happened, and how close was the prediction? Then climb the next rung.

Part 1 · Understand it

How anxiety learns

Sound familiar?Your body treats it like an emergency. It usually isn't one.
  • You rehearsed a call to the doctor's office for twenty minutes, then asked a parent to make it.
  • Your chest tightens in the parking lot before a game, an interview, or a party.
  • You left a store, a class, or a party because your heart was pounding and you weren't sure why.
  • You check the grade portal or the group chat again and again, and the relief lasts about a minute.
  • You're turning down things you want, like the job, the trip, or the audition, because of what anxiety predicts will happen.

You're far from alone. In a national survey of more than 10,000 U.S. teens, about 1 in 3 met criteria for an anxiety disorder at some point by age 18 (Merikangas et al., 2010). This lesson goes past "it's common": how anxiety learns, and how to teach it something new.

The avoidance loop, in one picture

Leaving: fast relief, bigger alarm

Escaping feels great for a minute. Your brain files it as proof: "That was dangerous. Good thing we left." Next time the alarm fires sooner and louder, and the list of things you avoid grows.

Staying: the alarm runs its course

Your body can't stay at peak alarm for long. When you stay, the feeling crests and settles, and your brain collects new evidence: I handled that.

Repeating: the prediction updates

Each time the disaster doesn't happen, the old fear has to compete with a new memory. That is how the alarm gets recalibrated.

The researchExposure works by proving the prediction wrong.
Anxiety is a forecast.
Your brain is predicting danger. Avoiding means the forecast never gets checked, so it sticks around.
You don't have to feel calm for it to work.
Michelle Craske's team at UCLA argues that exposure works mainly through expectation violation: you predicted something bad, it didn't happen, and your brain stores a new memory that competes with the fear (Craske et al., 2014). So the useful questions are "What exactly do I expect?" before, and "What actually happened?" after.
Reassurance is avoidance in disguise.
Asking "Are you sure it's fine?" or having someone else handle it brings relief and keeps the prediction untested. In one trial with kids ages 7 to 14, a program that coached parents to stop accommodating anxiety, while staying warm and supportive, worked as well as therapy with the child directly (Lebowitz et al., 2020).
Treatment works.
In a major U.S. trial with ages 7 to 17, about 60% improved with 12 weeks of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), 55% with the medication sertraline, and 81% with both, compared with 24% on placebo (Walkup et al., 2008).
PanicIt feels like an emergency. It isn't one.

A panic attack is a surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes: pounding heart, short breath, dizziness, shaking, a sense that something terrible is happening. It is your alarm firing at full volume with no fire. It is frightening, and it passes.

If you've never had symptoms like chest pain checked by a doctor, get checked once. After that, the work is learning that the sensations themselves are safe.

The alarm feels like it's telling the truth. It's just loud.

Different words for itNot every family calls it anxiety.

Some families call it stress, nerves, or overthinking. In some, it shows up mostly as stomachaches, headaches, or trouble sleeping, and gets treated as a body problem. Some handle it through faith, family, or community, and some don't talk about it much at all. Any of those can sit alongside the tools here.

What words does your family use for this feeling? Who do people in your family go to when it's bad?

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Teach the alarm something new

The core skill

Run the experiment.

Therapists call this exposure. Think of it as testing a prediction, one step at a time.

Name the predictionBe specific: "If I call, I'll freeze, and they'll think I'm an idiot."
Rate itHow sure are you, from 0 to 100%? How bad would it be if it happened?
Drop the safety movesSkip the script, the excuse ready to go, the friend who talks for you. Safety moves keep the test from counting.
CompareWhat happened? How close was the prediction? What did you learn about what you can handle?

More tools. Tap to see how.

1 Call it excitement.

In experiments with karaoke, public speaking, and math tests, people who said "I am excited" beforehand did better than people who tried to calm down (Brooks, 2014). Anxiety and excitement feel alike in your body. The label changes how you use the energy.

2 Breathe out longer.

In for 4, out for 6. Use it to stay in the situation, not to get out of it.

3 Give worry an appointment.

Pick 15 minutes a day for worrying. When a worry shows up at another time, write it down and save it. Many look smaller by then.

4 Ask for coaching, not rescue.

Ask the adults in your life to back you while you do the hard thing, instead of doing it for you.

5 Watch the fuel.

Short sleep, energy drinks, and nicotine withdrawal from vaping all crank up the alarm. Regular exercise turns it down.

Try itBuild a ladder, and test it one rung at a time.

Pick one thing anxiety makes you avoid. Write four steps, from easiest at the bottom to hardest at the top. Before each step, jot your prediction. After, note what happened.

Example, phone calls: 1. Call a store to ask their hours. 2. Call to schedule a haircut. 3. Leave a voicemail for a coach or teacher. 4. Call to book your own doctor's appointment.

Repeat a step until the prediction stops holding up, then climb. Sliding back a step on a hard day is normal.

In a groupReal danger or burnt toast? Vote.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read each story out loud. These are made-up students, not anyone in the room.
  2. Everyone votes: real danger, false alarm, or somewhere in between. Raise hands or point to a corner.
  3. Tap to see what usually helps, then talk about the brave next step.

What usually helps

False alarm. Canceling would feel like relief and teach his brain that interviews are dangerous. A brave next step: write down his prediction, go, and compare. Interviewers expect nerves, especially for a first job.

What usually helps

Real fire. Her alarm is doing its job. Call 911, stay with her, and roll her onto her side. Anxiety work is about burnt toast. It never means ignoring real danger.

Toast or fire? · 3 of 3Dev and the silent group chat

Dev's friends haven't answered his message in three hours. He has reread it twenty times, he's sure he said something wrong, and he's writing an apology.

  1. Real danger
  2. False alarm
  3. Somewhere in between
What usually helps

Mostly a false alarm. People get busy. Rereading and apologizing keep the prediction from being tested. A brave next step: phone away for an hour, then see. If something did land wrong, a calm check-in later works better than a panicked apology.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • What are some common false alarms for people our age? Keep it general.
  • Why does avoiding feel so good in the moment, and when does it start to cost something?
  • What could a friend say that helps someone approach instead of avoid?
Tip for the person leading

Never ask students to name their own fears. If someone volunteers, keep it brief and thank them. Some students in the room may have panic attacks or an anxiety disorder, so let everyone know a counselor is available afterward.

Try itTwo moments. Which way do you step?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

You're about to present in history. Your heart is pounding and your mouth is dry.

You need to call and book your own dentist appointment for the first time. You've put it off for two weeks.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths that keep you stuckWhat the research says instead.
MYTH Avoiding it is self-care.

Rest and boundaries are self-care. Skipping things because anxiety predicts disaster usually makes the next time harder.

MYTH I have to feel calm before I can do it.

You can do hard things while anxious. Going in nervous and finding out it went fine is often what teaches your brain the most.

MYTH A vape or a hit takes the edge off.

It can numb things for a night. Nicotine withdrawal creates anxious feelings of its own, and none of it teaches your brain anything new about what you're avoiding.

MYTH Anxiety means something is wrong with me.

Anxiety is one of the most common conditions in teens, and one of the most treatable.

When to get more help

  • Anxiety shows up most days, for weeks.
  • You're missing school, practices, work, or time with friends because of it.
  • You're having panic attacks.
  • It's messing with your sleep or eating.
  • You're using vapes, weed, alcohol, or pills to cope.
  • You feel hopeless, or have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Anxiety responds well to treatment, and a counselor can help you find the right kind. See Get help below.
