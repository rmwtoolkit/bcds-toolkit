Mental Health at College

At college, you build the system that notices.

What's going on

At home, people noticed when you weren't okay. College replaces that with a system you run yourself. Help is opt-in, structure disappears, and your health information becomes yours.

Someone always noticed→You move away→Nobody notices by default→Help is opt-in→Waiting for "bad enough"

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "I'll wait until it's bad enough."
  • "Everything's great."
  • "I'm fine off my meds now."

🫀 Body

  • sleeping far more or less
  • one real meal a day
  • in bed past noon

🔁 Behaviors

  • skipping classes, meals, or plans
  • staying in the room all day
  • drinking more

Where to step in

Build the net before you need it.

  1. Plan the handoff: ask your provider who takes over at school and how refills work.
  2. Find the counseling center and put its after-hours number in your phone tonight.
  3. Make an appointment, not a decision. You don't have to know if it's bad enough.

Call or text 988, use your campus counseling after-hours line, tell an RA, or call campus safety or 911 in an emergency.

Part 1 · Understand it

What changes when you leave home

The 2 a.m. questionWho do you call at 2 a.m.?

Someone at school, not only at home, who would pick up, walk over, or notice you didn't come back.

If the answer is "not yet," that's normal in the first weeks. It's also the first thing to work on.

Everything in this series comes back to this question.

Sound familiar?Someone always noticed. Soon, nobody will by default.
  • At home, somebody noticed when you skipped dinner or stayed in your room all weekend.
  • A parent made your appointments, picked up your prescriptions, and filled out the forms.
  • Teachers, coaches, and counselors knew you well enough to say, "You seem off."
  • You're excited to leave, and a little worried about who you'll be without the people who know you.

College replaces all of that with a system you have to operate yourself. The good news: you can set most of it up before you go.

Why the timing matters

Many conditions start early

About a third of people who will ever have a mental health condition already have symptoms by 14. If you've dealt with anxiety, depression, ADHD, or an eating problem in high school, you're far from alone, and your plan should travel with you.

The college years are prime time

The peak age of onset is mid-adolescence, and new cases keep appearing through the late teens and early 20s. That's exactly when you're changing where you live, who you know, and how you sleep.

Don't wait for proof

Close to two-thirds of conditions have started by 25. Problems are usually easier to turn around when they're caught early, which is the case for booking the appointment before you're sure it's "bad enough."

What the data showsStruggling is common. Staying stuck is the signal.
35%of first-year students across 19 colleges in 8 countries screened positive for a lifetime mental health condition. 31% had one in the past year.
37%of U.S. college students had moderate to severe depression symptoms in 2024 to 2025. 32% had moderate to severe anxiety.
52%reported high loneliness. That number, like depression and anxiety, has improved since 2022.

Sources: the WHO World Mental Health International College Student Project (Auerbach and colleagues, 2018) and the Healthy Minds Study, 2024 to 2025 report. These are screening surveys, not diagnoses, so they measure symptoms, not who has a condition.

What changesHelp exists. You have to go get it.
Help is opt-in.
Campus counseling and health services are real and often free or low-cost. Nobody signs you up. Many centers have wait times in busy weeks, which is one more reason to find them early.
Structure disappears.
No bells, no curfew, no one making dinner. In a review of college sleep research, about half of students reported daytime sleepiness and most weren't getting enough sleep, which is tied to lower grades and worse mood (Hershner and Chervin, 2014).
Your health information becomes yours.
At 18, doctors and counselors generally can't share your health information with your parents without your permission, even if you're on their insurance. Grades and records work similarly. Decide with your family what you want shared, and sign release forms if you want them involved.
Your care might not travel.
A therapist or doctor licensed in your home state may not be able to keep seeing you once you live in another state. Ask now, before you need it.
You might be the one who notices.
Without parents around, a roommate or floormate is often the first to see that someone is struggling.

Warning signs, in you or someone else

Tap each one.

Pulling back

Skipping classes, meals, or plans. Staying in the room most of the day.

"Everything's great"

Telling family it's fine while grades, sleep, or friendships are slipping.

Big changes

Sleeping far more or less, drinking more, losing interest in things you used to care about.

"What's the point"

Hopeless talk, giving things away, saying people would be better off without them. That one means act now.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Build the safety net before you need it

The summer before

Six things to set up before move-in.

Tap each one.

1 Plan the handoff.

If you see a therapist or take medication, ask your provider now: Can you keep seeing me from another state? Who takes over at school? How do refills work there? Bring a short written history so you don't start from zero.

2 Find the counseling center.

Location, walk-in hours, how to book, and the after-hours number. Put it in your phone tonight.

3 Decide what your family can see.

Families differ a lot here. Some expect to know everything; some never talk about mental health. Talk it through, then decide whether to sign a release for health information, a health care proxy for emergencies, or both.

4 Pick two anchors.

A regular bedtime, real breakfast, a run, a weekly call home, a place of worship, a club. Protect two of them hardest in the first month.

5 Make an appointment, not a decision.

You don't have to know if it's "bad enough." Book one session and let a professional help you sort it out.

6 Tell the truth on the call home.

"It's been harder than I expected" is a full sentence. Most families would rather know.

ResearchHow you read a bad first month matters. In a Stanford study, first-year students read that worries about belonging are common at first and fade with time. Over the next three years, Black students who got that message earned higher grades, cutting the racial achievement gap roughly in half, and reported better health and fewer doctor visits (Walton and Cohen, 2011). It was one small study at one school, so read it as a strong hint: a rough start is usually a phase, not a verdict.

In a groupTwo stories. What would you do?

For advisory or class · about 20 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read each story out loud, slowly.
  2. Talk through the questions in small groups or as a class.
  3. Then tap to see moves that tend to help.
Story 1 of 2Imani

Imani is the first in her family to go to college, four hours from home. Her parents work long shifts and talk about her at church like she's already graduated. Five weeks in, she's stopped going to her 9 a.m. chemistry lecture and eats one real meal a day. On the Sunday call she says everything's great, because she doesn't want them to worry or think the sacrifice was wasted. In her family, you handle problems yourself or you pray about them. Therapy feels like something other people do.

  1. What's making it hard for Imani to ask for help?
  2. What makes this harder at college than at home?
  3. What's one step that fits who she is?
Moves that help

It counts, and she doesn't have to choose between her family's values and getting support. She could go to the counseling center, a first-generation student office, or a campus chaplain if faith is how she makes sense of things. She could email her chemistry professor before she falls further behind, and tell one person the truth. Rebuilding one anchor, like breakfast, is a real start.

Story 2 of 2Wes and Arjun

Wes and his roommate Arjun get along but aren't close. Arjun was diagnosed with depression in high school and stopped his medication when his prescription ran out in September. He didn't know how to get it refilled at school and figured he'd be fine. Lately he's in bed past noon, has missed two exams, and last night told Wes, "Honestly, I don't know why I'm here." Wes isn't sure if that means college or something worse.

  1. What would you do if you were Wes?
  2. What could Arjun have set up before move-in?
  3. Who on campus would help?
Moves that help

Wes can ask directly: "When you said you don't know why you're here, did you mean you're thinking about killing yourself?" If yes, or if he's unsure, he calls 988 or campus safety now and tells the RA. Either way, he can offer to walk Arjun to health services about the medication. Stopping some medications suddenly can make people feel worse, so that visit matters.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • How does your family talk about mental health, or not? How might that shape what you do at college?
  • What would you want a roommate to do if they were worried about you?
  • What's one thing you'll set up this summer so you're not starting from zero?
Tip for the person leading

Pause on the 2 a.m. question before you start, and let the silence work. Avoid treating one family's approach as the right one. Remind students a counselor here is available after the session.

What doesn't helpThese usually make it worse.
MYTH I'll wait until it's bad enough.

There's no bar to clear. Earlier is easier, and counseling centers would rather see you in week three than week ten.

MYTH Telling home it's great protects them.

It keeps the people who love you from helping. You can choose how much to share without pretending.

MYTH A few drinks help me sleep and relax.

Alcohol can knock you out, but it breaks up sleep later in the night and tends to worsen mood and anxiety over time.

MYTH I'm fine off my meds now.

Maybe, but make that call with a prescriber, not because a refill got complicated.

Act right away if you or someone else

  • Talks about wanting to die, not wanting to be here, or not seeing the point.
  • Is giving things away or saying goodbye.
  • Can't get out of bed for days, or stops eating.

Call or text 988, use your campus counseling after-hours line, tell an RA, or call campus safety or 911 in an emergency. See Get help below.
