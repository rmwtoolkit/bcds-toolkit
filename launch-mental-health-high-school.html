Part 1 · Understand it

What changes when you leave home

🌙 Who do you call at 2 a.m.?Who do you call at 2 a.m.?

Do you have that person yet? Someone at school, not just at home, who would pick up, walk over, or notice you didn't come back?

If the answer is "not yet," that's normal in the first weeks. It's also the thing to work on first.

Everything in this series comes back to this question.

Sound familiar?At home, someone always noticed.
  • At home, someone noticed when you skipped dinner or stayed in your room all weekend.
  • Your parents, teachers, coaches, and counselors knew you, and they checked in without you asking.
  • You've never had to find a doctor, book an appointment, or refill a prescription on your own.
  • You're excited to leave, and a little worried about who you'll be without the people who know you.

At home, someone always noticed. At college, you're the first line of defense, for yourself and for the people around you.

What changesHelp exists. You have to go get it.
🏫 Help exists, but you have to go get it.
Campus counseling centers are real and usually free. Nobody will sign you up. You have to find them and ask.
🌧 First semester is hard for almost everyone.
Homesickness, anxiety, and low mood are extremely common in the first months. Feeling off is normal. Staying stuck there is the thing to watch.
😴 Small things add up fast.
Less sleep, irregular meals, and being alone a lot can turn a rough week into a rough month.
🤐 Most people white-knuckle it.
Lots of students wait until things are really bad before asking for help. Stigma is real, and so is the cost of waiting.
👀 You might be the one who notices.
Without parents around, a roommate or floormate is often the first person to see that someone's struggling.

Warning signs, in you or someone else

Tap each one.

🛏Pulling back

Skipping classes, meals, or plans. Staying in the room most of the day.

🎭"Everything's great"

Telling family it's fine while things are slipping.

⚡Big changes

Sleeping way more or way less, drinking more, losing interest in things they used to love.

🕳"What's the point"

Hopeless talk, giving things away, saying people would be better off without them. That one means act now.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Building your own safety net

What actually helps

Know your resources before you need them.

Tap one to see how.

📇 Know your resources before move-in.

Knowing where the counseling center is before you need it is a form of self-care.

🧭 Keep a few anchors.

Sleep, real meals, moving your body, and one thing that feels like you. They protect your mood more than people expect.

🗓 Make an appointment, not a decision.

You don't have to know if it's "bad enough." Book one session and let a professional help you sort it out.

📞 Tell the truth on the Sunday call.

Your family would rather know. "It's been harder than I expected" is a full sentence.

🤝 Check on people, even if it's awkward.

"Hey, you've seemed off lately. Want to grab food?" Being a little awkward beats saying nothing.

🧑‍💼 Use your RA.

Noticing a floormate struggling and telling an RA is a way of helping. RAs are trained for exactly this.

👥 Maya and Tomás · 1 of 2Maya

Maya is four weeks into her first semester. She was a strong student in high school: involved, social, on top of everything. About two weeks after move-in, something shifted. She stopped going to her 8 a.m. lecture, then her Thursday seminar. She's in her room most of the day, sometimes not eating until late at night. Her parents call every Sunday and she says things are great, partly because they sacrificed a lot to get her here. Nothing dramatic happened. She's just not okay, and she doesn't know who to tell or whether it even counts as a real problem.

  1. What would you do in this situation?
  2. What makes this harder in college than at home?
  3. Who or what resources would help?
Moves that help

It counts. Maya could walk into the counseling center or book a first appointment, tell one person the truth (a parent, an RA, a friend from home), email one professor, and rebuild one anchor: a real breakfast, a walk, one class. She doesn't have to fix everything this week.

👥 Maya and Tomás · 2 of 2Jake and Tomás

Jake's roommate Tomás has been off for a while: quiet, skipping meals, in bed past noon. They're not close, and Jake doesn't want to make it weird. He invites Tomás to dinner a few times. Tomás always says no. One night Jake comes home and Tomás is sitting in the dark, clearly crying. Tomás says he's fine and turns toward the wall. Jake doesn't know if this is a "tell someone" situation or if he'd be overstepping.

  1. What would you do in this situation?
  2. What makes this harder in college than at home?
  3. Who or what resources would help?
Moves that help

Jake can sit down and say, "I'm not trying to pry. You don't seem fine, and I'm around if you want to talk." He can mention the counseling center, keep inviting, and tell their RA he's worried. If Tomás ever says anything about not wanting to be here, Jake tells the RA or calls 988 right away. Telling isn't betrayal. It's how people get help.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • If you were Tomás, what would you want Jake to do?
  • What gets in the way of asking for help, for you or people you know?
  • What's one thing you'll set up before you leave so you're not starting from zero?
Tip for the person leading

Pause on the 2 a.m. question before you start, and let the silence work. Remind students they can always call their parents, and that a counselor here is available after the session.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually make it worse.
🙂 "Things are great!"

Pretending on the phone keeps the people who love you from helping.

⏳ Waiting until it's bad enough.

There's no bar to clear. Earlier is easier.

🍺 Drinking or smoking to cope.

It numbs things for a night and makes mood and sleep worse over time.

🚪 Staying in the room.

Isolation makes everything heavier. One meal with people helps.

Act right away if you or someone else

  • Talks about wanting to die, not wanting to be here, or not seeing the point.
  • Is giving things away or saying goodbye.
  • Can't get out of bed for days, or stops eating.

Call or text 988, call campus safety, or 911 in an emergency. Tell an RA. See Get help below.
