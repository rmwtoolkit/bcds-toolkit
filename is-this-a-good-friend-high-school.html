A Good Friend?

What's going on

A good friendship leaves you more like yourself. Some cost you instead: the rollercoaster, the put-down, the controller. Mixed friendships can cost more than you'd think, because you can't fully relax.

Part 1 · Understand it

What a good friendship actually does

Sound familiar?Not toxic, exactly. Just costing you something.
  • You leave hangouts more tired than when you arrived, and you can't say why.
  • A friend's "jokes" about your grades, your body, or your family land a little too hard, a little too often.
  • You told a friend your good news and within a minute it was about them.
  • You and your closest friend can talk about your problems for hours, and you both end up feeling worse.
  • With this group, you do things you wouldn't choose on your own: in the car, at parties, in the group chat.

You probably learned the red flags years ago. This is the next layer: what research says separates friendships that help you from ones that cost you, including the gray areas where it's both.

The good-news test

Most people judge a friend by how they show up when things go wrong. Psychologist Shelly Gable and her colleagues found that how people respond when things go right may matter at least as much.

What they found

Couples whose partners responded to good news with real interest and questions were more satisfied and less likely to break up two months later. Shrugging, poking holes, or changing the subject went with dissatisfaction.

Why it's a useful test

Bad news can pull support out of almost anyone. Being glad for you, without competing, is harder. Notice which box your friends usually land in, and which box you usually land in.

Research

In a study at Brigham Young University, adults talked about a stressful event with a friend. Talking with an ambivalent friend, someone who is sometimes supportive and sometimes hurtful, raised their blood pressure more than talking with a supportive friend. Their resting heart rates were higher too. The researchers suggested people can't fully relax around a friend they can't predict.

The gray areasClose isn't automatically healthy.
Talking it to death.
Psychologist Amanda Rose studied "co-rumination": friends going over the same problems again and again. It was linked with closer, higher-quality friendships and with more anxiety and depression. A friendship can feel deeply supportive and still be keeping both of you stuck.
The audience effect.
In a driving video game, teenagers took more risks when friends were watching than when they played alone. The effect was much smaller in adults. Good friends can still make it harder to say no, especially in a car.
A friend nearby helps.
In a study of fifth and sixth graders, bad experiences raised the stress hormone cortisol and lowered self-worth less when a best friend was there. A good friend changes how stress hits you, in real time.
Patterns that wear you downThe problem is the pattern, not one bad day.
The rollercoaster.
Warm one week, cold the next. Unpredictable warmth keeps you working to earn it back, which is part of why these friendships are hard to leave.
The put-down.
Teasing that always lands on the same sore spots, followed by "relax, it's a joke." If you've said it hurts and it continues, it isn't a joke.
The controller.
Jealousy about your other friends, guilt trips about your time, keeping track of where you are, or holding screenshots and secrets over you.

The cost often shows up as shrinking. You get quieter, more careful, more worried. A good friendship tends to leave you more like yourself, not less.

OnlineA controlling friend can follow you everywhere.
  • They get upset when you don't answer fast, or ask why you were online and didn't reply.
  • They want your location on all the time, and punish you for turning it off.
  • They post or forward things about you as "jokes."
  • They threaten to share screenshots, private messages, or photos.

Online, a controlling friend is in your pocket all day. That makes it harder to get the distance you need to see the pattern clearly.

What actually helps

You can change a friendship without ending it.

Tap one to see how.

1 Judge over months, not moments.

Everyone has bad weeks. Look at the last few months. What's the usual pattern?

2 Say it once, clearly.

People don't always know how they come across. "When you joke about my grades in front of people, it gets to me. Can you drop it?" How they react tells you a lot.

3 Set a boundary you control.

A boundary is what you will do, not what they have to do. "If you keep checking my location, I'm turning it off." Then follow through.

4 Change the loop.

If you co-ruminate, put a limit on it: "Let's talk about it for ten minutes, then go do something." Talking still helps. Endless replaying doesn't.

5 Turn the volume down.

You don't have to end a friendship to change it. Spend less time together, share less, and put more energy into people who leave you better off.

6 Get an outside view.

A parent, an older sibling, a counselor, a friend outside the group. Someone without a stake in it can see the pattern faster.

It depends where you're from

What counts as a good friend varies. In some families and cultures, close friends are treated like family: constant contact, showing up without asking, sharing almost everything. In others, more privacy and independence is normal. Neither is wrong. The real questions are whether the friendship respects you and fits the values you and your family hold. Myths that keep you stuckWhat the research says instead.
MYTH A real friend would never hurt me.

Good friends hurt each other sometimes. What matters is whether they notice, own it, and change.

MYTH The closer we are, the healthier it is.

Closeness and co-rumination can go together. A friendship can be very close and still keep both people stuck.

MYTH It's not all bad, so I should stay.

Mixed friendships can cost more than you'd think, because you can't fully relax. That doesn't mean you have to end it. It means it's worth adjusting.

MYTH Setting a boundary is mean.

A clear boundary is information. It gives the friendship a chance to work. A good friend can handle it.

Online, right nowMute, restrict, block. That's a boundary, not a war.
Your location is yours.
You don't owe anyone a live map of where you are. Turning it off is not betrayal.
You don't owe instant replies.
Friends who respect you understand that you have a life.
Save evidence.
If someone threatens to share your messages or photos, screenshot the threat and tell an adult. For images of anyone under 18, the free Take It Down tool from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children can help get them removed.
Use mute, restrict, or block.
These tools exist for a reason. Using them is setting a boundary.

When it's more than a draining friendship

Talk to an adult right away if a friend:

  • Threatens to share your secrets, photos, or screenshots to control you.
  • Threatens to hurt you, themselves, or someone else.
  • Pressures you into things that are unsafe or that you don't want to do.
  • Gets others to gang up on you.

That's not a friendship problem you have to fix alone. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Stories have been testing friendships for a long time. A couple of these go back to when you were small.

The Hate U Give · Angie Thomas, 2017

Starr's friend Hailey keeps making comments about race and calls them jokes. When Starr says it hurts and it keeps happening, she has to decide what this friendship is costing her.

Heads up: The story starts with a police shooting of Starr's childhood friend. It includes violence, death and strong language.

🎬 Watch

Toy Story · Pixar, 1995 Throwback

Buzz shows up and everyone loves him. Woody can't be glad for him. It's the good-news test, failed in a big way, and then fixed. Rated G.

Parks and Recreation · NBC, 2009-2015

Leslie Knope is the top-left box in the good-news test. When Ann has good news, Leslie gets more excited than Ann does.

Heads up: Rated TV-PG. Some sexual humor; Common Sense Media says 13 and up.

Stand by Me · Ben E. King, 1961

"I won't be afraid, just as long as you stand by me." Researchers found the same thing with a best friend in the room: stress hits softer.

Peanuts: Lucy and the football · Charles M. Schulz, 1952 on Throwback

Every time, Lucy promises she'll hold the ball, and every time, she pulls it away. Charlie Brown keeps coming back. That's the rollercoaster, in four panels.
