Mushrooms and Psychedelics

Low addiction risk. Real psychological risk.

What's going on

Psilocybin and LSD switch on serotonin 2A receptors in the cortex, and areas that normally keep each other in check lose their grip. Once it starts, it can't be switched off for 4 to 12 hours.

An unknown product or dose→The inner narrator quiets→The filter drops→Patterns that aren't there→Hours of panic, no exit

Part 1 · Understand it

What a psychedelic actually changes

Sound familiar?Miracle cure or brain damage. Neither is the full story.
  • A podcast host calls psilocybin the future of mental health.
  • A smoke shop or gas station near you sells "mushroom" gummies in bright packaging.
  • Someone says they microdose before exams to stay calm and creative.
  • You know someone who had a trip they still don't like talking about.

You've heard the hype and the scare version. This is what the research shows, including what it hasn't shown yet.

Four areas where the trip happens

Psilocybin (in mushrooms) and LSD work mainly by switching on one type of serotonin receptor, called 2A. Those receptors are packed into the outer layer of the brain, the cortex. When they fire, areas that normally keep each other in check start to lose their grip.

Medial prefrontal cortex: your inner narrator

A hub of the default mode network, the system that keeps a running story of who you are and what might happen next. In a 2012 brain-imaging study, psilocybin lowered activity here, and the bigger the drop, the stronger people rated the trip.

Posterior cingulate: the other hub

The same study found psilocybin weakened the connection between this area and the prefrontal cortex. Researchers link that to the feeling of losing your sense of self. Some people find it freeing. Others find it terrifying, and you don't get to pick in advance.

Thalamus: the filter

Think of it as a sound engineer deciding which tracks reach your awareness and how loud. Its activity dropped too. With less filtering, sounds, colors, and thoughts arrive faster and less sorted.

Visual cortex: where the visuals come from

Dense with 2A receptors. When its signals stop being checked against the rest of the brain as tightly, you see patterns, motion, and color that aren't there.

None of this can be switched off once it starts. Depending on the drug and the dose, it lasts 4 to 12 hours.

The researchPromising in clinics. Early, small, and adults only.
The trials are real.
In a 2021 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine, 59 adults with long-term depression got either two psilocybin sessions or six weeks of a standard antidepressant, both with therapy. Psilocybin did about as well. On the main measure it wasn't significantly better, though several other measures leaned its way.
The conditions are strict.
Research guidelines published in 2008 still shape these studies: screen out anyone with a personal or family history of psychosis, use a measured dose of pure drug, and keep trained monitors in the room the whole time. The authors named overwhelming distress during the session as the most likely risk.
Teens aren't in the studies.
The trials enroll adults. Your brain is still wiring its long-distance connections into your mid-20s, and nobody has tested what strong doses do during that window.
Microdosing mostly matches placebo.
In a 2021 study, people who already microdosed set up their own blind test with real doses and empty capsules. Over four weeks, both groups improved about the same. Expectation did most of the work.
What can go wrongLow addiction risk. Real psychological risk.
Hard trips.
Panic, paranoia, or the certainty that you're dying or going insane, for hours, with no exit. This is the most common harm.
Accidents.
Perception and judgment change. Roads, water, heights, and crowds are where a bad trip becomes an injury.
Psychosis.
For people with a personal or family history of psychosis, schizophrenia, or bipolar disorder, a trip can set off an episode that doesn't end when the drug wears off. That's why trials screen for it.
Flashbacks that stay.
A small number of people keep having visual changes for weeks or longer, a condition called hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD).
Mixing.
Alcohol, weed, or other drugs on top make reactions harder to predict and harder to manage.

Some Indigenous communities have used psychedelic plants and mushrooms in ceremony for centuries, inside traditions with their own guides, rules, and preparation. That's a different thing from a party or a gummy from a shop, and it's worth not lumping them together.

What's in the bag"Mushroom" gummies are often not mushrooms.

In 2024, Diamond Shruumz chocolates and gummies were recalled nationwide. The FDA linked them to at least 48 illnesses in 24 states, including seizures, 27 hospitalizations, and one possible death. The company's own testing found high levels of muscimol, a compound from a different mushroom.

Between 2019 and 2023, poison center calls about psilocybin among 12 to 17 year olds rose 317%, according to a 2025 study in Annals of Internal Medicine.

Being sold in a store doesn't mean it was tested, or that it contains what the label says.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Thinking it through, and having a plan

Before anyone hands you anything

Four questions with real answers.

What is it, actually?Dried mushrooms vary a lot in strength. Gummies and "microdose" products may not contain psilocybin at all. You can't see a dose.
What's in my family?Psychosis, schizophrenia, or bipolar disorder in a parent or sibling raises your risk. If you don't know, that's worth finding out.
Where would I be?Strangers, traffic, water, and heights are where things go wrong. A trip doesn't end because the party did.
What's the law here?Psilocybin and LSD are illegal in Massachusetts. In 2024, about 57% of voters rejected a ballot question that would have legalized psilocybin and some other natural psychedelics.
If someone is having a hard trip

Stay, lower the noise, keep it simple.

Tap each step.

1 Stay with them.

Panic and accidents are much more likely alone. If you have to step away, hand off to someone you trust, by name.

2 Turn the input down.

A quieter, dimmer spot away from the crowd. Sitting down. Fewer people talking at them.

3 Say the same few things.

"You took something. It's going to wear off. I'm right here." Calm and repeated beats clever.

4 Know when to get help.

Get event medical staff or call 911 if they're hurting themselves, can't be calmed, have a seizure, chest pain, or trouble breathing, or took an unknown product and are getting sick. Massachusetts' Good Samaritan law protects people who call for help with a drug overdose from possession charges.

5 Check in the next day.

Some people feel shaky or low for days after a hard trip. A counselor can help them make sense of it.

MythsWhat the research says instead.
MYTH It's natural, so it's safe.

"Natural" describes where something comes from, not what it does. Some of the most toxic compounds known come from plants and mushrooms.

MYTH It isn't addictive, so it's harmless.

The low addiction risk is real. The harms come from somewhere else: hours of panic, injuries, and psychosis in people who are vulnerable.

MYTH A trip sitter makes it safe.

A sober friend lowers some risks. It doesn't fix an unknown dose, an unknown product, or a family history of psychosis.

MYTH The research proves it works.

The research says "promising, in small groups of screened adults, with therapy." That's a reason for more studies, not for trying it at 16.

For advisory or classHeadline vs. reality.

About 15 minutes. Read each headline, vote (whole story, half the story, or wrong), then open the research.

"Magic mushrooms cure depression."

Half the story. Early trials in adults are promising. "Cure" goes past the evidence, and none of it applies to unscreened use.

"Microdosing makes you calmer and more creative."

Mostly wrong. In a blinded study, real microdoses and empty capsules worked about the same.

"Psychedelics aren't addictive, so they're harmless."

Half the story. Low addiction risk, real psychological risk.

  • Why does a "breakthrough" headline spread faster than a careful one?
  • What would you need to know before trusting a study's result applies to you?
Tip for the person leading

Run it as media literacy. Keep it about headlines and research, not anyone's personal experience.

Get help right away if someone

  • Is trying to hurt themselves or someone else.
  • Has a seizure, trouble breathing, or chest pain.
  • Is so confused or terrified they could walk into danger.
  • Took a product and is getting very sick.

Call 911 or get event medical staff. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222, any time. If someone is in an emotional crisis but not in physical danger, call or text 988. See Get help below.
