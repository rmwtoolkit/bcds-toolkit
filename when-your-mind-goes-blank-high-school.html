When Your Mind Goes Blank

The knowledge is still stored.

What's going on

The amygdala reads the test as a threat. Stress hormones push the prefrontal cortex, your working memory, partly offline, and worry takes up the space that's left. That is the blank.

Part 1 · Understand it

What happens in the blank

Sound familiar?You knew it last night.
  • You opened the SAT math section, read the first question three times, and nothing landed.
  • You can do the problems at home. In class, with a timer on the board, your mind goes white.
  • Your heart is pounding at the start of the road test, and you forget which pedal is which for a second.
  • The answer shows up in the hallway, two minutes after you hand the test in.
  • Someone else flips their page and you lose your place in your own head.

You've probably heard that test anxiety is your alarm system going off at the wrong time. Here's what that alarm actually does to the parts of your brain you need for a test, and why some of the most effective fixes are counterintuitive.

The stress response, during a test

Amygdala: sounds the alarm

It reads the test, the timer, or the stakes as a threat. It reacts in a fraction of a second, before you've consciously decided anything.

Hypothalamus and pituitary: release the chemicals

Within seconds, your nervous system releases adrenaline: racing heart, fast breathing, sweaty hands. Over the next several minutes, a hormone chain ending at your adrenal glands adds cortisol.

Prefrontal cortex: goes partly offline

This is your working memory and reasoning. Neuroscientist Amy Arnsten's research shows that even mild stress you feel you can't control can quickly weaken it. That is the blank: the knowledge is still stored, but the part that pulls it up and works with it is struggling.

Hippocampus: harder to search

It helps store and retrieve memories. Stress hormones can make retrieval less reliable, which is why the answer often comes back once you've calmed down.

One more cost: worry itself takes up working memory. "I'm going to fail" runs in the background and uses space you need for the actual question.

The researchHow much it matters, and for whom.
It's real, and it's modest.
A 2018 review by Nathaniel von der Embse and colleagues pulled together 238 studies. Test anxiety was consistently linked to lower scores, with some of the strongest links on university entrance exams and state tests in high school. The effects are small to moderate: anxiety is one factor in a score, not a verdict.
Pressure feels personal, and sometimes it's shared.
Scholarships, college, a parent's sacrifice, being the first in your family to take the SAT. In some families a test result feels like it belongs to everyone. That can be motivating and heavy at the same time. It's worth naming what the stakes feel like for you.
Avoidance makes it worse.
When thinking about the test feels bad, it's tempting to study less. Then you're less prepared, which gives the alarm a better reason to go off.
The counterintuitive part"Calm down" is not the best advice.

Harvard researcher Alison Wood Brooks found that most people believe calming down is the best way to handle performance nerves. In her 2014 experiments, people who instead told themselves "I am excited" before singing, giving a speech, or taking a math test felt more excited and performed better than people told to calm down.

A 2010 study by Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues found something similar with the GRE, a graduate school entrance exam. Students told that a racing heart can help performance did better on the math section, in the lab and on their real exam months later.

Your body's arousal isn't the enemy. The story you tell about it changes what it does to you.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

A plan for before, during, and after

The test-day system

What to do, and when.

THE WEEK BEFORE
  • Study by testing yourself. In a 2016 study by Amy Smith and colleagues, people who learned by practice testing kept their memory under acute stress. People who learned by rereading lost more, especially after the stressor.
  • Practice in test conditions. Timed, no notes, same format. Familiar conditions give the alarm less to react to.
  • Protect sleep, most of all the last two nights.
10 MINUTES BEFORE
  • Write out the worry. In Gerardo Ramirez and Sian Beilock's 2011 study, high school students who wrote freely about their test worries for about ten minutes before a final scored higher than those who didn't. The benefit was concentrated in the most anxious students.
  • Relabel the feeling. "My body is getting ready." "I'm excited." Same heartbeat, different instructions.
DURING
  • Breathe out longer than in. In for 4, out for 6, two or three times.
  • Skip and return. Answering easier questions settles the alarm, and blanked answers often surface on the way back.
  • Unload early. If it's allowed, jot formulas or key facts on scratch paper at the start, before stress builds.
AFTER
  • Debrief, don't replay. What went well, what you'd change, then stop. Rehashing every question feeds the next round of dread.
Try itOne minute. Breathe with the circle.

A longer exhale activates the part of your nervous system that slows the heart. Use this before a test, or during one if you feel the alarm rising. Breathe in as the circle grows, out as it shrinks.

If your mind is still racing, ground yourself: name three things you can see, two you can hear, and one you can feel, like your feet on the floor.

Try itThe ten-minute worry write.

This is the method from the Ramirez and Beilock study. Write as openly as you can about what you're worried will happen on the test and why. Don't edit. Nothing here is saved.

Try itThree high-stakes moments. What's the best move?

Tariq is halfway through the SAT math section. A question he's sure he practiced looks completely unfamiliar, and his heart starts pounding.

Ana's road test is tomorrow. She's practiced for months, but she failed the written test once and keeps picturing the examiner writing her up.

Mei's family has sacrificed a lot for her schooling, and she feels the AP exam score belongs to them too. The night before, she can't stop studying and can't sleep.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths about test anxietyWhat the research says instead.
MYTH I have to be calm to do well.

The research on reappraisal says you can be keyed up and do well. Aim for "ready," not "calm."

MYTH If I'm anxious, I'm not prepared.

Anxiety tracks how much something matters to you, not how much you know.

MYTH More caffeine will sharpen me.

A normal amount is fine if you're used to it. Extra caffeine or energy drinks add to the racing heart you're trying to manage.

MYTH One test decides my future.

Most big tests can be retaken, and most decisions use many pieces of information. The thought feels true in the moment. It usually isn't.

When to ask for more help

  • Anxiety is hurting your scores even when you know the material well.
  • You feel sick, panicky, or can't sleep before most tests, or you avoid school on test days.
  • Worry about school is spreading into the rest of your life.

A counselor can help, and anxiety responds well to treatment. Some students qualify for testing accommodations, such as extended time or a separate room, through a 504 plan. The College Board and ACT also review accommodation requests, usually through your school. See Get help below.
