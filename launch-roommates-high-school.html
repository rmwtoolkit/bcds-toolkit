Living With a Stranger

The first sentence keeps a small problem small.

What's going on

Roommate conflict is one of the most common first-year problems, and one of the most preventable. Most people have a default conflict style, learned at home. Your roommate may have learned the opposite rules.

Fine on paper→Different unspoken rules→Something bugs you→You say nothing→It comes out sideways

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "If I say nothing, it'll pass."
  • "I don't want it awkward."
  • "They don't like me."

🏠 In the room

  • loud sighs, headphones on hard
  • lights on after 1 a.m.
  • your food and charger, borrowed

🔁 Behaviors

  • putting up with almost anything
  • venting in the group chat
  • leaving a passive-aggressive note

Where to step in

Start soft and specific.

  1. Use notice, effect, ask: "When the lights are on after 1, I can't sleep. Could we..."
  2. Bring it up early and small. Week one is a conversation. Week eight is a fight.
  3. Write a roommate agreement, check in every few weeks, and use your RA.

Tell an RA, call campus safety or 911, or call or text 988.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why roommate stuff goes sideways

Sound familiar?Fine on paper. Untested in real life.
  • You haven't shared a bedroom since you were little, or you've always shared one with siblings and have your own rules.
  • You and your future roommate have followed each other and texted a few times. You seem fine on paper.
  • You'd rather put up with almost anything than make it awkward.
  • You have strong feelings about sleep, noise, guests, or cleanliness that you've never had to say out loud.

Roommate conflict is one of the most common problems first-years run into, and one of the most preventable.

The researchYour roommate shapes more than your room.
Roommates rub off on each other.
Dartmouth assigns first-year roommates at random, which makes it a natural experiment. Students' grades moved with their roommates' grades, and roommates influenced whether each other joined fraternities (Sacerdote, 2001).
Habits travel, too.
At another school with random assignment, men who were placed with a roommate who drank often before college ended up with GPAs about a quarter point lower. Women's grades weren't affected (Kremer and Levy, 2008).
Relationship quality matters for well-being.
A review of the research ties the quality of your roommate relationship to how well you adjust to college and to your mental health (Erb and colleagues, 2014).
Different backgrounds take more work, and pay off.
In a study of randomly assigned first-years, white students living with a Black roommate reported less satisfaction early on. By the end of the term, their anxiety about interacting across race had dropped and their attitudes had improved (Shook and Fazio, 2008).

None of this means you need a best friend. It means the room is worth some effort.

Five ways people handle conflict

Competing

"Lights off at 11. That's it." Useful when something is non-negotiable, like safety. Used for everything, it turns the room into a standoff.

Collaborating

"You study late, I have 8 a.m.s. What if you use a desk lamp after midnight and I get a sleep mask?" Takes the most time. Works best for things you'll live with all year.

Compromising

"Guests until midnight on weeknights, later on weekends." Fast and fair enough. Fine for small stuff.

Avoiding

Earbuds in, say nothing. Fine for a one-time annoyance. For anything that repeats, it builds resentment until it comes out sideways.

Accommodating

"No, it's fine, have them over." Generous when it really doesn't matter to you. A problem when it does, and you're the one who always gives.

Where your style comes fromFamilies and cultures teach different rules.

In some families, you say what bothers you right away and it's over in five minutes. In others, raising a complaint directly is rude, and people hint, wait, or go through someone else. Some households share everything; in others, touching someone's stuff without asking is a serious line. Neither is wrong. Your roommate may have learned the opposite rules.

Think about it: how did your family handle disagreements? Which style did you learn, and which one do you want to use?

The big topics to cover early

Tap each one.

Sleep

Lights out, alarms, early classes, naps, phone calls in bed.

Guests

Friends on school nights, overnight guests, partners, and how much notice.

Clean and stuff

Dishes, trash, food, and what's shared versus off-limits.

Study and quiet

Quiet hours, music, calls home, where each of you works best.

Values

Alcohol or weed in the room, prayer or religious practice, what's private. You don't have to agree. You do have to know.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Having the conversation

The first sentence

How you start predicts how it ends.

In a study of 124 newlywed couples, how the first three minutes of a disagreement went predicted which couples would divorce over the next six years. Couples who opened with criticism of the person, instead of a specific complaint, did worse (Carrère and Gottman, 1999). Different relationship, same lesson: start soft and specific.

1. What I notice"When the lights are on after 1 a.m...."
2. How it affects me"...I can't fall asleep, and I have an 8 a.m. every day."
3. What I'm asking"Could we try lights out at 12:30 on weeknights, and you use a desk lamp?"

More tools. Tap to see how.

1 Write a roommate agreement.

It feels formal. It works because you settle things before anyone is annoyed. Most RAs will help in the first week.

2 Bring it up early and small.

A talk in week one is a conversation. A talk in week eight is a fight.

3 Ask, then listen.

"Is there anything I do that bugs you?" makes it two-way, and you'll learn things.

4 Check in again.

"How's the room working for you?" every few weeks keeps small things small.

5 Use your RA.

Mediating roommate problems is part of their job. Asking isn't snitching.

Story 1 of 2Rosa and Hannah

Rosa calls home every night on speaker, usually around 10, often with her grandmother and little brothers on the line. It's how her family stays close, and it's in Spanish, which Hannah doesn't speak. Hannah grew up as an only child in a quiet house and goes to bed at 10:30. She hasn't said anything, but she's started sighing loudly and putting her headphones on hard. Rosa has noticed and thinks Hannah doesn't like her family, or doesn't like her.

  1. What style is each of them using right now?
  2. What might each of them be assuming?
  3. What would a collaborating version sound like?
Moves that help

Hannah can name the specific thing without judging the calls: "I love that your family is so close. The volume at 10 is hard for me because I'm trying to sleep by 10:30. Could we figure something out?" Options: earbuds, an earlier call, the lounge on some nights. Rosa can also open it herself: "Are my calls bothering you? I'd rather know."

Story 2 of 2Kwame and Luke

For the first month, Kwame's main complaint about Luke was the mess. Lately it's different. Luke has barely left the room in five days, isn't showering, and has stopped going to class. Kwame overheard him on the phone saying he "doesn't see the point." They aren't close. Telling the RA feels like going behind Luke's back, but Kwame keeps thinking about that call and wondering if he's the only one who heard it.

  1. Is this still a roommate problem?
  2. What would you say to Luke?
  3. Who should Kwame tell, and when?
Moves that help

This is a safety situation, not a roommate problem. Kwame can check in directly ("You've seemed really down. I'm worried about you. Are you thinking about hurting yourself?") and tell the RA today. If Luke says anything about not wanting to live, Kwame calls 988 or campus safety right away. Telling isn't betrayal. It might be what helps.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Say Hannah's first sentence out loud. Then try Rosa's.
  • When does a roommate problem stop being about the room and start being about safety?
  • Which conflict style did your family teach you? When does it help, and when does it get in the way?
Tip for the person leading

In Rosa and Hannah's story, steer away from deciding who's right. Both habits are reasonable. For Kwame's story, remind students that telling an RA about a roommate who "doesn't see the point" is the right call, and that they can always call home too.

Harder than the dishesValues, identity, and where the line is.
Different values, same room.
One of you drinks and one doesn't. One prays five times a day, one has never been religious. You don't have to share beliefs. You do need an agreement about what happens in the shared space.
Identity isn't a preference to negotiate.
A roommate's race, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, or disability isn't a "conflict." Slurs, mocking, or treating someone as less than isn't a style difference. Tell the RA or use your school's bias reporting system.
Room changes exist.
If you've tried in good faith and it's still not livable, or if you ever feel unsafe, ask about a room change. It isn't failing.
What doesn't helpThese usually make it worse.
MYTH If I say nothing, it'll pass.

For something that repeats, avoiding just stores it up. It usually comes out later, bigger.

MYTH Venting in the group chat is harmless.

Roommates find out. Then you have the original problem plus a trust problem.

MYTH A note avoids the awkward talk.

Passive-aggressive notes read as criticism with no tone of voice. Talk instead.

MYTH Being right settles it.

The goal is a room that works for both of you, not a verdict.

Get help right away if

  • A roommate talks about not wanting to live, or not seeing the point.
  • You feel unsafe in your room.
  • A roommate is very drunk or high and can't be woken, or is breathing strangely.

Tell an RA, call campus safety or 911, or call or text 988. See Get help below.
