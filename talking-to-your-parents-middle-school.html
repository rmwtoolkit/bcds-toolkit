Talking to Your Parents

Talking can actually earn you more freedom.

What's going on

You want freedom. They want you safe. Both can be true at the same time. Talks go sideways from bad timing, big feelings on both sides, or different goals. Talking with them is a skill.

You ask for something→Bad timing, big feelings→A no before you finish→You say nothing→More worry, more no

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "They'll just say no."
  • "They don't get it."
  • "It's easier to say nothing."

🏠 Situations

  • asking as they walk in
  • advice when you wanted listening
  • a hard thing you can't start

🔁 Behaviors

  • yelling or slamming doors
  • shutting down completely
  • "Everyone else gets to!"

The loop

Talk lessSaying nothing seems easier
WorryThey don't know what's going on
More noFewer yeses, more rules
Big feelingsYelling, slamming doors
Shut downSilence, hiding things
Less trustSecrets come out later

and around it goes

Where to step in

Right moment, clear ask, calm voice.

  1. Pick your moment. Ask, "Is now a good time to talk about something?"
  2. Say what you want: advice, help, permission, or just someone to listen.
  3. Offer a plan: "Can I go if I text you when I get there?"

Don't feel safe at home? Tell a counselor or trusted adult at school. If you're in danger right now, call 911, or call or text 988.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why talking with parents can be hard

Sound familiar?You tried to explain. It turned into a fight.
  • You tried to explain something and it turned into an argument.
  • You asked for something (a sleepover, a phone, more freedom) and got a no before you finished talking.
  • You wanted to tell them something hard but didn't know how to start.
  • They gave advice when you just wanted them to listen.
  • You felt like they don't get what being a kid is like now, especially online.
  • You said nothing at all because it seemed easier.

In this module, "parents" means whoever is raising you: one parent or two, two moms or two dads, a grandparent, a foster parent, a guardian, or an aunt or older sibling. Every kind of family counts. Talking with them is a skill, and skills get better with practice.

Why conversations go sideways

Tap each one.

⏰Bad timing

Asking right when they walk in the door, are driving, or are stressed almost never goes well.

🔥Big feelings on both sides

When you're upset, it's hard to explain. When they're worried, it's hard for them to listen.

🧩Different goals

You want freedom or understanding. They want you safe. Both can be true at the same time.

Why it's harder at your ageYou want freedom. They want you safe.
You're growing more independent.
That's healthy and normal. It also means more moments where you and your parents see things differently.
Your brain and theirs are wired differently right now.
Teen brains are tuned toward friends and new experiences. Parent brains are often tuned toward worry.
Their world was different.
Group chats, Snap Map, and social media weren't part of growing up for most parents. That can make them more worried, or just confused.
Every family has its own rules for talking.
In some homes kids are expected to speak up and debate. In others, respect means listening first. Some families switch between languages, and you might be the one who translates. Notice how yours works, and use the tips that fit.
Most parents want to understand.
Even when it doesn't look like it, most parents want to know what's going on with you. They just don't always know how to ask.
💬 They're learning tooTalking more can earn you more freedom.

Most parents are figuring out how to parent a kid your age for the first time, even if you have older siblings. They'll get it wrong sometimes, and so will you. That's how families learn.

The worst part: the less you talk, the more they worry, and the more they worry, the more they say no. Talking can actually earn you more freedom.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

Right moment, clear ask, calm voice.

Tap one to see how.

🕰 Pick your moment.

Car rides, walks, or cooking together often work better than sitting face to face. Ask, "Is now a good time to talk about something?"

🎯 Know what you want.

Before you start, decide: do you want advice, help, permission, or just someone to listen? Then say it. "I don't need you to fix it, I just want to vent."

🧊 Stay calm(ish).

If you feel yourself getting heated, say, "Can we take a break and come back to this?" Then actually come back.

👂 Show you heard them.

"I get that you're worried about..." goes a long way, even if you disagree.

🤝 Offer a plan.

Instead of "Can I go?" try "Can I go if I text you when I get there and I'm home by 10?" Plans show you've thought it through.

✉️ Write it down if talking is hard.

A text or note can help you say something hard without getting interrupted or flustered.

Try itPlan it before you start it.

Think of something you want to talk to a parent about. Fill this in to get ready. Nothing is saved.

Try itWhich line actually gets heard?

Pick the line most likely to be heard.

You want to go to a friend's house after school, but your parent doesn't know the family.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually lead to a no.
📢 Yelling or slamming doors.

It's a sign the feeling is huge, but it makes parents focus on the yelling instead of what you need.

🙊 Shutting down completely.

Silence often makes parents worry more and push harder.

🎭 "Everyone else" arguments.

Parents tend to care about you, not everyone else. Talk about your situation.

🕳 Hiding things.

Secrets that come out later damage trust, and trust is what earns freedom.

When to talk to another adult

  • You don't feel safe talking to your parents, or at home.
  • Conversations at home often end in threats or someone getting hurt.
  • You need help with something you can't bring to them yet.
  • You want to share something big, like who you like or your gender, and you're not sure how they'll react.

A counselor can listen and help you figure out next steps. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Kids and parents have been trying to understand each other forever. These stories show it can work.

📖 Read

Where the Wild Things Are · Maurice Sendak, 1963 Throwback

Max yells, gets sent to his room, and sails off to be king of the wild things. When he comes back, his supper is waiting, still hot. Take a break, then come back.

Front Desk · Kelly Yang, 2018

Mia and her parents run a motel after moving from China. Mia solves problems by writing letters, and her writing gets more powerful as the book goes on. Writing it down works.

🎬 Watch

Turning Red · Pixar, 2022

Mei has always been the perfect daughter, and her mom wants it to stay that way. Mei finally says what she wants out loud. Rated PG.

Brave · Pixar, 2012

Merida and her mom both want to be heard and neither is listening. It takes a lot of trouble for them to start thinking like each other. Rated PG.

🎧 Listen

Speechless · Alan Menken, Benj Pasek and Justin Paul, sung by Naomi Scott, 2019

Jasmine's song from the live-action Aladdin, about refusing to stay quiet. For when saying nothing seems easier.

🖼️ Look

Pashmina · Nidhi Chanani, 2017

Priyanka wants to know about India and about her father, and her mom won't talk about either. A magic shawl shows her some of it. The rest she has to ask. A graphic novel.

✏️ Make your own

Use "Plan it before you start it" to write a note to a parent or the adult who raises you. Say what you want to talk about and what you want from them: advice, permission, help, or just listening. You decide if you give it to them.
