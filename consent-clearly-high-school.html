Consent, Clearly

Four checks. All of them, every time.

What's going on

A yes counts only when it passes four checks: capacity, freely given, specific, and ongoing. A no at any step means stop. Silence and stillness can be fear, not agreement.

Nobody asks out loud→Signals get guessed at→Hesitation gets pushed past→Quiet gets read as yes→"They didn't say no"

How you'll know

💭 Myths

  • "They didn't say no."
  • "We're together, so it's fine."
  • "Asking kills the mood."

👀 Signals to stop

  • "Um," "I guess," a pause
  • going quiet and still
  • turning away

⚠️ Situations

  • someone too drunk to decide
  • a yes after asking five times
  • a private photo passed around

Where to step in

Ask in ways that make no easy.

  1. Ask questions that are easy to say no to: "Is this okay?" "Want to slow down?"
  2. Treat hesitation as a no. Check in when anything changes: "Still good?"
  3. If you see it going wrong, step in: direct, distract, or delegate.

If something happened, it is not your fault. RAINN, free and confidential, 24/7: 1-800-656-4673. In danger, call 911.

Couples still ask. Being together means asking gets easier. It never becomes optional.

Part 1 · Understand it

Consent, past the definition

Sound familiar?You know the definition. Real moments are messier.
  • You've been in a moment where you couldn't tell if the other person was into it, or just not saying no.
  • Someone at a party is clearly drunk, and the person they're with doesn't seem to care.
  • A friend told you about a night that didn't sit right with them, and they don't know what to call it.
  • You said yes to one thing and got treated like you'd agreed to everything.
  • A photo you sent to one person ended up in front of people you never meant to see it.

You know the basics already: ask, and respect the answer. This is the next layer. What a yes needs in order to count, why people freeze instead of saying no, and why reading signals is less reliable than most people assume.

Four checks. All of them, every time.

Capacity: can they actually decide?

Asleep, passed out, or too drunk or high to follow what's happening means no capacity, whatever was said earlier. Age counts too. In Massachusetts, a person under 16 can't legally consent to sex, and the law has no exception for partners who are close in age.

Freely given: could they say no at no cost?

A yes that comes after asking five times, a guilt trip, a threat to break up, or pressure from someone with power over you (older, more popular, holding your photos) isn't free.

Specific: a yes to what?

Yes to kissing isn't yes to more. Yes last weekend isn't yes tonight. Sending a photo to one person isn't permission to show anyone else.

Ongoing: still yes?

Anyone can stop at any point, including partway through and including in a long relationship. Being someone's partner never means automatic yes.

The researchWhy "they didn't say no" proves nothing.
Freezing is a reflex, not a choice.
In a 2017 Swedish study of 298 women seen at an emergency clinic after a sexual assault, 70% said they experienced significant involuntary paralysis during the assault, and 48% extreme paralysis. Researchers call it tonic immobility. The study included only women, but the reflex is human. Silence and stillness can be fear, not agreement.
Signals get misread, in both directions.
In a 2012 speed-dating study, men on average overestimated how sexually interested women were in them, and women tended to underestimate men's interest. If trained adults misread three minutes of conversation, guessing in a dark room goes worse. Asking fixes it.
Alcohol is involved more often than not.
A review of research on college students found that at least half of sexual assaults involve alcohol, drunk by the person who did it, the person it happened to, or both. Alcohol slows thinking and makes stop signals easier to miss. It never moves responsibility onto the person who was drinking.
This happens to people your age.
In the CDC's 2021 national survey of high school students, 14% of girls said they had been forced to have sex at some point, the first increase in ten years. Boys and nonbinary students are assaulted too, and are often less likely to tell anyone.
Images and screensA photo sent to one person is not permission to share it.

A 2018 review of 39 studies with more than 110,000 teens found about 15% had sent a sext and 27% had received one. About 12% had forwarded one without permission, and 8% had their own forwarded without their consent.

Massachusetts law changed in 2024. Sharing someone's explicit images without consent is now a crime, including fake images made with AI. For people under 18, sexting can still fall under child exploitation laws, but the new law lets teens be sent to an education program instead of being charged in most cases.

If an image of you is out there: you didn't cause this by trusting someone. If it was taken when you were under 18, Take It Down (takeitdown.ncmec.org) can help get it removed without you sending the image to anyone. A counselor can help with the rest.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

What to say, and what to do

Asking well

Asking is a skill. It gets smoother with practice.

Tap each one.

1 Ask questions that are easy to say no to.

"Is this okay?" "Want to slow down?" "What do you want to do?" When no is easy to say, you get a more honest answer.

2 Treat hesitation as a no.

"Um," "I guess," a pause, going still, turning away. Stop and ask. Don't push through it.

3 Check in when anything changes.

A new place, a new activity, more drinking, a long pause. "Still good?" takes two seconds.

4 Take a no well.

"Okay." Then actually change course. No sulking, no asking again ten minutes later. How you handle a no decides whether people feel safe telling you the truth.

5 Say no your way.

"I don't want to." "Not tonight." "Stop." No reason required. If the words won't come, an exit works too: "I need the bathroom," or a code text to a friend who'll call you.

Try itFour situations. Does the yes count?

Run each one through the four checks.

Dev and Aaliyah are making out at his place. Aaliyah has gone quiet and still. She hasn't said no.

At a party, Jules has had a lot to drink and is slurring. Someone Jules met an hour ago asks if they want to go upstairs, and Jules says yes.

Kenji is an 18-year-old senior. Ana, a 15-year-old sophomore, is into him, and both want to have sex.

Mateo's girlfriend sent him a private photo months ago. After they break up, she asks him to delete it.

Try itYou're the one who sees it. What's your move?

Bystanders change outcomes. In a trial across 26 Kentucky high schools, schools that trained students to step in (direct, distract, or delegate) had less sexual violence in the years that followed than schools that didn't.

At a party, an older guy keeps refilling Maya's cup and steering her toward a back room. She looks unsteady. You barely know her.

Someone in a group chat posts a classmate's private photo.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths that blur the lineWhat people say, and what's true.
MYTH If they didn't fight back, they wanted it.

Freezing is one of the most common reactions to a threat. Not resisting tells you nothing about consent.

MYTH Couples don't need to ask.

Being together means you know each other better, so asking gets easier. It doesn't become optional.

MYTH If they were drinking, it's partly on them.

Responsibility belongs to the person who went ahead without consent. Drinking is never an invitation.

MYTH Guys always want it.

Anyone can be pressured, and anyone can be assaulted. Boys can say no, and that no counts the same.

MYTH Asking kills the mood.

Pressure kills the mood. A quick question tells the other person you care what they want.

If something happened

  • It is not your fault, whatever you drank, wore, said, or did before.
  • You get to decide what to call it and what to do next.
  • Medical care helps even if you never report, and going sooner keeps more options open.
  • Who you tell first can depend on your family and culture: a friend, a sibling, an aunt, a coach, a faith leader, a counselor. Any start counts.

RAINN is free and confidential, 24/7: call 1-800-656-4673. School counselors can help, and you can ask them up front what they'd have to share and with whom.

If you're in danger, call 911. If you're overwhelmed, call or text 988. See Get help below.
