Why it’s so hard to start

When everything is due, it all looks equally urgent. Your brain stalls on “which one first?” and grabs the nearest exit.

Everything is due
↓
You freeze
↓
Phone, bed, “I just want to go home”
↓
It piles up
↓
“What’s wrong with me?”
↺ and around again

The way out is to make the first step so small it feels almost silly. Doing comes first. Motivation usually shows up a few minutes in.

When you just want to go home

At school, a sleepover, a trip, even a college visit: sometimes everything in you says, “I need to go home.” Home is where you feel safe, so of course you want to be there.

The tricky part: every time you leave early, the next time gets a little harder. Staying a little longer than you want to teaches your body that you can handle it.

Call to get picked up→Wait ten minutes first
Stay home from school→Go for the first two classes
Text home all day→One check-in at lunch

A counselor can help you plan these, one small step at a time.

When you lock up

Under stress, most of us get rigid. There’s one right way to do it, and if it can’t be done that way, it doesn’t get done at all.

Try asking: what’s the good-enough version?

Finish the whole essay→Write one messy paragraph
Clean my whole room→Clear my bed
Catch up on everything→The one that’s due first
The perfect study plan→Twenty-five minutes on one subject

Done and good enough beats perfect and waiting.

When home is stressed too

Sometimes the grown-ups at home are overwhelmed too. Maybe they’re short-tempered, or they’ve gone quiet.

Fixing that isn’t your job. You can still help with one small thing, the way anyone in a family does, and leave the grown-up stuff to the grown-ups.

If it feels like too much to carry, talk with a counselor or another adult you trust.

Grown-ups who can help

Some things you can ask for:

  • A parent: “Can you sit with me while I start?” Working next to someone makes starting easier for a lot of people.
  • A parent: “Can you help me make the list, not do it for me?”
  • A teacher: “I’m behind. Can we figure out what matters most?”
  • Your advisor: “Can you check in with me Friday about how it went?”
  • Learning support, if you have a learning plan or think you might need one.
Five things, any room

A trick from KC Davis, a therapist who writes about ADHD and keeping a space livable. Every messy room has only five kinds of things in it:

🗑Trash
🍽Dishes
🧺Laundry
📦Has a spot
❓No spot yet

Pick one kind and do only that. A messy room says nothing about what kind of person you are.

Your family, your culture

Every family has its own rules about chores, school and what “a clean room” means. Some families expect a lot of help at home. Some students care for siblings or work after school. Some split time between two homes.

What does your family expect? What do you wish they knew about how hard things feel right now?

Other ways in
  • Watch: Inside Out 2 (Pixar, 2024). A teenager’s Anxiety takes over the control panel and tries to plan everything at once.
  • Read: Turtles All the Way Down, John Green. A teen whose thoughts spiral, and the friends who stick around. Heads up: anxiety and intrusive thoughts.
  • Listen: “Three Little Birds,” Bob Marley. Every little thing.
  • Look: Kintsugi, the Japanese art of mending broken pottery with gold, so the repair becomes part of the beauty.
  • Make your own: take a photo of your clear desk or bed, and keep it on your phone.
