Snapshot · Middle School · Substance use

Addiction at Home

Didn't cause it. Can't cure it. Can't control it.

What's going on

Addiction is a health condition that changes how a brain handles cravings and choices. Nothing you did made it start, and nothing you do can make it stop. Unpredictability keeps your brain on alert.

Never knowing what you'll find→Your brain stays on alert→Covering and making excuses→Taking care of everyone→Don't talk, trust, or feel

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "Is it my fault?"
  • "My house is boring."
  • "Don't tell anyone."

🫀 Feelings

  • angry and protective at once
  • hard to sleep or focus
  • embarrassed, guilty, numb

🔁 Behaviors

  • checking moods before inviting friends
  • covering for a parent
  • caring for younger siblings

Where to step in

Tell one safe adult.

  1. Tell one safe adult. "Things are hard at home" is a fine start.
  2. Have a safety plan: who to call, where to go, and how to get there.
  3. Find people who get it: Alateen is a free group for kids in your spot.

A parent can't be woken up or has trouble breathing? Call 911 for an ambulance. The Childhelp hotline is 1-800-422-4453, any time.

Part 1 · Understand it

Living with someone else's addiction

Sound familiar?If this sounds like home, it's not your fault.
  • A parent acts like a different person after drinking.
  • You check what kind of mood someone's in before you decide whether to bring friends over.
  • You've made excuses for a parent, or covered for them.
  • Promises get broken: pickups missed, plans forgotten, apologies that don't stick.
  • You take care of younger siblings more than your friends do.
  • Home feels unpredictable, and school is where you can finally breathe. Or the other way around.

It might be a mom, a dad, a grandparent, a stepparent, an older sibling, or whoever raises you. If any of that sounds familiar, you are far from alone. And none of it is your fault.

You're not the only oneAbout 1 in 4 kids lives with this.
The three CsDidn't cause it. Can't cure it. Can't control it.
Why it's so confusingLove and anger can both be true.
🎭 Two versions of the same person.
The parent you love and the parent when they're using can feel like different people. Loving one and being angry at the other can both be true.
🤫 The unspoken rules.
Lots of families have rules nobody says out loud: don't talk about it, don't trust outsiders, don't feel too much. Some families and cultures also care a lot about keeping private things private. You can respect your family and still tell one safe adult.
🌀 Never knowing what you'll walk into.
Unpredictability keeps your brain on alert. That's why it can be hard to focus at school, sleep, or relax, even on good days.
😶 Mixed-up feelings.
Angry, sad, embarrassed, guilty, protective, numb, hopeful. Sometimes all in one evening. Every one of them makes sense.
Part 2 · Practice the skills

Taking care of you

What actually helps

Tell one safe adult.

Tap one to see how.

🗣 Tell one safe adult.

A counselor, a teacher, a coach, a relative, a faith leader. You don't have to tell the whole story at once. "Things are hard at home" is a fine start.

🧭 Have a safety plan.

Know who you can call, where you can go, and how to get there if home feels unsafe.

🛟 Find people who get it.

Alateen is a free group just for kids who have someone in their life who drinks too much.

📓 Get the feelings out.

Write, draw, play music, run. Feelings that stay stuck inside tend to come out sideways.

🎒 Protect your own stuff.

Sleep, friends, a team, a club, a place that's yours. Those are worth guarding, and you deserve them.

💛 Be a kid when you can.

You might be carrying a lot at home. You still get to have fun, be goofy, and not be in charge sometimes.

Try itWho's in your corner?

Think of the people you could go to. Write a name next to any that fit.

👥 In a groupHow could a friend help Jamie?

👥 For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read the story out loud. It's about a friend, Jamie, not anyone in the room.
  2. In small groups, talk about the three questions on the next screens.
  3. Each group shares one idea. Collect them on the board.
👥 What would help Jamie? · 1 of 3Meet Jamie

Jamie's mom drinks most nights. Jamie hasn't had a friend over in months and says, "My house is boring." Lately Jamie seems tired and keeps missing practice.

  1. What might Jamie be feeling?
  2. What might Jamie be afraid of?
Ideas to add

Embarrassed, worried, tired, angry, protective of their parent, scared of what happens if anyone finds out. All of those make sense.

👥 What would help Jamie? · 2 of 3What could a friend say?

If Jamie finally tells you what's going on, what would actually help?

  1. What would you say?
  2. What would you avoid saying?
Ideas to add

"Thanks for telling me." "That sounds really hard." "It's not your fault." "Want to go talk to a counselor together?" Skip "Just tell your mom to stop" or telling other people.

👥 What would help Jamie? · 3 of 3When does a friend tell an adult?

Jamie asks you to keep it secret. When should you tell an adult anyway?

  1. Never, a secret is a secret
  2. If Jamie seems unsafe or is hurting
  3. Only if Jamie says it's okay
Ideas to add

If Jamie seems unsafe, is being hurt, or is talking about hurting themselves, telling a trusted adult is the caring move, even if Jamie is upset at first. See When a Friend Tells You Something Scary.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • What makes it hard for kids like Jamie to tell anyone?
  • What could our school do so kids like Jamie know where to go?
  • What's one thing you'd want Jamie to know?
Tip for the person leading

Assume some students in the room are living with this. Keep it about Jamie, never ask anyone to share their own family, and let students know before you start that a counselor is available afterward. If a student discloses, thank them privately and follow your school's procedures.

Try itReal moments. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

Your dad was supposed to pick you up from practice, and he's an hour late again. When he shows up, you can smell alcohol.

Your friend asks why you never invite anyone over.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Get help right away if

  • Someone at home hurts you, threatens you, or you feel unsafe.
  • A parent can't be woken up, has trouble breathing, or may have overdosed. Call 911 for an ambulance.
  • You don't have food, heat, or someone sober to take care of you.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Call 911 in an emergency. If you're not sure it's an emergency, 988 can help and can send a crisis team instead of police. The Childhelp hotline is 1-800-422-4453, any time. Call or text 988 if you're struggling. See Get help below.

Other ways in

You are far from the only kid living with this. Some of these stories were made for kids just like you.

📖 Read

Because of Winn-Dixie · Kate DiCamillo, 2000 Throwback

Opal finds out her mom left because she couldn't stop drinking. Then she meets Gloria Dump, who hangs old bottles on a tree to remember the things she did wrong when she drank. Gloria is in recovery, and she becomes one of Opal's people.

Heads up: Talks about a parent's drinking and a parent who left.

🎬 Watch

Encanto · Disney, 2021

The Madrigals have a rule nobody says out loud: we don't talk about Bruno. Lots of families have unspoken rules. Watch what changes when Mirabel starts talking. Rated PG.

Sesame Street: Parental Addiction · Sesame Workshop, 2019 Throwback

Karli's mom has an addiction. Karli tells Elmo she used to think it was her fault. It wasn't. Made for younger kids, and still worth a few minutes.

Heads up: About a parent's addiction, told gently.

🎧 Listen

What Do You Do with the Mad That You Feel? · Fred Rogers, 1968 Throwback

Mister Rogers wrote it after a boy asked him what to do with angry feelings. The lesson says feelings that stay stuck come out sideways. This song is about letting them out in a way that doesn't hurt anyone.

Lean on Me · Bill Withers, 1972

A song about letting someone help carry it. That's what telling one safe adult is.

🖼️ Look

Sunny Side Up · Jennifer L. Holm and Matthew Holm, 2015

Sunny gets sent to her grandpa's in Florida for the summer. In flashbacks we learn why: her older brother's drinking and drug use, and a family that won't talk about it. Her grandpa finally does.

Heads up: Shows an older brother's drug and alcohol use.

✏️ Make your own

Draw your corner from the Try it. Put yourself in the middle and draw the people around you: someone at school, someone in your family or a friend's family, and a place you could go fast. Keep it somewhere private.

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
  • LGBTQ+? The Trevor Project: call 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678-678.
  • Tell a counselor, teacher, coach, parent or caregiver, or another adult you trust today. You can show them this screen.
  • If you might act on thoughts of hurting yourself, call 911 or go to an emergency room. 988 can also send a crisis team to you.
