What Makes You Happy

Want it. Get it. Get used to it.

What's going on

Your brain is a surprisingly bad guesser about what will make you happy. A study that followed people for 85 years found it wasn't money or fame. It was close relationships.

Part 1 · Understand it

What actually lasts

Sound familiar?Got the thing you wanted? Felt great. For a week.
  • You finally got the thing you wanted, a new phone or game or shoes, and it felt amazing for about a week.
  • You've thought "I'll be happy when I'm in that friend group" or "when I'm older."
  • Your feed makes it look like everyone else has figured out how to be happy.
  • You have lots of followers or group chats, and still not many people you'd really call.
  • You're on the edge of a big friend group and figure you'd be happier in the middle of it.
  • You felt better after one real conversation than after hours online.

Your brain is a surprisingly bad guesser about what will make you happy. That's true for adults too. The good news is that there's real research on what actually works.

Want it. Get it. Get used to it.

Tap each one.

🎯Want it

"I'll be happy when..." Your brain locks onto the next thing: the item, the grade, the group, the follower count.

🎉Get it

And it works! You get a real lift. For a while.

😐Get used to it

The lift fades, and your brain is already pointing at the next "when." Psychologists call this getting used to things, or adaptation.

The longest study of happinessResearchers followed people for 85 years.
It started in 1938.
Harvard researchers began following 268 college students. A second group joined: 456 boys from Boston neighborhoods, many of them teenagers.
They never stopped.
For more than 85 years: questionnaires, medical records, brain scans, and interviews in people's living rooms. Today the study includes their families too.
Every kind of life.
Rich and poor, famous and unknown, through wars, marriages, divorces, and old age.
An honest note.
The original groups were all men, and mostly white. Later waves added wives and children, and other research points the same way. Still, it's worth knowing.
🤝 The big findingIt wasn't money or fame. It was people.

The researchers looked at people in middle age to see what predicted who would be healthy and happy at 80. It wasn't their cholesterol. It was how satisfied they were in their relationships.

"The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80."
Robert Waldinger, the study's director

Close relationships, more than money or fame, kept people happy across their lives.

The good part: you don't have to wait until you're 50. Relationships are something you can work on now.

Why our guesses go wrongYour brain is a bad happiness predictor.
🔄 We get used to things.
The new phone feeling fades in weeks. So does the new shoes feeling.
📏 We compare.
A good grade feels great until you hear someone got a better one.
🔮 We overestimate.
We think the good feeling will be bigger and last longer than it actually does.
🕰 We put people off.
Friends will be there later. Homework, games, and the feed feel urgent now.
Connection protects your body and brainGood friends are good for your health.
🚬 Loneliness is hard on the body.
Waldinger compares the health risk of loneliness to smoking about half a pack of cigarettes a day.
🧠 Connection protects the brain.
People who could count on someone kept sharper memories longer.
⚡ Arguing is allowed.
People who bickered still did fine, as long as they felt they could rely on each other.
👥 Quality beats count.
You don't need a crowd. You need people you can count on: family, friends, chosen family, a coach or mentor, whoever they are for you.
✨ Something bigger than yourselfCare about something beyond you. It helps.

The people in the study who did best often found ways to care about something beyond themselves, like teaching, helping younger people, or building something that would last.

For you, that could look like:

  • Helping a younger sibling or neighbor with something you're good at.
  • Being part of a team, a band, a club, or a cause.
  • Faith, nature, music, art, or your family's culture and traditions.
  • Volunteering, even for an hour.

Moments of awe count too: a huge night sky, a mountain, a loud concert, a choir. Research on awe finds it quiets the "me" part of your brain for a moment, and most people feel better for it.

📱 Likes are a quick liftLikes are a quick lift. Then they fade.

Apps run on the same loop: want it, get it, get used to it. Online it can look like:

  • Checking how many likes a post got, then checking again.
  • Seeing everyone's best moments and deciding they're happier than you.
  • Having hundreds of followers and still nobody to sit with at lunch.
  • Keeping streaks going with people you barely talk to.
  • Feeling worse after scrolling than before you started.

The worst part: the feed counts likes and followers. The study counted the people who would show up for you.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Building a good life

What actually helps

Small reps with people, every week.

Tap one to see how.

💪 Build social fitness.

Waldinger calls it social fitness. Friendships work like muscles: a few real conversations a week keep them strong. Skip a month and they drift.

💭 Ask yourself: who do I miss?

Then reach out. One short text is enough. Most of the time, the other person is glad you did.

🔁 See the same faces every week.

Pick a club, team, or lunch table and keep showing up. Seeing the same people again and again is how strangers turn into friends.

🧍 Find at least one solid person.

You don't need to be in the middle of the big group. You need at least one person you can count on when things fall apart. If you'd rather have one close friend than a big group, that's not a problem to fix.

🧭 Do something that points beyond you.

Help a younger kid, teach someone a skill, make something, or pitch in at home or in your neighborhood.

🛑 Catch the "I'll be happy when..."

When you hear yourself think it, treat it as a signal. Your goals are worth chasing. Just don't put your happiness on hold until you reach them.

Try itWho do you miss? One text is enough.

Think of one person you miss: an old friend, a cousin, a grandparent far away, someone from camp or your old school. Nothing here is saved.

Short is fine. The reply is often warmer than you'd guess.

Try itHappy when...? Try a better question.

Every "I'll be happy when..." thought has a better question hiding behind it.

"I'll be happy when I'm in that friend group."
Who already likes hanging out with me?
"I'll be happy when I get a phone like theirs."
What could I enjoy today with what I already have?
"I'll be happy when I'm older and have more freedom."
What can I enjoy now?
Try itYou don't need a crowd. You need one solid person.

Think of one person. Check anything that's true about them.

If no name came to mind, that's important information, not a failure. A counselor is a good person to talk with about it.

Try itLikes, lunch, or a free Saturday. What's your move?

Pick the move that builds something that lasts.

Your post got way fewer likes than your friend's. You feel kind of crummy.

At lunch you sit on the edge of a big friend group. There's one person in it you really click with.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese keep you on the treadmill.
🏃 Chasing the next thing.

The next item, grade, or follower count will feel good for a while. Then it fades, and there's another one.

📊 Measuring your life against feeds.

You're comparing your everyday to everyone else's best moments.

⏸ Putting happiness on hold.

"After this test, after this season, after I'm older." There's always another "after."

🧱 Going it alone.

Handling everything yourself feels strong. It's also lonely, and loneliness is hard on your body and mind.

What to do about social media right nowFollowers aren't friends.
💬 Turn one like into a real message.
Reply to someone's story with an actual question, or text them about something you both care about.
📵 Trade one scroll for one person.
Next time you reach for your phone out of boredom, text someone you miss instead.
🙈 Stop counting.
Some apps let you hide like counts. Followers aren't friends, and the number isn't you.
🧘 Notice the after-feeling.
If an app leaves you feeling worse most times, that's information. Try a set time to check instead of all day.

If it gets heavy

  • Loneliness or low mood has been around most days for a while.
  • You feel hopeless, or like nobody would notice if you weren't around.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be here.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult right away. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night.

Other ways in

Stories figured out what the 85-year study found a long time ago. A couple of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

Last Stop on Market Street · Matt de la Peña, illustrated by Christian Robinson, 2015 Throwback

CJ wants to know why they don't have a car. His Nana keeps showing him what's good right there on the bus. A picture book that won the Newbery Medal.

Because of Winn-Dixie · Kate DiCamillo, 2000

Opal is new in town and lonely. One person at a time, she builds a whole circle, and it ends with a party where everyone she's met shows up.

Heads up: Opal's mother left when she was three, and that sadness runs through the book.

🎬 Watch

Up · Pixar, 2009

Carl spent years saving for a big adventure. The adventure turns out to be the ordinary days he already had with Ellie. Rated PG for some peril and action.

Heads up: The opening shows Ellie getting sick and dying. It's sad, and gentle.

🎧 Listen

Lean on Me · Bill Withers, 1972 Throwback

Withers wrote it missing the people in the small coal town where he grew up. You don't need a crowd. You need someone to lean on.

🖼️ Look

Cumpleaños de Lala y Tudi · Carmen Lomas Garza, 1991

A backyard birthday party, a piñata, cousins and grandparents everywhere. Look at how many people are in it, and how few of them are looking at anything but each other.
