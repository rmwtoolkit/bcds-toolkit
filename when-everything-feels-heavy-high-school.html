Part 1 · Understand it

What depression is, and how it works

Sound familiar?Like someone turned the volume down on everything.
  • Your sport, your music, your friends: all of it feels muted.
  • You sleep ten hours and wake up tired, or you're awake at 3am for no reason.
  • You're snapping at people you actually like.
  • Work that used to take an hour now takes the whole night, if it happens at all.
  • You cancel plans, then feel lonely because you canceled.

Everyone has stretches like this. When they stack up for two weeks or more and get in the way of your life, it may be depression. It's common, it's nobody's fault, and it's one of the most treatable conditions there is.

You're far from the only one
40%of U.S. high school students in the CDC's 2023 survey felt so sad or hopeless, almost every day for two weeks or more, that they stopped some usual activities. Among girls it was 53%.

A separate federal survey found about 18% of 12- to 17-year-olds, nearly 1 in 5, had a full episode of major depression in the past year. Look around a classroom. You're not the only one carrying this.

What depression changes in the brain

Striatum: the fun gets turned down

In a study of teens with depression, this reward area responded less when they won money. The weaker the response, the less positive they felt in daily life, measured by check-ins on their phones. Doctors call this anhedonia.

Amygdala: tilts toward the negative

Many studies find it reacts more to sad or threatening faces and words during depression. Your attention gets pulled to what's wrong, which is part of why the bad stuff feels like the whole story.

Subgenual cingulate: a mood hub

A small area under the front of the brain's midline. It's often overactive in depression. It's also a target in experimental treatments for the most severe, hard-to-treat cases, with mixed results so far.

Hippocampus: memory and stress

A study pooling scans from nearly 9,000 people found it was slightly smaller, on average, in people with repeated episodes or depression that began by age 21. People in a first episode didn't differ. One more reason to get help early.

Prefrontal cortex: focus slows down

Planning, concentrating, and deciding all get harder. That's why grades often slip. It isn't laziness. The system you'd use to push through is one of the things affected.

A bad week, or depression?Sad comes and goes. Depression sticks.
Sadness
Usually a response to something real: a loss, a breakup, a rejection. It comes in waves, and in between you can still enjoy things.
Depression
Low mood or lost interest most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, plus other changes: sleep, appetite, energy, focus, guilt, or how you see yourself. It can show up with no obvious cause.
In teens it often looks like irritability.
Short temper, "I don't care," anger more than tears. Doctors count an irritable mood as a core sign of depression in people your age.
It can hide well.
Plenty of people with depression keep their grades up, joke at lunch, and post like everything's fine. Then they get home and crash. Looking okay and being okay aren't the same.
Why it happensNot weakness. Not a choice.

There's rarely one cause. Genes, long-running stress, loss, bullying, discrimination, loneliness, poor sleep, hormones, other health conditions, and substance use can all add to the load. Sometimes it shows up with no clear trigger.

Weed deserves a specific mention, since some people use it to cope. A review pooling 11 long-term studies found teens who used cannabis had about 37% higher odds of depression as young adults, even after accounting for earlier depression. That's a link, not proof of cause, and the individual risk is modest. It's still the opposite of a treatment.

The downward spiralWaiting to feel like it keeps you stuck.

Depression says, "Wait until you feel like it." So you skip practice, stop texting back, stay in your room. Fewer good moments and fewer people means a lower mood, which means doing even less.

The loop runs on its own. The way out runs the other direction: action first, mood later.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Climbing out, one small step at a time

Behavioral activation

Action first. Mood follows.

Therapists call it behavioral activation: you schedule small, doable things before you feel like doing them, and let the mood catch up. A review of 26 trials found a solid, moderate benefit compared with control groups.

MoveA 2024 review of 218 trials (mostly adults) found walking or jogging, yoga, and strength training eased depression, more so at higher effort. Ten minutes outside counts as a start.
Stay near peopleDepression pushes you to hide. Sit with people at lunch even if you're quiet. Answer one text.
Anchor your morningsSame wake time every day and daylight early. Sleep and mood move together. Running on Empty has more.
Count small winsWrite down one thing you did each day. Depression erases wins. A list keeps the record straight.

More tools. Tap to see how.

1 Shrink the task until it's almost silly.

Not "do the essay." Open the doc. Put on your shoes. Reply to one message. Then you're allowed to stop. Often you won't.

2 Tell one adult, imperfectly.

A counselor, a parent, a coach, a doctor, a faith leader. "I haven't felt like myself for a while" is enough. Families talk about mental health in different ways. If yours doesn't use words like "depression," you can start with sleep, energy, or stress.

3 Treat the story as a symptom.

"Nobody cares." "Nothing will change." "I'm a burden." These feel like facts. They're one of the most predictable symptoms of the illness. The Inner Critic has more on talking back.

4 Ask about treatment.

Therapy (especially CBT or behavioral activation), and for some people medicine, help most teens. It's normal to need a few tries to find the right fit.

Try itBuild a menu for the hard days.

Write one tiny thing for each line, while you're able to think clearly. On a stuck day, pick one and do it for five minutes. Nothing here is saved.

Five minutes counts. You can stop after. A lot of the time you won't want to.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials

  1. Read each statement out loud.
  2. Everyone votes: agree, disagree, or not sure.
  3. Open what the research says, then talk about it.

1 of 5. If you can keep your grades up, you can't be depressed.

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Disagree. Depression can hide behind a normal-looking day, including good grades. What matters is how someone is doing on the inside, and for how long.

2 of 5. In teens, depression often looks like being irritable or angry.

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Agree. It's common enough that doctors count irritability as a core sign for people under 18.

3 of 5. Asking a friend directly about suicide could put the idea in their head.

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Disagree. In a study of more than 2,300 New York high school students, being asked about suicide didn't raise distress or suicidal thinking. The most at-risk students seemed less distressed afterward. Asking can be a relief.

4 of 5. Most teens with depression get better with treatment.

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Agree. In a large federally funded trial, about 8 in 10 teens with depression responded after nine months of therapy, medicine, or both, and combining the two worked fastest.

5 of 5. Weed helps with depression.

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Disagree. It can numb a bad night. Long-term studies link teen cannabis use with higher odds of depression later, not lower.

Talk it over

  • Why is "I'm not okay" so hard to say at our age?
  • If a friend seemed off for a few weeks, what's one thing you could say?
  • Who at our school could a student go to if they were struggling?
Tip for the person leading

Assume some students in the room are dealing with depression right now. Don't ask anyone to share their own experience. Let students know a counselor is available afterward, and if anyone mentions self-harm or suicide, check in with them privately and follow your school's procedures.

You've felt flat for a month. When you mention it, a parent says, "Everyone gets sad. You just need to get out more."

Late at night you see a post from someone in your grade: "won't be a problem much longer."

What doesn't helpThese usually make it heavier.
MYTH I'll start once I feel motivated.

With depression, motivation usually comes after action. Waiting is the spiral.

MYTH Hours alone will help me reset.

Rest is good. Whole days alone with your thoughts usually make them darker.

MYTH I just need to think positive.

Depression isn't an attitude problem. Ordering yourself to cheer up mostly adds guilt.

MYTH A drink or a hit takes the edge off.

Alcohol is a depressant, and teen cannabis use is linked to more depression later. Both can numb one night and deepen the next week.

Get help right away if you or a friend

  • Has thoughts of dying, suicide, or hurting yourself.
  • Says things like "Everyone would be better off without me" or "I won't be a problem much longer."
  • Is giving away things they care about, or saying goodbye.
  • Feels completely hopeless or trapped.

Call or text 988 any time, or text HOME to 741741. In Massachusetts, the Behavioral Health Help Line (833-773-2445, call or text, any time) can send a mobile crisis team. Tell a trusted adult today, or go to an emergency room. Call 911 if someone is in danger right now. See Get help below.

Talk to someone soon if

  • You've felt low, empty, or irritable most days for two weeks or more.
  • You've stopped doing things you used to like.
  • Sleep, eating, or schoolwork has really changed.
  • You're using vapes, weed, alcohol, or pills to cope.

Depression is very treatable. A counselor is a good first stop.

What might help right now

What this might mean

Feeling nothing can be as hard as feeling too much. It's a common part of depression, not a sign you're broken.

What might help right now

  • Put on music that used to move you, even if it doesn't yet.
  • Move your body. Feelings often follow movement.
  • If it's been two weeks or more, tell a counselor.

What this might mean

Snapping at everything can be depression or exhaustion showing up, not proof you're a bad person.

What might help right now

  • Burn some of it off: run, lift, walk fast.
  • Check your sleep. Short sleep turns irritability up.
  • Anger Is Information has more ideas.

What might help right now

  • Send one text: "Want to get food this week?"
  • Sit with people at lunch, even if you're quiet.
  • Stop by the counseling office. That's what it's for.

What this might mean

Lighter days count. Notice what helped.

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
  • Tell a counselor, teacher, coach, or parent today. You can show them this screen.
