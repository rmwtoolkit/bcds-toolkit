Part 1 · Understand it

What's really going on

Sound familiar?The group was fine yesterday. Today something's off.
  • The group made a new chat, and you found out it existed by accident.
  • One person decides where everyone sits, and nobody says it out loud.
  • A friend is really nice when it's just the two of you, and different when the group is around.
  • "Don't tell anyone, but..." turned into everyone knowing by lunch.
  • Someone said, "If you hang out with her, you can't hang out with us."
  • The guys keep roasting the same kid, and now he's not invited to play.
  • You're nonbinary or trans, and you can tell the group isn't sure which "side" you belong on.
  • You laughed at a comment about someone's clothes and felt bad about it later.
  • You've been the one leaving someone out, and you're not totally sure how it started.

This happens in a lot of friend groups, especially in middle school: groups of girls, groups of guys, mixed groups, online groups. It can look different in each one. Most kids, of every gender, don't run their friendships this way. If you've been on either side of it, you're not a bad person. You're learning how groups work, like everyone else your age.

🧠 Why it hurts so muchBeing left out sets off a real alarm in your brain.

In a famous study, people played a simple online game of catch. Partway through, the other players stopped throwing them the ball. Brain scans showed activity in some of the same areas that respond to physical pain.

Your brain treats belonging as something that matters a lot, because it does.

That was a game of catch with strangers, and it still stung. When it's your actual friends, of course it hurts.

Popular isn't the same as likedResearchers measure them separately for a reason.

When researchers ask students "Who's popular?" and then "Who do you like?", they often get different lists. The students with the most social power aren't always the ones people like most.

Using meanness to get power tends to make someone seem more popular and less liked at the same time.

Psychologist Lisa Damour, who works with teen girls, puts it this way: some kids are popular because they're fun to be around. Others have a lot of social power, and they don't always use it well.

Four ways friendship gets used as a weapon

Tap each one.

🚪Leaving out on purpose

Planning hangouts, chats, or seats so one person knows they weren't invited.

🤫Secrets and rumors

Sharing something private, or starting a story, to change how people see someone.

🧊The freeze-out

Silent treatment, eye rolls, leaving texts on read. Someone feels it, and nobody has to say anything.

🔗"Pick me or her"

Using the friendship as a threat: "I'll only be your friend if you do what I want."

Researchers call this relational aggression. Kids of every gender do it.

How it can look in different groupsSame power game. Different moves.
👭 In a lot of girls' groups
It's often quieter. A new chat without you, a secret that got out, the freeze-out.
👬 In a lot of guys' groups
It's often louder. Roasting that always lands on the same kid, "jokes" about being soft or gay, and getting dropped from the game, the team, or the lobby.
👥 In mixed groups
Crushes and dating get pulled in. Who likes who can decide who's in, and someone can end up used as a pawn.
🏳️‍⚧️ For trans and nonbinary kids
Lots of groups split by gender, so a trans or nonbinary kid can get left out of both. Sharing someone's old name or photos, or outing them, is one of the most hurtful versions of this.

These are patterns, not rules. Any of them can happen in any group.

😶 Why everyone goes alongNobody wants to be next.

In a lot of groups, several people feel uncomfortable and say nothing, because they're worried they'll be the next one left out. So everyone is quietly nervous, and the group looks like it agrees.

Researchers who watched kids at recess found that other kids were around for almost 9 out of 10 bullying moments, and stepped in only about 1 in 5 times.

Here's the part most people don't know: when someone did step in, it stopped more than half the time.

What a good friend group feels likeYou can relax in it.
🫶 Safe
You don't have to keep checking the chat to see if you're still in.
🔓 Open
People can have other friends, and nobody calls it betrayal.
🗣 Honest
Problems get talked about with the person, not behind their back.
🔁 Fair
The same person isn't always in charge of the plans, the seats, or the mood.
🧩 Room to be different
Different families, cultures, faiths, styles, and bodies all fit. So do different ways of being a girl, and friends who aren't girls at all.

What friendship looks like can depend on your family and culture too. In some families, friends are treated like family and loyalty runs deep. In others, people keep more to themselves. Worth asking: what did the adults who raise you teach you about friendship?

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Staying yourself in the group

What actually helps

Not a doormat. Not a bully. The middle.

Tap one to see how.

🧭 Assertive is the middle.

Psychologist Lisa Damour, who works with teen girls, says many of them think there are only two choices: go along with everything, or be mean. Plenty of guys get stuck there too. There's a third. Stand up for yourself and still be kind.

🗣 Say it to the person.

"Hey, I felt left out when you made a new chat. Did something happen?" One on one, calm, and short.

🤐 Let it stop with you.

When a secret or rumor reaches you, you don't have to pass it on.

🌱 Have more than one group.

A friend from your team, your neighborhood, your place of worship, a club, or a cousin. If one group goes cold, you're not alone.

🛡 Check on the person left out.

"Want to sit with us?" or a quick text. It costs you a little and means a lot to them.

🧑‍🏫 Tell an adult if it keeps going.

A counselor can help without turning it into a big public thing.

Try itThree moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd actually do.

Your group's photos from a hangout are all over everyone's stories. You weren't invited, and nobody told you.

Jada says, "If you sit with Priya at lunch, don't bother sitting with us."

Your friends have roasted Eli all week. Now they start a game without him and say, "Don't invite him, he's so soft."

You won't always get it right. Doing it once is more than most people do.

👥 In a groupPick a corner. Then see if your guess was right.

👥 For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move or point to a corner of the room: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. No one has to share.
  3. After each one, the leader taps it to show what research says.
👥 Four corners · 1 of 4"Only girls leave people out on purpose."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not true. A review of 148 studies found boys do this almost as much as girls do. Boys also get into more physical fights.

👥 Four corners · 2 of 4"The most popular kids are the most liked."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Often not. When researchers ask "Who's popular?" and "Who do you like?", they get different names. Kids who are kind and trustworthy are the ones people like.

👥 Four corners · 3 of 4"If you stand up for someone, you'll get kicked out of the group."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Sometimes it's awkward for a minute. In one study at recess, when a kid stepped in, the bullying stopped more than half the time.

👥 Four corners · 4 of 4"Talking about a problem over and over with your best friend always helps."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not always. Friends who go over the same problem again and again feel closer, and they also tend to feel more worried and down. Talk it through, then go do something together.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Why do you think people go along with leaving someone out?
  • What's the difference between a close group and a closed group?
  • What did your family teach you about friendship and loyalty?
Tip for the person leading

Keep it about situations, not named students or groups. Someone in the room may be in the middle of this right now. Don't ask anyone to share who did what.

When you're the one left outA rough week isn't the rest of your life.
📆 Groups change a lot right now.
Friend groups shift often in middle school. The group you're in now may look very different by next year.
🧍 Find one person.
You don't need a whole group. One good friend makes a big difference.
📵 Skip the highlight reel.
You don't have to watch their stories tonight. Muting is allowed.
🧑‍🏫 Talk to someone.
A counselor, a coach, a parent or caregiver, an older cousin. You don't have to sort this out alone.
If you've been the one doing itMost people have, at some point.

Sometimes it starts small: a chat you didn't add someone to, a joke that kept going. Noticing it is the hard part, and you just did.

🔄 Change one thing.
Add them back. Save them a seat. Stop the joke.
💬 Keep the apology short.
"I left you out, and that wasn't okay. I'm sorry." No speech needed.
🤔 Ask what was going on for you.
Were you scared of being left out yourself? That's very common, and worth talking about with someone you trust.

When it crosses a line

  • Someone is sharing photos, real or fake, or private information about a person.
  • A group is threatening someone, or it's been going on for weeks.
  • Being left out has you dreading school, not eating, or not sleeping.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult today. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night.

Other ways in

Friend-group power shows up in a lot of stories, for every kind of kid. One of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

The Recess Queen · Alexis O'Neill, 2002 Throwback

Mean Jean runs the playground and nobody dares play until she does. Then a new kid asks her to jump rope. A picture book about power and what it's covering for.

Restart · Gordon Korman, 2017

Chase falls off a roof and can't remember being the football star who ran the school with his two friends. When he finds out how they treated people, he starts hanging out with the kids they used to roast.

Heads up: Includes bullying by Chase's old friends.

🎬 Watch

Monsters University · Pixar, 2013

The top fraternity on campus decides who matters and who's a joke. Mike and Sulley start out on opposite sides of that line. Rated G.

🎧 Listen

Stick to the Status Quo · High School Musical cast, 2006

Everyone in the cafeteria has a box, and the group gets loud when anyone steps out of theirs. That's the group making the rules.

🖼️ Look

Invisible Emmie · Terri Libenson, 2017

Quiet Emmie writes an embarrassing note, and it falls into the wrong hands. By the end of the day everyone's talking. A graphic novel about secrets and rumors.

What this might mean

Being left out hurts, and your brain is built to notice it. It says more about how the group is working right now than about what you're worth.

What might help right now

  • 🧍Reach out to one person today, inside or outside the group.
  • 📵Mute the stories and the chat for tonight.
  • 🧑‍🏫Tell a counselor or trusted adult, especially if it keeps happening.

What this might mean

Being pulled between friends is exhausting. You're allowed to like more than one person.

What might help right now

  • 🧭Try: "I like you both. I'm not picking."
  • 🤐Don't carry messages back and forth.
  • 🗣Talk it over with someone outside the group.
