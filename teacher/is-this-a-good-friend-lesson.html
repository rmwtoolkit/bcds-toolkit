Lesson plan

Goal

Students can use the good-news test and the green and red flags to judge a friendship by its pattern over months, recognize the gray areas (talking problems to death, risk with friends watching), and name one way to change a friendship without ending it. Nobody names or describes a real friend out loud.

Materials

  • Index cards for the private flag check and the exit card
  • Small slips with a piece of "good news" for the pair practice (or let students make one up)
  • The take-home link (last slide) to share after class

Flow

  1. 5 minMyth or fact (stand up, sit down)
  2. 8 minThe good-news test (pair practice)
  3. 7 minGray areas and patterns that wear you down
  4. 7 minGreen flags, red flags (private card)
  5. 10 minGreen, red, or gray? (small groups)
  6. 5 minChange it without ending it
  7. 3 minExit card and take-home link

Short on time (20-minute advisory)

Myth or fact (5), the good-news test (5), green and red flags (5), one adjust step and the link (5). The take-home sheet has the full flag check and the online moves.

Before you teach

Friends are sitting in the same room.

The friend a student is thinking about may be two desks away. No names, initials, or stories about real friendships, in pairs or out loud. The flag check stays on a card that goes home.

Steer away from "toxic" and "cut them off."

Most friendships in the gray zone are worth adjusting, and many 9th graders have one or two friends to lose. The research supports judging patterns over months and changing the friendship before ending it.

Some red flags are an adult's job.

Threats to share secrets, screenshots, or photos to control someone, threats to hurt anyone, pressure into unsafe things, or getting others to gang up: thank the student and connect them with a counselor that day. For sexual images of anyone under 18, the free Take It Down tool from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children can help get them removed.

If a student says a friend talks about hurting themselves.

That goes to a counselor right away, and the student should hear that telling is looking out for the friend. Don't discuss it in front of the class.

Co-rumination is a kind friend's habit.

Students who talk a friend through every problem are trying to help. Present the ten-minute limit as a better way to help, and keep blame out of it. If what's being replayed sounds like more than ordinary stress, point both students to a counselor.

Dating comes up.

Tracking, phone checks, and jealousy show up in dating too. The same flags apply. Keep examples to "someone you're close to" or "someone you like" without assuming genders.

Every student in the room

Culture, family, identity, ability and money: what to keep in mind for this topic.

What a good friend looks like varies.

In some families and cultures close friends are treated like family: constant contact, showing up unannounced, sharing almost everything. In others more privacy is normal. Neither is wrong. Ask students what a good friend looks like where they come from instead of naming one norm as healthy.

Accent jokes are a real red flag.

The Imani card is about a joke about someone's accent. Students who speak another language at home, or are new to the country, hear these often. Name it as a put-down that hits a sore spot, and treat Imani's private feedback as the green flag.

LGBTQ+ students and secrets held over them.

"I'll tell people" can mean threatening to out someone. That's the controller pattern and a counselor conversation that day. Students who aren't out may need a safe adult other than family. The Trevor Project (call 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678-678) is free.

Reading social cues comes harder for some students.

Autistic students and others who find tone hard to read may miss when a joke has turned into a put-down, or be the target of "jokes" more often. The green and red flags give them concrete checks. Never treat eye contact or being outgoing as the sign of a good friend.

The outside view comes from anyone trusted.

"A parent or older sibling" doesn't fit every home. Say "an adult you trust": a grandparent, an aunt, a coach, a foster parent, a counselor, someone at a faith community, or a friend outside the group.

Money shows up in put-downs and plans.

Jokes about clothes, phones, or where someone lives hit students with less money hardest. And "turn the volume down" can simply mean skipping the plans that cost money and putting time into free ones with people who leave you better off.

What the research says

Couples whose partners responded to good news with interest and questions were more satisfied and less likely to break up two months later. Shrugging, poking holes, or changing the subject went with dissatisfaction. The studies were with dating couples and are widely applied to friendship.

Gable, Gonzaga and Strachman, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2006

Adults who talked about a stressful event with an ambivalent friend (sometimes supportive, sometimes hurtful) had a bigger rise in blood pressure than with a supportive friend, and higher resting heart rates.

Holt-Lunstad et al., Annals of Behavioral Medicine, 2007 (Brigham Young University)

Co-rumination, friends going over the same problems again and again, was linked with closer, higher-quality friendships and with more anxiety and depression.

Rose, Child Development, 2002

In a driving video game, teenagers took more risks when friends were watching than when they played alone. The effect was much smaller in adults.

Gardner and Steinberg, Developmental Psychology, 2005

In fifth and sixth graders, bad experiences raised cortisol and lowered self-worth less when a best friend was present.

Adams, Santo and Bukowski, Developmental Psychology, 2011

Take It Down is a free tool that helps remove sexual images of anyone under 18 from online platforms.

National Center for Missing & Exploited Children

Slides and notes

Tap any slide to present from there.

Slide 1 · Notes

Open plainly. Something like: "You learned the obvious red flags years ago. Today is the next layer: friendships that aren't toxic, exactly, but are costing you something. Nobody's going to name a friend today. Everything personal stays on paper that goes home with you."

Slide 2 · Notes

5 MINUTES. Read each one. Students stand for fact, stay seated for myth. Don't reveal answers yet, just notice where the room splits. Interactive option: have a student read the statements.

Slide 3 · Notes

Point out the one the room split on most. #4 comes from a study where teenagers played a driving video game. With friends watching, they took more risks than playing alone. The effect was much smaller in adults. These were friends, which is the point: good friends can still make it harder to say no, especially in a car. #3 we'll come back to in a minute.

Slide 4 · Notes

8 MINUTES with the next slide. Most people judge a friend by how they show up when things go wrong. Psychologist Shelly Gable found that how people respond when things go right may matter at least as much. Couples whose partners responded to good news with real interest and questions were more satisfied and less likely to break up two months later. Her studies were with dating couples; the pattern is widely applied to friends but less tested there. Why it's a useful test: bad news pulls support out of almost anyone. Being glad for you without competing is harder. Ask students to notice which column their friends usually land in, and which column they usually land in.

Slide 5 · Notes

Assign partners so nobody has to pick. The news can be small or made up: a good quiz grade, a new shift at a job, a song they learned. One minute each way. Then by thumbs: "Thumbs up if that felt good to hear." Most go up. Point out how easy it is to skip: a follow-up question takes five seconds. Anyone can pass or write their answer instead of saying it.

Slide 6 · Notes

7 MINUTES. Talking points. Psychologist Amanda Rose studied co-rumination, friends going over the same problems again and again. It went with closer, higher-quality friendships and with more anxiety and depression. A friendship can feel deeply supportive and still keep both people stuck. The rollercoaster: in a Brigham Young University study, talking about a stressful event with an ambivalent friend, sometimes supportive and sometimes hurtful, raised blood pressure more than talking with a supportive friend. The researchers suggested people can't fully relax around a friend they can't predict. The put-down: if you've said it hurts and it continues, it isn't a joke. The controller follows you online: upset when you don't reply fast, wants your location on all the time, threatens to share messages or photos. The other side of the research: in fifth and sixth graders, a best friend nearby made bad experiences raise the stress hormone cortisol less. A good friend changes how stress hits you. Close with: the cost of a bad pattern often shows up as shrinking. Quieter, more careful, more worried. A good friend leaves you more like yourself.

Slide 7 · Notes

7 MINUTES. Private. On an index card, students think of one friend (no names, no initials) and mark which lines are usually true. The card goes home. Talking points: judge over months, not moments. Everyone has bad weeks. Most friendships have a mix; the question is the usual pattern. More green flags from the student's list: they keep private things private, and they're glad you have other friends. More red flags: jokes that keep hitting the same sore spots, and tracking you, checking your phone, or holding screenshots over you. If a student looks upset, check in quietly after class.

Slide 8 · Notes

10 MINUTES. One card per group, then report back. Dev: red flag, worth a boundary. Caring about safety doesn't require a live map, and being punished for turning it off is control. Leaving it on and avoiding places he'd mind is the shrinking pattern. A boundary sounds like: "I'm keeping location off. I'll text you when I get home." Imani: green flag. She told you privately and gave you a chance to fix it. "She's starting drama" is the defensive reaction; the friend who says the hard thing kindly is usually a keeper. Luis: gray area and the audience effect in real life. Line up another ride and say something once ("can you slow down? I get carsick" works fine). Dropping the whole group is bigger than it needs to be; "it's his car" leaves everyone at risk. If students mention who has a license, keep it about the moment, not about reporting anyone.

Slide 9 · Notes

5 MINUTES. How a friend reacts to step 1 tells you a lot; people don't always know how they come across. A boundary is what you will do, and then you follow through. Step 3 is for co-rumination: talking still helps; endless replaying doesn't. Online: mute, restrict, and block are boundaries, and you don't owe anyone instant replies or a live map. If someone threatens to share messages or photos, screenshot the threat and tell an adult. A note on culture: in some families close friends are treated like family, with constant contact; in others more privacy is normal. The question is whether the friendship respects you and fits the values you and your family hold.

Slide 10 · Notes

3 MINUTES. Students keep the card. The second option is for students who don't have a friendship to fix right now. Share the take-home link (post it on your class page too). It has the flags, the adjust steps, the online moves, a private check-in, and who to talk to. Remind students: a friend who threatens to share secrets or photos, threatens to hurt anyone, pressures them into unsafe things, or gets others to gang up on them is not a friendship problem to fix alone. Tell an adult right away.
