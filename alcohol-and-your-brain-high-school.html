Part 1 · Understand it

What alcohol does inside your brain

Sound familiar?Everyone has an opinion. Few people know the mechanism.
  • The plan for the dance includes a pregame, and you're not sure how you feel about it.
  • Monday's story is that someone "blacked out," told like a highlight.
  • An older friend says he's fine to drive because he "only had a couple."
  • At home, drinking is either a normal part of dinner or completely off limits, and neither one came with an explanation.
  • You've wondered whether college will be like this, and whether you'll have to choose.

You've probably heard the basics already. This goes a level down: what alcohol does to specific parts of the brain, what the research on teen drinkers shows, and what to do when a night goes wrong.

76%of U.S. 10th graders, and 59% of seniors, did not drink at all in the past year. Teen drinking is near the lowest level this survey has recorded.Monitoring the Future, 2025

Four regions, in roughly the order alcohol reaches them

1. Prefrontal cortex: judgment goes first

Alcohol strengthens GABA, the brain's main "slow down" signal. The prefrontal cortex, which weighs consequences, feels it early. That's why the next drink, the dare, or the drive home starts to sound reasonable before you feel drunk.

2. Hippocampus: memory stops saving

The hippocampus writes new memories using glutamate receptors that alcohol blocks. With enough alcohol, fast, recording stops while you're still awake, talking, and making choices. That gap is a blackout. It is not the same as passing out.

3. Cerebellum: balance and speech

Coordination, balance, and clear speech come from here. Stumbling and slurring are the cerebellum falling behind.

4. Brainstem: breathing and the gag reflex

The brainstem runs breathing, heart rate, body temperature, and the reflex that stops you from choking on vomit. When alcohol slows it, that is alcohol poisoning, and it can kill.

The researchThe warning comes late. The effects can last.
Teen brains get fewer "stop" signals.
In animal research reviewed by neuroscientist Linda Spear (2018), adolescents were less sensitive than adults to the sleepy, wobbly effects that tell people they've had enough, and more sensitive to the social rewards. Human teens also drink more per occasion than adults.
Heavy drinking tracks with faster gray matter loss.
A study led by Lindsay Squeglia (2015) scanned 134 young people repeatedly between ages 12 and 24. Those who became heavy drinkers lost gray matter faster in frontal and temporal areas than those who didn't, and the pattern held after accounting for marijuana. A 2018 study of 483 teens scanned before they ever drank found the same frontal pattern after heavy drinking began. These studies show a link, not proof of cause.
Blackouts are common, and so is what happens during them.
In a survey of 772 college students (White and colleagues, 2002), 51% of those who had ever drunk alcohol reported at least one blackout. Many later learned they had done things they couldn't remember, including driving and having sex.
Starting age matters.
In a national survey of 43,093 adults (Hingson, 2006), 47% of people who started drinking before 14 developed alcohol dependence at some point, compared with 9% of those who waited until 21 or later. Early drinking may also be a marker of other risks, so the age itself isn't the whole story.
The curveThe last drink is not the peak.

Alcohol keeps moving from the stomach into the blood for 30 to 90 minutes after the last sip. Then the liver clears roughly one standard drink an hour, at a pace nothing speeds up. Coffee, a cold shower, food, or throwing up don't lower it. Someone who seems fine at 10:30 can be in danger at 11:15.

One standard drink is 12 oz of beer (about 5%), 5 oz of wine (about 12%), or 1.5 oz of liquor (40%). A mixed drink in a party cup is often two or more, and nobody's measuring.

Behind the wheelFor drivers under 21, the legal limit is close to zero.
ZADOR, KRAWCHUK, AND VOAS, 2000

For male drivers aged 16 to 20, every 0.02 rise in blood alcohol more than doubled the risk of dying in a single-vehicle crash. Young drivers hit dangerous risk at lower levels than older drivers.

In Massachusetts, a driver under 21 with a blood alcohol level of 0.02 or higher loses their license on the spot, before any court date. An OUI charge adds a court case and a longer suspension on top of that.

If the driver has been drinking, everyone in the car shares the risk. Your plan for getting home matters as much as your plan for the night.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Running your own night

What actually helps

Know the signs. Make the call.

When a night goes wrong, there are usually other people around. What matters is whether one of them knows what they're looking at and doesn't wait.

Tap each one.

1 Know the signs of alcohol poisoning.

Confused or can't be woken. Vomiting while passed out. Slow breathing (fewer than 8 breaths a minute) or gaps of 10 seconds or more. Cold, clammy, pale, or bluish skin. A seizure. Any one of these: call 911. They don't need all of them.

2 Side, not back. And stay.

Roll them onto their side, top knee bent, head tilted so anything comes out instead of in. Stay until help arrives. "Let them sleep it off" is how people choke.

3 Make the call, even when everyone is scared.

Massachusetts' Good Samaritan law protects people who call 911 for a drug overdose from drug-possession charges. It doesn't specifically cover underage drinking. Call anyway. EMTs are there for the medical emergency, and a consequence for drinking is something people get through. Many schools and colleges also have amnesty policies for students who call. Ask about yours.

4 Set up your exit plan before you go.

Pick one person you can text a code word to, no explanation needed: a parent, an older sibling, a cousin, an aunt, a family friend. Families handle this differently. Some will pick you up with no questions that night, and some won't. Figure out who your person is ahead of time.

5 If alcohol is there, protect the basics.

Most of your grade isn't drinking, and not drinking is a normal choice. If someone does drink: eat first, know what a standard drink is, go slow, never mix with weed or pills, keep your own cup in your hand, and never drive or ride with someone who drank.

6 Talk to someone.

Worried about your drinking, a friend's, or someone's at home? Counselors handle this all the time. You can ask about privacy first.

Try itThree real moments. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

Your ride home, Jae, had two beers about three hours ago. He's 18, says he feels completely fine, and everyone else has already left.

Your friend Amara laughs about blacking out last weekend. You realize it's the third time this semester.

At a family celebration, an uncle pours you a small glass of wine. Your parents are fine with it.

The best answer is the one you'll actually do.

In a groupOne graduation party. Four decisions. You vote.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read each stop out loud. Dani is at a graduation party at a classmate's house. The parents are home.
  2. Everyone votes with fingers: 1, 2, or 3. No talking until the vote.
  3. Ask a couple of people why. Then tap to see what usually happens.
  4. Stop 4 matters most.
Graduation party · 1 of 4Stop 1 · 8:30pm

A friend's dad is mixing drinks for anyone who wants one. "Better here than somewhere worse," he says.

  1. Take one. An adult said it's fine
  2. "I'm good," and grab a soda
  3. Take one and nurse it all night
What usually happens

2 is the simplest. An adult being there doesn't change what alcohol does. In Massachusetts, an adult who gives alcohol to someone else's kid under 21 can be fined up to $2,000 or jailed for up to a year, and can be sued if someone gets hurt.

Graduation party · 2 of 4Stop 2 · 10:00pm

Dani's friend Theo did four shots in twenty minutes on a dare. "I feel nothing," he says.

  1. Believe him
  2. Get him water and stay close for the next hour
  3. Tell him to slow down and walk away
What usually happens

2. Four shots that fast haven't fully hit yet. Remember the curve: he'll keep rising for up to an hour and a half.

Graduation party · 3 of 4Stop 3 · 11:00pm

Theo is slurring, has thrown up, and wants to lie down alone in the basement.

  1. Let him sleep it off
  2. Go with him, keep him on his side, and stay
  3. Check on him in an hour
What usually happens

2. Never leave someone alone to sleep it off. Keep checking for the signs of alcohol poisoning.

Graduation party · 4 of 4Stop 4 · 11:20pm

Theo won't wake up. His breathing has long pauses. Someone says, "Don't call. His parents will kill him, and the host's parents could get arrested."

  1. Wait and see
  2. Call 911
  3. Drive him home
What usually happens

Call 911, every time. Pauses in breathing mean his brainstem is slowing down. Trouble is temporary. This might not be.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • At which stop was the right move hardest? What made it hard?
  • How did the adults at the party change what people thought was safe?
  • What would make it easier for people our age to call 911?
  • How do families you know handle alcohol differently? What did that teach people?
Tip for the person leading

Make Stop 4 completely clear: call 911. Keep it about Dani and Theo, not anyone in the room. Families and cultures handle alcohol very differently; avoid treating one as the right one.

Myths that hold up badlyWhat the research says instead.
MYTH I feel fine, so I'm fine to drive.

Feeling fine is the prefrontal cortex talking, and it's the first area alcohol impairs. Blood alcohol can still be rising.

MYTH Blacking out means you passed out.

A blackout means memory stopped recording while you were still awake and acting. Passing out is losing consciousness. Both are signs of a lot of alcohol.

MYTH Drinking with family teaches you to drink safely.

A six-year study of about 1,900 Australian teens (Mattick, 2018) found no protective effect: teens whose parents gave them alcohol were more likely to get it from other sources too. It's one study, and families make this choice for many reasons.

MYTH Everyone drinks in high school.

Three out of four 10th graders didn't drink in the past year. Most people overestimate how many classmates drink.

MYTH Energy drinks cancel it out.

Caffeine makes a drunk person feel more awake. It doesn't lower blood alcohol, and feeling awake often leads to drinking more.

Call 911 right away if someone

  • Can't be woken up, or keeps passing out.
  • Has slow, irregular, or gasping breathing.
  • Has cold, clammy, pale, or bluish skin.
  • Vomits while passed out, or has a seizure.
  • Mixed alcohol with pills or other drugs.

Roll them on their side and stay with them. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222. See Get help below.
