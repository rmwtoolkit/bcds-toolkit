Part 1 · Understand it

Why it hits this hard

Sound familiar?You know it's over. Your brain hasn't caught up.
  • You know it's over, and you still reread old messages at 1 a.m.
  • You're fine all day, then one song in the car wrecks you.
  • You ended it, it was the right call, and you still feel terrible.
  • You sit two rows from them in chemistry every day.
  • People keep saying "it was only a few months."
  • Your family didn't know about the relationship, so you can't talk about it at home.

You already know breakups hurt. Here's why the pain takes the shape it does, and what research says about getting through it.

Your brain is still wired for them

VTA: the wanting doesn't switch off

A hub of the brain's dopamine system. In a 2010 brain-imaging study of people recently rejected but still in love, it lit up when they looked at a photo of their ex, much as it does in people who are happily in love. The relationship ended. The wanting didn't, yet.

Nucleus accumbens: the pull to check

Part of the reward system, also active in that study. The researchers noted overlap with regions involved in craving. That's one reason the urge to text or check their story feels like a pull more than a decision. Whether rejection truly works like addiction is still debated.

Insula: why it can feel physical

In a 2011 study, 40 people who'd had an unwanted breakup in the past six months looked at their ex's photo in a scanner. Areas that also process physical pain became active, including part of the insula. Other scientists argue these areas aren't pain-specific, so "heartbreak is physical pain" goes too far. The overlap is still real.

Orbitofrontal cortex: making sense of it

Weighs gains and losses and helps regulate emotion. It was active as rejected people tried to understand what happened. It's the part you're using when you step back and rethink the story.

The researchMost people recover faster than they expect. Not everyone.
You'll probably feel better sooner than you predict.
A Northwestern study asked college students in relationships to predict how upset they'd be if they broke up, then followed the ones who did. Most overestimated their distress, and the people most in love overestimated it the most.
And breakups are a real risk factor.
In a study of 1,470 older teens, a recent romantic breakup was linked to a higher chance of a first episode of major depression. Both things are true: most people recover well, and some need more help. If the low isn't lifting, that's a signal, not a weakness.
Rebuilding "me" is the work.
In a 2015 study of 210 young adults after a breakup, the ones who reflected on it repeatedly regained a clearer sense of who they were. That clearer sense of self, apart from "us," predicted less loneliness and fewer intrusive thoughts.
Checking their profileEvery check resets the clock.

In a study of 464 people, those who kept checking an ex's profile reported more distress, more longing, and less personal growth, even after accounting for how much they saw the ex in person. The study can't prove checking causes the pain. People who miss someone more may check more.

Still, muting them for two weeks is the cheapest experiment you can run on yourself.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Getting through it

Strategies with research behind them

What works, and what each one costs.

Rethink the storyIn a 2018 study, deliberately focusing on an ex's flaws lowered love feelings, but made people feel worse in the moment. Useful when you're stuck idealizing them. Go easy.
Distract on purposeSame study: distraction lifted mood but didn't change love feelings. Good for getting through a bad night.
Cut the exposureMute, archive, move the app. Fewer reminders means fewer restarts.
Rebuild "me"Friends, plans, and interests that were yours before. A clearer sense of self predicted better recovery.

More moves. Tap to see how.

1 Plan for the hard hours.

Nights, weekends, the drive home. Know in advance who you'll text and what you'll do instead of scrolling.

2 Decide how to handle shared spaces.

Same class, team, or friends? Pick your script ahead of time: a nod, polite and short, no big talks in public.

3 Keep your routine.

Sleep, food, practice, work shifts. Routine keeps a bad week from turning into a bad month.

4 Let it be as big as it is.

Short relationships, secret ones, and first ones can hurt a lot. You don't need anyone's permission to grieve it.

Try itSay all of it. You won't hit send.

Pick the most helpful move.

Two weeks after the breakup, Rafael's ex texts at 11:40 p.m.: "I miss you. Can we talk?" He's still hurting.

Priyanka's ex is now dating someone in their friend group, and they all sit at the same lunch table.

Malik has dated someone for five months and wants to end it. He doesn't want to be cruel.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths about getting over itWhat people say, and what's closer to true.
MYTH If it was short, it shouldn't hurt this much.

Your reward system doesn't check the calendar. How much you invested matters more than how long it lasted.

MYTH Staying friends right away is the mature move.

In the profile study, people who stayed online friends with an ex reported less longing but also less personal growth. Friendship can come later, if you both want it.

MYTH Someone new will fix it.

A new person can be a distraction, and distraction helps for a while. It doesn't do the work of rebuilding your own sense of self.

MYTH If I ended it, I don't get to be sad.

You can lose something you chose to leave. Guilt and relief can sit next to each other.

When to get more help

  • The sadness isn't easing after a few weeks, or you feel hopeless.
  • You're having trouble eating, sleeping, or getting to school.
  • Your ex is threatening you, harassing you, or sharing private images. In Massachusetts, sharing explicit images without consent is a crime.
  • You're thinking about hurting yourself. Call or text 988 any time.

A counselor can help, and so can love is respect (text LOVEIS to 22522) if your ex won't leave you alone. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Every generation writes the breakup song again. Here are a few that get the hard part right.

📖 Read

Why We Broke Up · Daniel Handler, art by Maira Kalman, 2011

Min writes Ed a long letter and drops it off with a box of everything from their six weeks together. It's the message you're not sending, written all the way out. A Printz Honor book.

Heads up: Frank about sex and teen relationships, though not graphic. Librarians often suggest it for older high schoolers.

🎬 Watch

(500) Days of Summer · Marc Webb, 2009

Watch the split screen where Tom's expectations play next to what actually happens. He has to rethink the story before he can get back to architecture, the thing that was his before her.

Heads up: Rated PG-13 for sexual material and language, with some drinking.

Legally Blonde · Robert Luketic, 2001

Warner dumps Elle, so she gets into Harvard Law to win him back. By the end she wants something of her own, and him far less. That's rebuilding me.

Heads up: Rated PG-13 for language and sexual references.

🎧 Listen

When She Loved Me · Sarah McLachlan, written by Randy Newman, 1999 Throwback

Jessie's song from Toy Story 2, about Emily slowly outgrowing her. Proof your reward system doesn't check the calendar, even for a cowgirl doll.

Dancing On My Own · Robyn, 2010

She's in the corner of the club watching her ex with someone new, and she can't stop looking. Every check resets the clock. This is what that feels like.

✏️ Make your own

Make the box from Why We Broke Up, on paper. List or sketch five things from the relationship. Next to each one, decide whether to keep it or let it go. Then add one thing that was yours before them.
