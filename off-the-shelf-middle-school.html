Snapshot · Middle School · Substance use

Off the Shelf

"Legal" and "natural" are marketing words.

What's going on

Some of the riskiest highs are also the cheapest and easiest to buy. Every inhalant hit cuts your brain's oxygen. Nitrous wipes out vitamin B12, which your nerves need to keep their protective coating.

A bright tank or package→"It's legal, so it's safe"→A one-minute high→Less oxygen, passing out→Nerve damage that builds

How you'll know

💭 What people say

  • "Just laughing gas, like the dentist."
  • "It's legal, so it's fine."
  • "It's like an energy drink."

🫀 Warning signs

  • numb hands and feet
  • tingling
  • weak legs, trouble walking

🏷️ On the package

  • "natural," "legal," "botanical"
  • "not for human consumption"
  • made to look like candy

Where to step in

Read past the marketing.

  1. "Natural," "legal," and "botanical" are sales words. They say nothing about safety.
  2. Have your line: "Nah, that stuff wrecks your nerves." Short and true.
  3. If someone uses near you, keep them sitting so they don't pass out and fall.

Call 911 right away if someone passes out and won't wake up, or isn't breathing normally.

Part 1 · Understand it

Whippets, dusters, and the stuff behind the counter

Sound familiar?Cheap, easy to buy, and misunderstood.
  • You've seen colorful "Galaxy Gas" tanks in a smoke shop or online.
  • Someone called whippets "just laughing gas, like at the dentist."
  • You've heard of kids huffing spray cans or computer dusters.
  • You've seen shots or gummies behind a gas station counter with names you don't recognize.
  • Someone said it's legal, so it can't be that bad.

Some of the riskiest highs are also the cheapest and easiest to buy. Here's what they actually do.

What's on the shelfWhippets, dusters, kratom, and more.
🎈 Nitrous oxide.
Laughing gas. Sold as whipped-cream chargers (whippets) and big flavored tanks like Galaxy Gas. People inhale it from balloons.
🧴 Household inhalants.
Computer dusters, spray paint, glue, markers, and other chemicals people sniff or huff.
🌿 Kratom and 7-OH.
Plant products sold as shots, capsules, and gummies. At higher doses they act like opioids.
⚠️ Tianeptine.
Sold as "Neptune's Fix." Not approved for any use in the U.S.
What inhalants do in your brainEvery hit cuts your brain's oxygen.
Nitrous wears down your nervesNitrous eats the coating on your nerves.
Other ways inhalants go wrongFrostbite. Falls. Heart stops.
🧊 Frostbite.
Gas comes out of the tank freezing cold. It can burn your lips, mouth, and throat.
😵‍💫 Passing out.
Blood pressure drops and people faint, often hitting their heads on the way down.
💓 Sudden sniffing death.
Breathing in chemicals like dusters can stop the heart, sometimes the very first time.
"Legal" vs. testedSold in a store. Rarely tested.
Part 2 · Practice the skills

Making your own call

What actually helps

Read past the marketing.

Tap one to see how.

🔎 Read past the marketing.

"Natural," "legal," and "botanical" are sales words. They say nothing about safety. (Remedies your family trusts are a different conversation. Ask them or a doctor.)

💬 Have your line.

"Nah, that stuff wrecks your nerves." Short and true.

🪑 If someone uses near you, keep them sitting.

Most inhalant injuries happen when people stand up, pass out, and fall.

🦶 Notice warning signs.

Numbness, tingling, or weak legs after nitrous means see a doctor soon. Treatment works better early.

🆘 Know when to call 911.

Won't wake up, slow or no breathing, blue lips, seizure, chest pain, or collapse.

Try itJust like the dentist? Let's check.

Tap an answer for each one.

Nitrous is safe because dentists use it.

You can't get hurt the first time you sniff a duster.

Kratom can act like an opioid at higher doses.

If a store sells it, the government says it's safe.

👥 In a groupRead the label like a detective.

👥 For advisory or class · about 10 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Imagine a bright package that says: "Natural. Energy and calm. Not for human consumption."
  2. In small groups, list what the package wants you to believe, and what it leaves out.
  3. Go through the next screens and vote on each claim.
👥 Read the label · 1 of 4"It only lasts a minute, so it can't hurt you."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

One minute without enough oxygen is enough to pass out and fall, and nerve damage builds with repeated use.

👥 Read the label · 2 of 4"It's basically what the dentist gives you."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Dentists mix it with oxygen and monitor you. Pure gas from a balloon is a different situation.

👥 Read the label · 3 of 4"It's legal, so it's been tested."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Most aren't FDA reviewed. Legal often just means not banned yet.

👥 Read the label · 4 of 4"It's natural, so it's safe."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Kratom is a plant and can still cause dependence, liver problems, and overdose.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Why would a product say "not for human consumption" when it's obviously meant to be used?
  • Why do you think companies make these tanks and packages look like candy?
  • What questions should you ask before trusting any product?
Tip for the person leading

Treat this as a media literacy activity. Keep it about products and claims, not personal use. Students may know family members who use kratom for pain, and families have very different rules and beliefs about substances, so stay non-judgmental.

Try itReal moments. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

At a friend's house, someone grabs a computer duster and says, "Watch this."

An older kid shows you a kratom shot from a gas station and says it's like an energy drink.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Call 911 right away if someone

  • Passes out and won't wake up, or isn't breathing normally.
  • Has blue lips, a seizure, chest pain, or an irregular heartbeat.
  • Hits their head when they fall.

Not sure it's an emergency? Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222, is free any time and can tell you whether to call 911 or go to an ER. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Long before Galaxy Gas, stories warned about shiny things with a hidden cost. A few of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

The Lorax · Dr. Seuss, 1971 Throwback

The Once-ler's sales line: "A Thneed's a Fine-Something-That-All-People-Need!" Read it like a label detective. What is he leaving out?

The Toothpaste Millionaire · Jean Merrill, 1972

Sixth grader Rufus Mayflower thinks the toothpaste at the store costs way too much, so he figures out what's really in it and makes his own. Reading past the marketing, in action.

🎬 Watch

Zootopia · Disney, 2016

The trouble in the city comes from Night Howlers, a pretty flower that turns gentle animals savage. Natural doesn't mean safe. Rated PG.

Pinocchio · Disney, 1940 Throwback

Honest John sells Pinocchio on a free trip to Pleasure Island. It looks like fun. The boys who go start turning into donkeys. Rated G.

Heads up: The boys on Pleasure Island smoke and drink, and it goes badly for them.

🎧 Listen

Poor Unfortunate Souls · Howard Ashman and Alan Menken, 1989 Throwback

Ursula's big sales pitch in The Little Mermaid. Ariel trades her voice for a deal that sounds great and leaves out the catch.

🖼️ Look

Campbell's Soup Cans · Andy Warhol, 1962

Thirty-two paintings of soup labels, hanging in a museum. Warhol made people stare at packaging. Try it with the next bright package you see at a counter.

✏️ Make your own

Redesign a Galaxy Gas tank or a gas station shot with an honest label. Use the "Legal vs. tested" chart from the lesson: is it FDA reviewed, is the dose accurate, who's selling it? Draw the label the company would never print.
