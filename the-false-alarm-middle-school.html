The False Alarm

Your alarm can't tell toast from fire.

What's going on

Deep in your brain, the amygdala works like a smoke detector. It's built to protect you, but it screams as loud for burnt toast as for a real fire. The feelings are uncomfortable, not dangerous.

Something feels scary→The alarm goes off→Heart pounds, stomach flips→You leave or skip it→The alarm gets louder

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "What if...?" over and over
  • "Are you sure it'll be okay?"
  • a brain that won't stop at bedtime

🫀 Body

  • Sunday night stomachaches
  • pounding heart
  • going blank

🔁 Behaviors

  • going to the nurse to get out
  • not raising your hand
  • eating in the library

Part 1 · Understand it

What anxiety is, and why it sticks

Sound familiar?If this sounds like you, you're in good company.
  • Your stomach hurts on Sunday nights, or before school.
  • You've gone to the nurse to get out of something scary.
  • You ask a parent "What if...?" over and over, even after they answer.
  • You know the answer but don't raise your hand.
  • Your brain won't stop at bedtime.
  • Your heart pounds when you have to order food or talk to an adult.

Anxiety is one of the most common things teens deal with. It's also one of the most treatable.

You're far from the only oneAbout 1 in 3 teens. Seriously.
Your brain's smoke detectorYour alarm can't tell toast from fire.
🚨 It's built to protect you.
Deep in your brain, a small area called the amygdala works like a smoke detector. When it senses danger, it sets off fight, flight, or freeze, fast, before you've had time to think.
🍞 It can't tell toast from fire.
A smoke detector screams just as loud for burnt toast as for a real fire. Your alarm does the same thing: a presentation can feel as scary as a bear.
🎚 Some alarms are set more sensitive.
Genes, stress, and what you've been through all tune how easily yours goes off. So can moving somewhere new, being treated unfairly for who you are, or noise and crowds hitting you harder than most. A sensitive alarm says nothing about how brave or strong you are.

What the alarm does to your body

Tap each one.

❤️Heart pounds

Your body is pumping blood to your muscles so you can run.

🌬Breathing speeds up

You might feel dizzy, tingly, or like you can't get a full breath.

🤢Stomach flips

Digestion pauses during an alarm. Hello, butterflies, nausea, and extra bathroom trips.

🌫Thinking gets foggy

The planning part of your brain steps back while the alarm runs things. That's why you go blank.

💡 These feelings are uncomfortable, not dangerousUncomfortable. Not dangerous.

Your body is doing exactly what it was built to do. The feelings rise, peak, and come back down, even if you do nothing at all.

The alarm feels like it's telling the truth. It's just loud.

The avoidance trapRunning away makes it bigger.

Some shapes anxiety takes

Tap each one.

🌀Worry that won't quit

Your brain keeps asking "What if?" about school, family, friends, everything.

👀Social anxiety

Being really scared of being embarrassed or judged by other kids.

💥Panic attacks

A sudden rush of fear with a racing heart, shaky hands, and a feeling that something terrible is happening. Scary, and not dangerous.

🎯Big specific fears

A really strong fear of one thing, like throwing up, dogs, or being away from a parent at sleepovers or camp.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Turning the alarm down

What actually helps

Walk toward it, one step at a time.

Tap one to see how.

🪜 Face it in small steps.

Pick something you avoid. Break it into small steps, from a little scary to really scary. Start at the bottom.

🌊 Ride the wave.

When the feeling hits, name it: "This is my alarm." Then stay. It rises, peaks, and falls, usually faster than you'd guess.

🌬 Make your breath out longer.

Breathe in for 4, out for 6. A long exhale tells your body the danger has passed. Prayer, meditation, or music can work too. When Your Mind Goes Blank has more resets like this.

🕵 Check the prediction.

Ask: What am I scared will happen? How likely is it really? If it did happen, could I handle it?

🗓 Give worry an appointment.

Pick 15 minutes a day for worrying. When a worry shows up at other times, jot it down and save it for then. Lots of worries look smaller by the time their appointment comes.

🙋 Ask for coaching instead of rescuing.

Asking "Are you sure it'll be okay?" again and again helps for a minute, then the worry comes right back. Ask a parent or another adult you trust to cheer you on while you try it yourself.

⚡ Watch the fuel.

Sleep, food, and moving your body all turn the alarm down. Energy drinks and lots of caffeine turn it up.

Try itStart at the bottom rung.

Pick one thing anxiety makes you avoid. Then write four steps, from easiest at the bottom to hardest at the top.

Example, raising your hand: 1. Answer a question in a small group. 2. Ask a teacher a question after class. 3. Raise your hand once when you're sure. 4. Raise your hand when you're not sure.

Stay on a step until it feels about half as scary, then climb. Going back down a step on a hard day is normal.

👥 For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read each story out loud. These are made-up students, not anyone in the room.
  2. Everyone votes: real danger, false alarm, or somewhere in between. Stand up, raise hands, or point to a corner.
  3. Tap to see what usually helps, then talk about the brave next step.
👥 Toast or fire? · 1 of 3Ava and the cafeteria

Ava's heart races every time she walks into the cafeteria, because she's not sure where to sit. Lately she's been eating in the library.

  1. Real danger
  2. False alarm
  3. Somewhere in between
What usually helps

False alarm. It feels awful, and it isn't dangerous. Hiding in the library makes tomorrow harder. A brave next step: sit with someone she half knows, or ask to join a table, and stay long enough for the alarm to settle.

👥 Toast or fire? · 2 of 3Ava and the ride

At a friend's house, an older teen who's been drinking offers to drive Ava home. Her stomach drops.

  1. Real danger
  2. False alarm
  3. Somewhere in between
What usually helps

Real fire. The alarm is doing exactly its job here. Listen to it: don't get in, and call a parent or another adult for a ride. Anxiety work is about burnt toast, never about ignoring real danger.

👥 Toast or fire? · 3 of 3Marcus and the test

Marcus has a big test Friday. Every night he asks his mom, "What if I fail?" She says he'll be fine. Ten minutes later he asks again.

  1. Real danger
  2. False alarm
  3. Somewhere in between
What usually helps

In between. The test is real and worth preparing for. The checking and asking keep the alarm loud without helping him learn. A brave next step: a set study plan, then let worry time handle the rest. When Your Mind Goes Blank has more on test anxiety.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • What are some false alarms a lot of kids our age get? (Keep it general.)
  • Why does avoiding feel so good in the moment?
  • What could a friend say that helps someone approach instead of avoid?
Tip for the person leading

Never ask students to name their own fears. If someone volunteers, keep it brief and thank them. Some students in the room may have panic attacks or an anxiety disorder, so let everyone know a counselor is available afterward.

Try itReal moments. Which way do you step?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

You have to read your story out loud in English tomorrow. Your stomach already hurts.

You're at a sleepover and start missing home at 10 p.m. You want to call home to get picked up.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually make the alarm louder.
🚪 Avoiding or escaping.

Quick relief, bigger fear next time.

🔁 Asking "Are you sure?" again and again.

Reassurance wears off fast, and you end up needing more of it.

📱 Scrolling to escape the feeling.

Distraction helps for a bit. Hours of it keep you from the thing that would actually help.

🙄 "Just calm down."

Nobody has ever calmed down because someone told them to. Including yourself.

🥤 Lots of caffeine.

Energy drinks and lots of soda can make your heart race and your alarm louder.

When to get more help

  • Anxiety shows up most days, for weeks.
  • You're missing school, practices, or time with friends because of it.
  • You're having panic attacks.
  • It's messing with your sleep or eating.
  • You're using vapes, weed, alcohol, or pills to cope.
  • You feel hopeless, or have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Anxiety is very treatable. A counselor can help you build your ladder and climb it. In one big study, most kids with anxiety got much better with this kind of help. See Get help below.
