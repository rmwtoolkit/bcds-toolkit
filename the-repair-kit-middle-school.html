Part 1 · Understand it

Why fights happen, and why repair matters

Sound familiar?Every real friendship hits a rough patch.
  • You said something in the heat of the moment that you can't stop thinking about.
  • A friend said something that really hurt, and now things are weird.
  • You had a fight at recess or lunch, and now your whole friend group is picking sides.
  • Neither of you has said sorry, and every day it gets more awkward.
  • Someone apologized, but it didn't feel like a real apology.
  • You told a secret that wasn't yours to tell.

Fights happen in every real friendship. What matters most is what happens after.

The different kinds of fights

Tap each one.

⚡The flare-up

A fast argument over something small, usually when someone's tired, stressed, or hungry. Often easy to fix if someone goes first.

🧊The cold war

Nobody's yelling. Nobody's talking either. Silence can do more damage than the fight did.

🪨The real hurt

Something broke trust: a secret shared, a betrayal, something said behind someone's back. Takes more than "sorry" to fix.

Why repair is so hardGoing first feels like losing. It isn't.
Your brain wants to be right.
When you feel attacked, your brain goes into defense mode. It's hard to see the other person's side when part of you is still in the fight.
Going first feels like losing.
Many people wait for the other person to apologize first. Meanwhile, the friendship stays stuck.
Apologies feel risky.
What if they don't accept it? What if they use it against you? Being the one who reaches out takes guts.
🧰 Repair is a skill, not a personality traitNobody's born good at fixing fights.

Nobody is born knowing how to fix a fight. It's something you get better at with practice. Research on relationships shows it's often not whether people fight, but how they repair afterward, that keeps relationships strong.

The worst part: waiting. The longer a fight sits, the bigger it tends to feel, and the harder that first message becomes.

📱 When the fight is onlineOnline fights come with an audience.

A lot of fights start, or get worse, online:

  • An argument in the group chat that everyone watched.
  • A comment or post that felt like it was aimed at you.
  • Screenshots of the fight getting passed around.
  • Getting left on read after you tried to apologize.
  • Being unfollowed or blocked in the middle of it.

The worst part: online fights have an audience and a record. People pick sides from screenshots without hearing the whole story.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

How to fix things

Step 1: Cool down firstYou can't fix it while you're still hot.

You can't repair while you're still hot. Give it time: an hour, a night, sometimes a few days. Signs you're ready:

  • You can think about it without your heart racing.
  • You're curious about their side, not just waiting to argue.
  • You want the friendship back more than you want to win.
Step 2: A real apology has four partsSorry, but... isn't sorry.
  1. Name what you did. Specific, not vague. "I'm sorry I told everyone about your crush," not "sorry for whatever."
  2. Say how it might have affected them. "That probably felt embarrassing and like you couldn't trust me."
  3. Skip the "but." "I'm sorry, but you started it" cancels the apology. Save your side for later.
  4. Say what you'll do differently. "I won't share stuff you tell me again."

Apologizing for your part doesn't mean everything was your fault. It means you're taking care of your side of the street. Families do sorry differently, too. Some say it in words, some show it by showing up or sharing food. Both can count.

Step 3: Listen to their sideListen to understand, not to win.

After you apologize, or if you're the one who was hurt, the next move is listening. Try:

"Can you tell me what it was like from your side?"
"What would help make this better?"

Listen to understand, not to plan your comeback. You can disagree about what happened and still both feel heard.

Step 4: If you were the one who got hurtYour hurt counts too. Say it simply.
  • Say it simply. "When you ___, I felt ___." Stick to one thing, not a list of every problem.
  • Let them answer. They may not have known how it landed.
  • Forgiving isn't forgetting. You can accept an apology and still need time to trust again.
  • You don't have to accept every apology. If something keeps happening, sorry without change isn't enough.
What doesn't actually helpThese usually make the fight bigger.
📱 Fighting over text.

No tone, no faces, and screenshots. If it's a real conversation, do it in person or on a call.

👥 Recruiting a team.

Getting everyone on your side makes the fight bigger and makes it harder for anyone to back down.

📜 Bringing up everything ever.

"And another thing, in fourth grade..." Stick to what happened this time.

🙊 Acting like it never happened.

Sometimes small stuff can just pass. But skipping repair on something big usually means it comes back later.

What to do about social media right nowTake it offline. Real repair happens face to face.
📞 Move it offline.
Real repair goes better in person or on a call. Text: "can we talk tomorrow?" and stop there. If talking out loud is really hard for you, a short, careful message can work too.
🧹 Clean up what you posted.
If you posted something in the heat of it, delete it. That's part of the repair.
🚫 Don't screenshot and share.
Even if you're right. Sharing the fight makes it bigger and harder to fix.
⏳ Give "read" some grace.
No reply to an apology can mean they're still thinking. Give it a day before deciding it means no.

When it's not something to repair alone

Some situations need an adult, not just an apology:

  • Someone is threatening you or anyone else.
  • The fight is turning into a pile-on, online or at school.
  • A friend keeps hurting you and apologizing, over and over.
  • Something happened that made you feel unsafe.
  • Someone keeps putting you down for your race, religion, body, disability, or who you are.
