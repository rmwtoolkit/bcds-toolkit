Where Did the Afternoon Go?

Time can disappear, and it's not because you don't care.

What's going on

Lots of brains have trouble feeling time pass. "Later" and "now" feel the same, until later suddenly is now. Almost everyone underestimates how long things take, and some apps make time disappear on purpose.

"I have plenty of time"→Sit down for a minute→No stopping points→Later feels like now→Twenty minutes takes two hours→"It's due tomorrow"

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "I'll have time later."
  • "Just checking one thing."
  • "This will take 20 minutes."

🫀 Body

  • surprised it's already dinner
  • rushed every morning
  • panic the night before

🔁 Behaviors

  • last one ready in the morning
  • a to-do list with no times
  • looking up 45 minutes later

Where to step in

If you can see time, you can feel it.

  1. Make time visible with a timer you can see or an analog clock.
  2. Double your estimate, then block your afternoon into real time slots.
  3. Set stopping points. Decide the end before you open an app or game.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why time gets away from you

Sound familiar?Sat down for a minute. Suddenly it's dinner.
  • You got home at 3:30, sat down for "a minute," and suddenly it was dinner.
  • You thought the assignment would take 20 minutes. It took two hours.
  • You're always the last one ready in the morning, even when you got up on time.
  • "I have plenty of time" turned into "it's due tomorrow" overnight.
  • You open your phone to check one thing and look up 45 minutes later.

If time seems to slip away from you, you're not careless. Lots of brains have trouble feeling time pass.

How time gets away from you

Tap each one.

🫥Time blindness

Time doesn't feel real until it's almost gone. "Later" and "now" feel the same, until later suddenly is now.

🧮The planning fallacy

Almost everyone underestimates how long things take. Psychologists have studied this for decades. You're far from the only one.

🌀The time sinks

Phones, videos, and games make time disappear because they're designed to keep you there.

Why it's so hardEverything takes longer than you think.
Your brain's clock is still developing.
Sensing time passing and planning around it are executive skills that keep growing through your teens. Some brains, including many with ADHD, find this especially hard. That's a difference in wiring, not a flaw.
Fun time and boring time feel different.
An hour of a game feels like 10 minutes. 10 minutes of a worksheet feels like an hour. Your sense of time bends depending on what you're doing.
You plan for the best case.
When you guess how long something will take, you picture everything going smoothly, with no interruptions, no confusion, no breaks.
Every afternoon looks different.
Maybe you ride a long bus, watch a younger sibling, help with dinner or a family business, go to religious school, or switch between two homes. Plan around your real afternoon, not someone else's.
📱 The scroll that eats the afternoonNobody decides to scroll for an hour.

Endless feeds and autoplay remove the natural stopping points that would tell you time has passed. There's no end of the chapter, no last page. That's on purpose.

The worst part: you don't decide to spend an hour. You decide to spend a minute, about sixty times.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

If you can see time, you can feel it.

Tap one to see how.

⏲ Make time visible.

Use a timer you can see, an analog clock, or a visual countdown. If you can see time moving, your brain can feel it.

✖️ Double your estimate.

However long you think it'll take, plan for about twice that. If you finish early, you get free time.

🧱 Block your afternoon.

Instead of a to-do list, put tasks into actual time slots: 4:00 to 4:30 math, 4:30 to 4:45 break.

🛑 Set stopping points.

Before you open an app or game, set an alarm for when you'll stop. Decide the end before you start.

🔙 Plan backward.

Start from the due date and work back. If the project is due Friday, when does each piece need to be done?

Try itGuess it. Time it. See how close you get.

Guess how long a task will take, do it, then check. Over time, you'll learn your real numbers. Nothing here is saved, so jot your results somewhere you'll see them.

0:00

Try itSame afternoon, different plans. Which one works?

What's the best plan?

You get home at 3:30. You have math, a reading log, and soccer at 5:30. You want some downtime too.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually make time disappear faster.
🔮 "I'll have time later."

Later is when other stuff shows up too. Put it on the calendar or it probably won't happen.

🧾 To-do lists without times.

A list tells you what, not when. Your brain needs both.

📱 "Just checking one thing."

Set a timer first, or the one thing becomes ten.

😤 Getting mad at yourself for being late.

Shame doesn't fix time blindness. Tools do.

When to ask for more help

  • You're late or behind most days, even with tools.
  • Losing track of time is hurting your grades, sleep, or relationships.
  • You think something like ADHD might be part of it.

A counselor can help you sort it out. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Writers and artists have been trying to show time slipping away for a long time. One of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

The Phantom Tollbooth · Norton Juster, 1961

Milo thinks almost everything is a waste of time. He gets stuck in the Doldrums, killing time with the Lethargarians, until Tock, a watchdog with a clock in his body, gets him moving.

Momo · Michael Ende, 1973

The Men in Grey talk a whole town into "saving" its time and then steal it. A girl named Momo goes to get it back. It's the time sinks, as a fantasy.

🎬 Watch

Alice in Wonderland · Disney, 1951 Throwback

The White Rabbit is always late for a very important date and keeps checking his watch. Rated G.

🎧 Listen

Phineas and Ferb theme · Bowling for Soup, 2007 Throwback

"There's a hundred and four days of summer vacation." Then they plan every one of them. Block your afternoon like Phineas blocks his summer.

🖼️ Look

The Persistence of Memory · Salvador Dalí, 1931

Melting clocks. Ten minutes of a worksheet and an hour of a game feel like this.

✏️ Make your own

Draw today's afternoon as a clock or a column of time blocks, from when you get home to dinner. Color in what you planned. Tomorrow, color in what really happened next to it.
