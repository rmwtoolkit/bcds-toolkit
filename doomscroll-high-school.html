Why it’s so hard to stop

Most posts are fine. Every so often one is great, and you never know which pull will hit. Rewards that come sometimes keep people going longer than rewards that come every time. Slot machines run on the same idea. Infinite scroll and autoplay take away the stopping points, so you bring your own.

What the research says
  • Sleep takes the clearest hit: in about 60 of 67 studies, more screen time went with shorter sleep or later bedtimes.
  • Mood is still argued about. Heavy use is linked to more anxiety for some teens, but the average effect is small. How you feel after a scroll tells you more than any headline.
  • News: after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing, people who took in six or more hours of coverage a day reported more stress than some people who were at the finish line.
Skip these
  • Cold turkey on everythingChange the one app and time of day that cost the most.
  • “Ten more minutes, then sleep”Phone across the room, lights off.
  • Refreshing bad news for hoursCheck one source, close it, talk to someone.
Other ways in

  • ReadHow to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy Jenny Odell, 2019Odell, an artist in Oakland, argues that your attention is something companies are competing to take, and that paying attention to the place you live is a way to take it back.
  • ReadDigital Minimalism Cal Newport, 2019Newport's 30-day declutter: drop the optional apps, find out what you actually miss, then bring back only what earns its place. A long version of changing your setup instead of your willpower.
  • WatchWALL-E Pixar, 2008 ThrowbackRemember the Axiom? Every passenger floats in a chair with a screen in front of their face. When WALL-E knocks John and Mary out of their screens, they finally look up and notice each other. Rated G.
  • ListenEverything Now Arcade Fire, 2017A bright, danceable song about wanting everything, all at once, all the time. Critics heard it as a jab at constant access and consumer excess. Listen for how the loop never quite ends.
  • LookRemoved Eric Pickersgill, photo seriesPickersgill photographs people in ordinary moments, then has them hold the pose while he takes their phones away. What's left is the reach, frozen, and the people beside them being missed.
