Name the Need

Every feeling is a notification from a need.

What's going on

"Fine" covers a lot of ground. Putting a precise word to a feeling turns down the brain's fast alarm, and the word points to the need underneath. From there you can make a clear ask.

Something happens→Your body knows first→You call it "fine"→The need goes unnamed→Feeling jumps to blame→"What do you need?" Nothing comes

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "I'm fine."
  • "I feel used."
  • "Bored? Lonely? Avoiding?"

🫀 Body

  • tight chest or jaw
  • a knot in your stomach
  • off for days, no word

🔁 Behaviors

  • short with someone you care about
  • figuring out why hours later
  • scrolling to dodge the feeling

Where to step in

Feeling. Need. Request.

  1. Get granular: go one word past "stressed," and check where it sits in your body.
  2. Find the need: what am I missing right now? Rest, connection, a say in this?
  3. Make a clear, doable request. A request can get a no. If no isn't allowed, it's a demand.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why naming a feeling changes it

Sound familiar?"Fine" covers a lot of ground.
  • Someone asks how you are and you say "fine," "tired," or "stressed," no matter what's going on.
  • You were short with someone you care about and only figured out why hours later.
  • You felt off for days before you realized it was about one specific thing.
  • You're scrolling and can't tell if you're bored, lonely, or avoiding something.
  • Someone asks "What do you need?" and nothing comes.

Think of every feeling as a notification from a need. If you've worked with feeling words before, this goes further: what naming does inside your brain, why precise words predict better mental health, and how to turn a feeling into a clear ask.

What happens when you put it into words

Insula: reads your body

It tracks signals from inside you: heartbeat, gut, breathing, muscle tension. Neuroscientist Bud Craig (2009) proposed that it's where those body signals become conscious feelings. Often your body knows before you have a word.

Amygdala: reacts fast

It responds quickly to emotional events, especially threatening ones, and drives the rush you feel before you've thought anything through.

Right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex: the labeler

In a UCLA brain-imaging study, putting a word to the emotion in a face ("angry," "scared") turned down amygdala activity and turned up activity here. The two moved in opposite directions (Lieberman et al., 2007).

The researchPrecise words, steadier mind.
Labeling helps with fear, too.
People afraid of spiders who described their feelings out loud while near one ("I'm anxious, it's terrifying") had smaller physical fear responses a week later than people who distracted themselves or tried to talk themselves out of it (Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske, 2012).
Granularity is protective.
People who describe their emotions in more detail, which psychologists call emotional granularity, are less likely to binge drink, lash out, or hurt themselves when upset, and report less severe anxiety and depression (Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight, 2015).
It's a skill, not a personality trait.
Like any vocabulary, it grows with use. The word banks below are a place to start.
Feelings across culturesWhich feelings count as "good" depends on where you learned them.

In research by Stanford psychologist Jeanne Tsai and colleagues, European American participants ideally wanted to feel excited and enthusiastic more than Hong Kong Chinese participants did, who valued calm and peace more. Chinese American participants landed in between (Tsai, Knutson & Fung, 2006). Neither is better.

Families also differ on which feelings get said out loud, to whom, and in which language. Some feelings don't translate neatly. If your family has a word for a feeling that English doesn't, use it.

Needs everyone hasSeven kinds of needs. Everyone has all of them.

These categories are adapted from Nonviolent Communication. Research on self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000) points to three needs at the core of motivation and well-being: autonomy (a say in your own life), competence (feeling capable), and relatedness (connection). You'll see them below as Freedom, Meaning, and Connection.

Connection.
Belonging, acceptance, trust, support, being seen and understood.
Body.
Rest and sleep, food, water, movement, physical safety.
Honesty.
Authenticity, integrity, being the same person in every room.
Play.
Joy, humor, fun for its own sake.
Peace.
Ease, calm, order, harmony, things feeling fair.
Freedom.
Choice, independence, space, a say in your own life.
Meaning.
Purpose, growth, challenge, creativity, contributing, to matter.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

From feeling, to need, to ask

The core skill

Feeling. Need. Request.

Most conflicts skip straight from feeling to blame. Slowing down to find the need in between changes what you say next.

Get granularGo one word past "stressed." Overwhelmed? Behind? Dreading one specific thing?
Check your bodyWhere is it: chest, stomach, jaw, shoulders? Body signals often arrive before the word does.
Find the needAsk: what am I missing right now? Rest, connection, a say in this, a sense that I can handle it?
Make a requestClear and doable: "Can we talk tonight instead of over text?" A request can get a no. If no isn't allowed, it's a demand.

Try it"I feel used" is a thought in disguise.

Some "feelings" are really judgments about what someone else did. If you can add "like" or "that" after "I feel," it's usually a thought. Pick one.

"I feel used."

"I feel resentful."

"I feel like I'm not good enough."

"I feel overwhelmed."

"I feel manipulated."

Thoughts in disguise aren't wrong. They're one step away from the real feeling, and the real feeling points to the need.

A need can be met more than one wayMost needs have more than one door.
"I need them to apologize."
The need might be respect, or repair. An apology is one door. Setting a boundary, or getting support from someone else, are others.
"I need to get into this school."
The need might be competence, belonging, or a future you can be proud of. That school is one door. There are others, even when it doesn't feel that way.
"I need my parents to stop asking about my grades."
The need might be trust and a say in your own life. A weekly check-in you lead yourself might meet it better than silence would.
Try itI feel ___ because I need ___.

Think of something small from this week. Fill in the blanks.

In a groupSame story, different feelings, one need.

For advisory or class · about 10 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Read each story out loud. These are made-up students, not anyone in the room.
  2. Everyone silently picks a feeling word the character might have. Use the word bank if it helps.
  3. A few people share their word. Different words are fine. That's the point.
  4. Vote on the need underneath. Then tap to see one answer.
  5. Flip it: what would the character feel if that need were met?

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Did people pick different feeling words for the same story? Why might that be?
  • Which was harder to spot, the feeling or the need?
  • When is "fine" the right answer, and when does it cost you something?
  • How do the rules about naming feelings change between home, school, and friends?
Tip for the person leading

Keep it on the characters. Nobody should have to share their own feelings out loud. If students disagree about the need, that's a good discussion: a feeling can come from more than one need.

When you're bored, lonely, or tired, the feed is an easy way to not feel it. The need is still there when you look up.

Myths that keep you stuckWhat the research says instead.
MYTH Naming a feeling makes it bigger.

The research says the opposite. Labeling tends to turn the alarm down, not up.

MYTH Some feelings are bad.

Feelings are information. Actions can hurt people. Feelings can't.

MYTH If they cared, they'd know what I need.

Nobody can read your notifications. Saying "I need ___" gives people a real chance to help.

MYTH There's only one way to meet this need.

"It has to be this person, this way" is a trap. Most needs have several doors.

If it gets heavy

  • A red notification won't go away for weeks, or keeps getting louder.
  • You feel numb or hopeless most of the time.
  • A need for safety isn't being met, at home, online, in a relationship, or anywhere else.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be here.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult soon. You can also call or text 988, any time. See Get help below.
