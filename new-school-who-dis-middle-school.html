New School

Starting over is a lot, even when it's exciting.

What's going on

The place, the work and the people all change at once. Uncertainty keeps your brain on alert, so the first weeks feel tiring. Friend groups take many hours together. A few weeks isn't long enough yet.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why starting somewhere new feels so big

Sound familiar?New building, new faces. Nervous is normal.
  • You're starting middle school and everything feels bigger: the building, the schedule, the older kids.
  • You got lost looking for a classroom, or you're worried you will.
  • You moved here from another town, state, or country, and even the way people talk feels different.
  • Your old friends ended up in different classes, sections, or lunch periods.
  • You don't know where to sit at lunch yet.
  • You watched stories of other people's first week and it looked like they already had a group.
  • You're excited and nervous at the same time, and it's hard to tell which one is winning.

Starting somewhere new is one of the biggest changes you'll go through, and almost everyone around you is feeling some version of it.

What changes all at once

Tap each one.

🗺The place

New building, new routines, new rules. Your brain is working overtime just to figure out where to go and when. Loud halls and crowded lunchrooms can be extra tiring if noise or crowds wear you out.

📚The work

More teachers, more homework, lockers, and switching classes. More to keep track of.

👥The people

Friend groups reshuffle. People you knew may change, and lots of people you don't know yet.

Why it feels so bigYou see their outsides. You feel your insides.
Uncertainty is tiring.
When you don't know what to expect, your brain stays on alert. That's why the first weeks can feel exhausting even if nothing goes wrong.
It looks easier for everyone else.
You see other people's confident outsides. You feel your own nervous insides.
Friend groups take time.
Research on friendship suggests it takes many hours together before someone feels like a real friend. A few weeks isn't long enough yet.
Old things end while new things start.
You can miss your old school, teachers, or routine even if you're glad to be somewhere new.
📱 The first weeks onlineFriend groups aren't locked in. Not even close.

Group chats form fast. Stories show people who seem to have found their group on day two. Snap Map shows clusters of classmates hanging out. Online, the first weeks can look settled when they really aren't.

The worst part: seeing a new group chat or a photo of people hanging out can make you feel like friendships are already locked in. They aren't. Groups keep shifting all year.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

Small moves add up fast.

Tap one to see how.

🗺 Learn the place.

Walk your schedule, find the bathrooms, figure out your locker or cubby. Less to figure out means less stress.

🙋 Say yes to small things.

Join one club, team, or activity, like an affinity group or GSA if your school has one. Shared activities are one of the easiest ways friendships start.

👋 Use simple openers.

"Is this seat taken?" "Did you get what we're supposed to do for homework?" "What lunch do you have?" Small talk is how big talk starts.

🧑‍🏫 Find one adult.

Pick one teacher, advisor, coach, or counselor you feel okay with. Knowing one adult makes a whole building feel smaller.

⏳ Give it time.

Most people take a few months to feel settled. Feeling unsure in September doesn't mean you'll feel that way in December.

🔗 Keep some old connections.

You can make new friends and keep old ones. It's not either or.

Try itPick a few small things. Start tomorrow.

Pick a few small things to try. Small is the point. Nothing is saved.

Try itLunch, old friends, new faces. What would you try?

Pick the move you'd try.

It's the first day of lunch. You don't see anyone you know, and every table looks full.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese make the first weeks harder.
📱 Comparing your first week to everyone's stories.

Stories show the highlights, not the awkward lunch tables.

🙈 Waiting for someone to come to you.

Sometimes they will. More often, it helps to make the first small move.

🏃 Deciding in week two how the whole year will go.

It's way too early to know.

🤐 Not telling anyone it's hard.

Adults at school expect this. Telling one of them is normal, not dramatic.

When to get more help

  • You dread school most mornings or feel sick before going.
  • After a couple of months, you still feel alone most of the time.
  • Someone is being unkind to you or excluding you on purpose.
  • Your sleep, eating, or mood has changed a lot.

A counselor helps new students all the time. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Almost everyone has been the new kid. A few of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

Wemberly Worried · Kevin Henkes, 2000 Throwback

Wemberly worries about everything, and the first day of school most of all. Then her teacher introduces her to Jewel, who's worried too.

Other Words for Home · Jasmine Warga, 2019

Jude moves from Syria to Cincinnati, starts a new school, and finds friends in her ESL class. A Newbery Honor book written as poems.

Heads up: Jude worries about family still in Syria during the war, and she faces anti-Muslim treatment.

🎬 Watch

Inside Out · Pixar, 2015 Throwback

Riley moves to San Francisco, and her first day at the new school goes badly. Watch how long it takes her to feel at home, and what helps. Rated PG.

🎧 Listen

Home · Phillip Phillips, 2012

Co-writer Greg Holden said it's about letting someone know you're there for them. Good for anyone still finding their spot.

🖼️ Look

Awkward · Svetlana Chmakova, 2015

Peppi's first day at Berrybrook Middle goes badly, and she pushes away the one kid who helps her. She finds her people in the art club. A graphic novel.

Heads up: Some kids get teased at school.

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
  • Tell a counselor, teacher, coach, parent or caregiver, or another adult you trust today. You can show them this screen.
  • If you might act on thoughts of hurting yourself, call 911, go to an emergency room, or call 988 and ask for a mobile crisis team.
  • LGBTQ+? The Trevor Project: call 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678-678, any time.
