Part 1 · Understand it

What's actually happening in your body at a party

Sound familiar?New place, new people, your call.
  • Next fall you could be at a party where you know almost nobody, with no parent down the hall and no curfew.
  • Someone hands you a cup and you have no idea what's in it, or how much.
  • You've watched a friend go from fun to sloppy faster than seemed possible.
  • You don't plan to drink much, or at all, and wonder whether that will make college lonely.

This lesson won't tell you what to choose. Families, faiths, and cultures see alcohol very differently, and you'll bring your own values with you. It's about understanding what alcohol and other drugs do, so you can keep yourself and the people around you safe.

The curve that catches people

Alcohol moves from your stomach and small intestine into your blood over time. So your blood alcohol concentration (BAC) can keep climbing after your last drink, and even after someone passes out. The government's alcohol research institute, NIAAA, puts that front and center in its overdose warnings.

Rising: you feel fine, then you don't

Early drinks are still being absorbed while you pour the next one. By the time you feel it, more is already on the way.

Peak: after you stop

The highest point usually comes after the last drink. That's why "they seemed okay when they went to bed" is such a dangerous sentence.

Falling: on your liver's schedule

Your liver clears alcohol at a fairly steady pace. Coffee, a cold shower, or a greasy meal afterward don't speed it up. Only time does.

One drink is smaller than the cup

A U.S. standard drink is 14 grams of alcohol. That's about:

12 ozbeer at about 5%
5 ozwine at about 12%
1.5 ozliquor at about 40%

When Duke researchers had college students free-pour drinks, the students poured more than a standard drink every time, and the bigger the cup, the bigger the overpour. A party cup of punch can hold several drinks.

The researchThe numbers students get wrong.
Everyone isn't drinking that much.
Across dozens of studies, college students consistently overestimate how much their peers drink and how fine their peers are with heavy drinking. Plenty of students drink lightly or not at all.
Blackouts are common.
In a survey of 772 Duke undergraduates, 51% of students who had ever had alcohol reported at least one blackout, and 40% had one in the past year. During a blackout, people still walk, talk, text, and make decisions they won't remember.
Energy drinks hide it.
In a 2008 study, college students who mixed alcohol with energy drinks reported more alcohol-related injuries and other serious consequences than students who drank alcohol alone. You feel less drunk than you are.
The worst outcomes are mostly accidents.
Researchers estimated that about 1,825 U.S. college students aged 18 to 24 died from alcohol-related unintentional injuries in 2005, most of them in traffic crashes.
Pills, powders, ediblesIf it's not from a pharmacy, you don't know.
Fake pills.
The DEA has warned that counterfeit pills pressed to look like Xanax, Percocet, oxycodone, or Adderall often contain fentanyl or methamphetamine. In DEA lab testing, two in five fake pills with fentanyl held a potentially lethal dose. Your friend can't see fentanyl either.
Mixing slows breathing.
Alcohol plus opioids, benzodiazepines like Xanax, or sleep medicine each slow breathing, and together they hit much harder.
Edibles are slow.
They take much longer to kick in than smoking. Taking more because "it isn't working" is the classic mistake.
Testing and Narcan.
Many campuses and health departments give out fentanyl test strips and naloxone (Narcan) for free. Narcan has been sold over the counter since 2023. It reverses an opioid overdose and won't harm someone who isn't having one.
Alcohol poisoningNobody should "sleep it off" alone.

Alcohol poisoning is when there's enough alcohol in the blood that the brain areas running breathing, heart rate, and temperature start to shut down. Warning signs include confusion, trouble staying awake, vomiting, seizures, slow or irregular breathing, clammy or bluish skin, and no gag reflex.

Because BAC can keep rising while someone is unconscious, putting them to bed and walking away is the most dangerous thing you can do.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Staying safe, whatever you choose

If you choose to drink

Decide the night before the night decides for you.

Set your number soberA limit you pick in your room is much easier to keep than one you pick at midnight.
Eat first, water betweenFood slows absorption. A water between drinks paces you without anyone noticing.
Pour your own, keep it with youYou know what's in it. If you set it down and lose track, get a new one.
Don't mixNo pills, no energy drinks, and be careful with weed on top. Each one changes the curve.
If someone is in trouble

Know CUPS. Call every time.

Call 911 or campus safety if you see any of these: Cold, clammy, pale, or bluish skin. Unresponsive, can't be woken. Puking while passed out. Slow or irregular breathing.

Then, step by step:

1 Roll them on their side.

The recovery position keeps the airway clear if they vomit. Never leave someone on their back.

2 Stay with them.

Until help arrives. If you have to leave, hand off to a specific person.

3 Give Narcan if pills or powder might be involved.

Blue lips, tiny pupils, or gurgling breathing after a pill or powder can mean an opioid overdose. One spray in a nostril. It can't hurt them if it isn't.

4 Don't let fear of trouble stop you.

Massachusetts' Good Samaritan law protects people who seek help for a drug overdose, and the person overdosing, from possession charges. For alcohol, most colleges have a medical amnesty policy. Look up your school's before you need it.

MythsWhat the research says instead.
MYTH Coffee or a shower sobers you up.

You end up awake and drunk. Only time lowers BAC.

MYTH They'll be fine if they sleep it off.

BAC can keep climbing while they sleep. That's when choking and stopped breathing happen.

MYTH I feel fine, so I'm fine to drive.

Feeling fine and being under the limit are different things, and you may not have peaked yet.

MYTH Everyone drinks a lot in college.

Third Friday of the semester. Coleen has had two drinks, already more than she's used to, and a guy from her orientation group keeps refilling her cup without asking. An hour later she feels much drunker than expected. Her roommate is asleep, and she doesn't know anyone well enough to say "I need to leave."

Across the room, Sarah's friend Malik has been drinking since before the party. He's slurring, and he gets annoyed when anyone slows him down. Then he has to grab the stair railing with both hands.

  1. What would you do in each situation?
  2. What makes it harder at college than at home?
  3. Who or what resources would help?
Moves that help

Coleen can switch to water, use safe-ride or safe-walk, video-call someone she trusts, or ask an RA for help getting home. Anyone who notices the refills can step in: "Want to grab some water with me?" Sarah doesn't need to win an argument. Water, a seat, a friend he listens to, and a safe way home. Any CUPS sign means calling 911 or campus safety.

Tip for the person leading

Encourage everyone to see themselves as a helper, guys included. Students who don't drink are often the most useful people at a party. Know your school's medical amnesty language if students ask.

Call 911 or campus safety right away if someone

  • Can't be woken, or has slow or irregular breathing.
  • Has cold, clammy, bluish skin, or vomits while passed out.
  • Has blue lips or tiny pupils after taking a pill or powder. Give Narcan.
  • Has a seizure or chest pain.

Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222, any time. If a friend is in emotional crisis rather than a medical one, call or text 988. See Get help below.
