What's going on

Your teen years are when you start working out who you are: what you like, what you believe, where you come from, and who you want to be. Trying things on is part of it.

Part 1 · Understand it

The big question of your teen years

Sound familiar?"Who am I, anyway?"
  • You loved something last year, and now it feels kind of babyish.
  • You act one way with your family, another with friends, and another online.
  • Someone said, "You've changed," and you're not sure if that's bad.
  • Your family has strong ideas about who you should be.
  • You're one of the only kids in your grade who shares your background, faith, or home language.
  • You've wondered about your gender, or who you like, and haven't said it out loud.
  • Everyone else seems to have a "thing," and you're still looking for yours.

If any of that fits, good news: you're right on schedule. Wondering who you are is the main job of being a teenager.

🧠 Why now?Your brain and your world are both changing.

A psychologist named Erik Erikson noticed that the teen years have one big task: figuring out who you are and where you fit. Your brain is getting better at big, abstract questions, your body is changing, and your friends matter more than ever. So the question "who am I?" shows up loud.

Think of it like a building with scaffolding up. It's supposed to look unfinished right now.

Four places you might be right now

Researchers who study identity describe a few common places people land. You can be in different places for different parts of yourself, and you'll move around.

💤Not thinking about it yet"I don't really know, and I haven't wondered much."
📦Going with what I was handed"This is what my family or friends do, so it's me."
🧪Trying things on"I'm exploring and not sure yet."
🧭Feels like me, for now"I've looked around, and this fits."

Trying things on is a healthy place to be. Most people who settle into a sense of themselves got there by exploring first.

The pieces of youYou're made of a lot of parts.
  • 🌍 Race and ethnicity
  • 🏠 Family and culture
  • 🗣 Language
  • ⚧️ Gender
  • 🕊 Faith or beliefs
  • 🎨 Interests
  • 🧭 Values
  • 💗 Who you like
  • 🦽 Body and ability
  • 📱 Online you

Some pieces you were born with or handed by your family. Some you choose. Some get louder at different times. Which ones feel biggest for you right now?

Race, culture, and where you come fromYour roots are part of the story.

For some kids, race and ethnicity come up every day, often because other people keep pointing it out. For others, especially white kids in mostly white places, it might not have come up much yet. Everyone has a background, and it's worth knowing yours.

Researchers looked at 46 studies of kids and teens of color. The ones who felt good about their racial or ethnic group tended to have better self-esteem, fewer sad and worried feelings, and an easier time at school.

Worth asking the adults who raise you: Where does our family come from? What traditions matter to us? What do you hope I keep?

GenderYou get to figure it out at your own pace.

Gender is part of identity too. Some kids feel sure about their gender from early on. Some wonder about it. Some realize they're transgender or nonbinary. Lots of kids also feel squeezed by rules about how a "real" boy or girl should act: what to wear, what to like, whether you're allowed to cry.

You don't owe anyone a label or an announcement. Who you tell, and when, is up to you.

If you want to talk it through, a counselor is a good place to start. The Trevor Project (in Get help below) is there for LGBTQ+ young people any time.

A different you in different placesThat doesn't make you fake.

Almost everybody acts a little different at home, at school, with friends, and online. Lots of kids switch how they talk depending on who's around, especially if home and school feel like two different worlds. That's a skill.

It gets heavy when there's nowhere you can be all of you. Everyone deserves at least one place like that.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Building you, one piece at a time

What actually helps

Try it on. Keep what's true.

Tap one to see how.

🧪 Try things that are easy to undo.

A club, an instrument, a sport, a new kind of music, a different haircut. If it doesn't fit, you can let it go.

🔎 Notice how it feels from the inside.

Do you feel more like yourself, or like you're playing a part? That feeling is good information.

👵 Ask about your roots.

Ask a parent, grandparent, or other relative about where your family comes from and what they were like at your age. You'll hear stories you didn't know.

🏡 Find one place to be all of you.

A friend, a team, a club, a cousin, a group at your place of worship. One is enough to start.

🔄 Let yourself change your mind.

Liking something new doesn't make the old you fake. It means you grew. Let your friends change too.

🧑‍🏫 Talk to an adult you trust.

A counselor, coach, teacher, or relative. You don't have to have it figured out to talk about it.

Try itThree moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd actually do.

You joined the art club. A friend says, "Since when are you an art kid? You're trying way too hard."

Your family speaks another language at home. A kid at school makes fun of your mom's accent at pickup.

Everyone in your group is posting "which aesthetic are you?" and you don't feel like any of them.

There's no one right answer. The best move is the one that lets you stay you.

👥 In a groupPick a corner. Then see if your guess was right.

👥 For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move or point to a corner of the room: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. No one has to share.
  3. After each one, the leader taps it to show what research says.
👥 Four corners · 1 of 2"By the end of middle school, you should know who you are."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not even close. Studies that follow people over time find identity keeps developing through high school, college, and beyond. Exploring now is right on time.

👥 Four corners · 2 of 2"If you act different with different people, you're being fake."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Usually not. Almost everyone adjusts to the room. It becomes a problem when you have to hide something important about yourself everywhere you go.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • What's something you used to love that you've outgrown? What took its place?
  • What's a tradition from your family or culture you'd want to keep when you're grown up?
  • Where do you feel most like yourself?
Tip for the person leading

Let students choose what they share. Identity can be private, especially around gender, sexuality, faith, or immigration. Never ask anyone to speak for their whole group.

When it's more than figuring things out

  • Someone is teasing or threatening you because of your race, religion, gender, or who you like.
  • You're being outed, or someone is sharing private things about you.
  • You feel like you have to hide who you are everywhere, and it's wearing you down.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult today. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night.

Jordan starts at a fancy private school where he's one of the only kids of color, and he feels like a different person in each of his two worlds. The first graphic novel to win the Newbery Medal.

A new girl at school doesn't try to fit in at all. Everyone has to decide what they think about that, including the boy telling the story.
