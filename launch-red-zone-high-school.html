The Red Zone

No network yet means no safety net yet.

What's going on

Researchers call the stretch from move-in to Thanksgiving the red zone. New people, heavy drinking, and no friends who notice yet are the conditions. The only person responsible for an assault is the person who commits it.

Move-in, everything new→No one knows you yet→Big party nights→Crowds and heavy drinking→Everyone waits for someone else

How you'll know

⚠️ At a party

  • someone who can barely stand
  • a stranger refilling their cup
  • steering them toward the stairs

💭 Myths

  • "It's not my business."
  • "I need to be sure first."
  • "Someone else will step in."

🧭 Your safety net

  • your 2 a.m. person
  • shared locations and a code word
  • arrive together, leave together

Where to step in

Decide ahead of time you're the someone.

  1. Use the four Ds: direct, distract, delegate, or delay and check in after.
  2. Arrive together, leave together. Share locations and a code word that means "come get me."
  3. If a friend tells you: "I believe you. It's not your fault. What do you need?"

If you or someone else is in danger, call 911 or campus safety. RAINN: 800-656-4673, or chat at rainn.org, 24/7, confidential.

Part 1 · Understand it

What the research shows

Before we startTake care of yourself as you read.

This lesson talks about sexual assault. Some people reading it have already experienced it, at home, at school, in a relationship, or somewhere else. Skip any part you need to, take breaks, and come back later if you want. The Get help section is at the bottom, and a counselor is available to talk.

If it ever happened to you, it was not your fault. Not because of what you drank, wore, said, or didn't say.

The red zone

First-years, first months

Researchers call the stretch from move-in to Thanksgiving the red zone. In one campus study, first-year women were at higher risk of unwanted sex than second-year women, especially early in the fall (Kimble and colleagues, 2008). Other studies are more mixed on exact timing, so treat it as a warning about new situations, not a calendar.

Big party days

An economics study of Division 1 schools found that reports of rape involving victims aged 17 to 24 rose 28% on football game days (Lindo, Siminski and Swensen, 2018). The game isn't the cause. Crowds, heavy drinking, and unfamiliar places are the conditions.

Mostly someone they know

In a national study of college women, about 9 in 10 victims of rape or attempted rape knew the person who did it: a classmate, a friend, a date, someone from the floor (Fisher, Cullen and Turner, 2000). "Stranger danger" misses most of the risk.

The numbersCommon, underreported, and not only women.

Sources: AAU Campus Climate Survey (2019), 33 universities and about 180,000 students; Bureau of Justice Statistics (Sinozich and Langton, 2014). Survey numbers depend on how questions are asked, which is why different studies give different figures.

Alcohol is a risk factor, never the causeIce makes falls likelier. The pusher is still at fault.

Think of icy sidewalks. Ice makes falls more likely, and people still fall on dry sidewalks. Clearing the ice helps. But if someone pushes you, the person who pushed you is always at fault.

A review of college research found that at least half of sexual assaults involve alcohol, used by the person who commits the assault, the victim, or both (Abbey, 2002). Being drunk never makes an assault the survivor's fault, and someone who is incapacitated can't consent.

The only person responsible for an assault is the person who commits it.

Why the first months are riskiestNo network yet means no safety net yet.
No one notices yet.
Without a friend group that knows you, there may be no one who sees you leave with someone or notices you didn't come home.
Everything is unfamiliar.
New people, new buildings, no sense yet of which parties are safe or how to get home. That's part of the excitement, and it's what makes situations harder to read.
People who commit assault use this.
Isolation, heavy drinking, and newness are conditions someone can take advantage of. That's on them, not on the person they target.
Who do you call at 2 a.m.?
Someone at school who would pick up, walk over, or notice you didn't come back. If you don't have that person yet, that's the first thing to build.
Part 2 · Practice the skills

Stepping in and looking out

Why nobody steps in

Everyone assumes someone else will.

In a classic experiment, people who believed they were the only one hearing someone have a seizure were much more likely to go for help than people who believed others could hear it too (Darley and Latané, 1968). Responsibility spreads thin in a crowd. A party is a crowd.

The fix is deciding ahead of time that you're the someone. Four ways to do it:

DirectSay something. "Hey, are you okay?" "She's coming with us." Polite can wait.
DistractInterrupt without a confrontation. "Your Uber's here." Spill a drink. Ask for help finding the bathroom.
DelegateGet someone with more pull: their friends, the host, an RA, campus safety, 911.
DelayIf you couldn't act in the moment, check in after. "I saw what happened last night. Are you okay?"
ResearchTraining helps people act. In a survey of about 2,500 undergraduates at one university, students who'd had active bystander training through the Green Dot program reported doing more to step in, and held fewer myths that blame victims, than students without training (Coker and colleagues, 2011).
Looking out for your peopleArrive together. Leave together.
Agree on the plan before you go.
Who you're arriving with, when you'll check in, and that nobody leaves alone or gets left, even if they say they're fine.
Share locations and a code word.
A text that means "come get me," no questions asked, then or later.
Trust the off feeling.
You don't need proof to check on someone. Being a little awkward is a small price.
Guys have extra pull.
When a friend makes a comment about getting someone drunk, another guy saying "Not cool, man" often lands harder than anything else.
Consent is clear, ongoing, and sober enough.
Someone who is very drunk, high, or asleep can't consent. See Consent, Clearly.
If it happens to you or a friendIt's not your fault. Every choice after is yours.
Get somewhere safe.
A friend's room, a public place, campus safety, or 911 if you're in danger.
Medical care is worth it, even if you never report.
A hospital can treat injuries, help prevent pregnancy and infections, and collect evidence. Evidence collection usually needs to happen within about five days (in Massachusetts, 120 hours, at any hospital). In Massachusetts, you can have the exam without reporting to police. The kit is stored, and you shouldn't get a bill.
Know which people are confidential.
At most colleges, counselors, health center staff, and sexual assault advocates are confidential. Many other staff, often including RAs, have to pass what you tell them to the Title IX office, which then reaches out with options. Check your school's policy so you choose who to tell.
You decide about reporting.
Campus advocates, the Title IX office, and RAINN (800-656-4673, or chat at rainn.org) can walk you through options. Many colleges have amnesty policies so students who report aren't disciplined for drinking. Reporting now, later, or never are all allowed.
If a friend tells you.
Believe them. "I'm so sorry. It's not your fault. What do you need?" Don't push them to report, don't investigate, and don't share it. Help them reach the people they choose.
In a groupTwo nights. What would you do?

Story 1 of 2Mateo at the party

It's the Saturday of the first home game. Mateo is at an off-campus party with guys from his floor. He notices a first-year he recognizes from orientation, Sienna, who can barely stand. A junior Mateo doesn't know keeps refilling her cup and is steering her toward the stairs. Her friends aren't around. One of Mateo's floormates laughs and says, "He's doing well tonight." Nobody else seems to be paying attention.

  1. What would you do if you were Mateo?
  2. What makes stepping in hard here?
  3. Which of the four Ds fits best?
Moves that help

Any of the Ds works. Direct: "Hey, Sienna, I think your friends are looking for you. Come get some water." Distract: block the stairs with a question for the junior. Delegate: tell the host, find someone who knows her, or call campus safety. He can also answer his floormate: "Not funny. She's wasted." Anyone can be targeted, and the bystander who acts may be the only one who does.

Story 2 of 2Priya the next morning

Priya's roommate, Jordan, comes back at 9 a.m. and gets into bed without talking. Later Jordan says, "I think something happened last night. I was really drunk. I don't remember most of it. It's probably my fault for going back to his room." Jordan doesn't want to call it anything and keeps saying, "Please don't make this a big deal."

  1. What should Priya say first?
  2. What should she avoid saying?
  3. How can she help without taking over?
Moves that help

First: "I believe you. It's not your fault, no matter how much you drank or where you were." She can let Jordan set the pace and mention options without pushing: medical care within about five days, a confidential counselor or advocate, RAINN. She avoids questions that sound like blame ("Why did you go to his room?"). She doesn't tell anyone else. If Jordan seems in danger or talks about self-harm, she gets help right away.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Does Mateo's choice change if the junior is on a team or in a fraternity he hopes to join?
  • What if the genders were different, or everyone was the same gender?
  • How do your family, culture, or faith talk about sex and assault? How might that affect who a survivor tells?
Tip for the person leading

Say before you start that some people in the room may have experienced assault, and that anyone can step out. Keep the frame on bystander action and on what the person who commits the assault chose. Never discuss what a victim should have done. Make sure a counselor is available afterward.

What doesn't helpThese usually make it worse.
MYTH It's not my business.

That's the crowd effect talking. If you noticed, it's your business.

MYTH Why was she drinking, though?

Questions about drinking, clothes, or whose room it was put the blame on the wrong person and stop survivors from getting help.

MYTH I need to be sure first.

Checking in doesn't require proof. If you're wrong, someone got a glass of water.

MYTH "I'm fine, go" settles it.

From someone very drunk, it isn't the final word. Leave together.

Get help right away

  • If you or someone else is in danger, call 911 or campus safety.
  • National Sexual Assault Hotline (RAINN): 800-656-4673, or chat at rainn.org, 24/7, confidential.
  • Call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741, if you're struggling.
