Part 1 · Understand it

What growing up around addiction does, and doesn't, decide

Sound familiar?You've been handling more than most people know.
  • You've had to decide whether to get in a car with a parent who'd been drinking.
  • You're the one who knows which bills are late, or who gets your younger siblings fed and to school.
  • You think about college or moving out partly as a way out, and feel guilty for thinking it.
  • A parent is in recovery, and on every rough day you're scanning for signs of a slip.
  • You've noticed you drink or use to get through weekends, and that scares you a little.

"You didn't cause it, can't cure it, can't control it" still holds. This goes further: what's happening in your body, what research says about how kids in your spot turn out, and what support can look like on your terms.

A stress system that never gets to stand down

Unpredictability is its own kind of stress. When you never know what you'll walk into, your body keeps running a stress chain that's built for short emergencies.

Amygdala: the threat detector

It learns the warning signs at home, a tone of voice, a car door, a certain kind of quiet, and reacts before you've decided anything.

Hypothalamus and pituitary: start the chain

The hypothalamus takes the alarm and signals the pituitary gland just below it. The pituitary sends a hormone through your blood to your adrenal glands.

Adrenal glands: release cortisol

Cortisol gets you ready for an emergency: faster heart, sharper alert, sleep and appetite pushed aside. Useful for an hour. Exhausting for months.

Hippocampus: "you're safe now"

Memory and context. It helps tell the alarm that this place, this moment, is okay. Long-running stress makes that signal weaker.

Prefrontal cortex: focus and calming down

Planning, concentrating, talking yourself down. When the alarm keeps running, it gets less bandwidth. That's why an essay at 11pm after a fight can feel impossible.

Pediatricians call stress that's severe, long-lasting, and not buffered by support toxic stress. A 2012 American Academy of Pediatrics report also named what buffers it best: steady, supportive relationships with adults.

The researchThe risk is real. The outcome isn't fixed.
You're far from the only one.
A 2025 study in JAMA Pediatrics estimated that about 19 million U.S. kids, roughly 1 in 4, live with a parent who has a substance use disorder. Alcohol was the most common.
Genes are about half the story.
Pooling twin and adoption studies, researchers in 2015 estimated alcohol use disorder is about 50% heritable. That's a real reason to be careful with alcohol and drugs. It also means the other half comes from environment, stress, and experience, and some of that is in your hands.
Most kids in this spot do okay.
A study that followed children of parents with alcoholism in Kauai, Hawaii, from birth found that most had not developed serious problems by 18. In the follow-up into adulthood, the ones who did well had leaned on more sources of support growing up: extended family, neighbors, and school.
Why it's so confusingLove, anger, loyalty, relief. All at once.
The unspoken rules.
Families living with addiction often run on three rules nobody says out loud: don't talk, don't trust, don't feel. They protect the secret, and they leave kids alone with it.
Helping, or carrying too much?
In many families and cultures, teens helping run the household is normal and a source of pride. Psychologists use the word parentification when it tips over: when it's constant, when nobody looks after you in return, or when it costs you sleep, school, or friends.
Recovery isn't a straight line.
Relapse is common in recovery from addiction, as it is in other long-term illnesses. A slip doesn't mean recovery failed, and it doesn't mean you missed something you should have caught.
Roles you might recognize.
The one with perfect grades so nobody worries. The caretaker. The one who cracks jokes to cut the tension. The one who stays in their room and needs nothing. They're ways of coping. They can also become a box.
Looking aheadWanting your own life isn't betrayal.

Thinking about college, a job, or moving out can come with guilt, especially if you've been holding things together at home. Some families expect kids to stay close, and that can be a real and good choice. The question is whether you're choosing it, or whether guilt is choosing for you.

If a parent can't help with applications or financial forms, tell your counselor early. Schools see this more than you'd think, and there are options.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Support on your own terms

Before you tell an adult

What happens when you talk to a counselor.

In Massachusetts, school counselors, teachers, and nurses are mandated reporters. If they have reason to believe a young person is being abused or neglected, the law requires them to report it to the Department of Children and Families (DCF). Telling a counselor that a parent drinks is not the same thing as a report. The question is whether you are safe and cared for.

You can ask first: "If I tell you something, what would you have to share, and with whom?" A good counselor will give you a straight answer.

Some families see telling an outsider as disloyal, or have real reasons to distrust agencies, including families with immigration worries or past bad experiences with systems. Those concerns make sense. A counselor can talk through options with you, and you can decide how much to share and when.

What actually helps

Six moves, in your own order.

Tap each one.

1 Pick one adult.

A counselor, coach, aunt, grandparent, faith leader, or a friend's parent. "Things are hard at home" is a complete first sentence.

2 Have a ride rule.

Never ride with someone who's been drinking or using, and don't let younger siblings either. Save two backup numbers now, before you need them.

3 Know the overdose basics.

If someone can't be woken or is breathing slowly, call 911, roll them on their side, and give naloxone (Narcan) if opioids could be involved. Narcan has been sold without a prescription since 2023. Massachusetts' Good Samaritan law protects the caller and the person overdosing from drug possession charges.

4 Find people who get it.

Alateen, part of Al-Anon, runs free meetings in person and online for teens affected by someone else's drinking. NACoA has resources for kids of parents with any addiction.

5 Guard what's yours.

Sleep, a team, a job, a friend's kitchen, a place to study. Those buffer stress. Protecting them isn't selfish.

6 Watch your own use.

With family history, the risk that drinking or using to cope becomes a pattern is higher. If you notice that happening, say it to someone. That's a strength, not a confession.

MythsWhat's actually true.
MYTH If I were easier to live with, they'd stop.

Addiction is a health condition that changes how the brain handles craving and choice. Perfect grades and a clean room don't touch it.

MYTH It only counts if someone gets violent.

Unpredictability, broken promises, and doing adult jobs at 15 are hard on their own. You don't need a worse story to deserve support.

MYTH I'm going to turn out like them.

Your risk is higher, and it isn't destiny. Most kids in long-term studies did well, especially the ones with more people in their corner.

MYTH Hiding bottles or pouring things out will help.

It usually leads to fights and doesn't change the drinking. Your energy is better spent on your own support.

For advisory or classWhat would help Jordan?

About 15 minutes. Read the story out loud. It's about Jordan, not anyone in the room. Talk in small groups, then share one idea each.

Jordan, a junior, has started skipping lunch to finish homework, works most nights, and hasn't had anyone over since freshman year. When a friend jokes about it, Jordan snaps. Jordan's mom drinks most nights, and Jordan has been covering for her with the school.

  1. What might Jordan be feeling, and afraid of?
  2. If Jordan finally tells a friend, what would help them hear? What should they skip?
  3. Jordan asks the friend to keep it secret. When should the friend tell an adult anyway?
Ideas to add

Exhausted, embarrassed, protective of their mom, scared of what happens if anyone finds out. Helpful: "Thanks for telling me." "That sounds like a lot." "Want to talk to a counselor together?" Skip "Just tell her to stop." If Jordan seems unsafe, is being hurt, or talks about hurting themselves, telling a trusted adult is the caring move, even if Jordan is upset at first.

Tip for the person leading

Assume some students in the room live with this. Keep it about Jordan, never ask anyone to share about their own family, and say before you start that a counselor is available afterward. If a student discloses, thank them privately and follow your school's procedures.

Get help right away if

  • Someone at home hurts you, threatens you, or you feel unsafe.
  • A parent can't be woken up, has trouble breathing, or may have overdosed. Call 911 and give Narcan if you have it.
  • You don't have food, heat, or a sober adult to take care of you.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Call 911 in an emergency. The Childhelp hotline is 1-800-422-4453, any time. Call or text 988 if you're struggling, or go to a trusted adult or an emergency room. See Get help below.

Other ways in

The research says steady adults and a wider circle make the difference. These stories show what that looks like.

Matilda's parents ignore her. Her teacher, Miss Honey, doesn't. The Kauai study found kids did better when they had support outside the house. Miss Honey is that support.

Heads up: Matilda's parents are neglectful and cruel to her, played mostly for laughs.
