Figuring out who you are is the job right now.

What's going on

Adolescence is when you start building an identity: your values, your roots, your gender, your beliefs, what you want. Exploring is the healthy part, and most people keep changing well into adulthood.

You try things on→Some fit, some don't→You notice what feels true→You keep the good parts→A sense of you grows

Part 1 · Understand it

Who am I, and who decides?

Sound familiar?Everyone asks what you want to be. Nobody asks who.
  • Your family has a plan for you: a college, a career, a faith, a way to act. You're not sure it's yours.
  • You're a different person at home, at school, on your team, and online, and you wonder which one is real.
  • You're one of few students here who share your race, your culture, your faith, or your home language.
  • Someone asked, "No, where are you really from?"
  • You've been questioning your gender or your sexuality, and you're figuring out who, if anyone, to tell.
  • You outgrew a friend group, or it outgrew you.
  • College essays want you to explain who you are in 650 words, and you're still working on it.

All of that is the work. Psychologist Erik Erikson called it the central task of adolescence: building a sense of who you are and where you fit. The scaffolding is supposed to be up right now.

Explore, then commit, then keep going

Psychologist James Marcia looked at two questions: Have you explored your options? Have you committed to something? The answers make four common places to be. You can be in a different place for each part of yourself.

💤DiffusionNot exploring, not committed. "I haven't really thought about it."
📦ForeclosureCommitted without exploring. "It's what I was handed."
🧪MoratoriumExploring, not committed yet. "I'm trying things on."
🧭AchievementExplored, then committed. "I looked, and this fits me."

Based on Marcia (1966). A commitment you picked after exploring can look just like the one you were handed. The difference is that you chose it.

Most people move forward. A review of 73 studies found that as people go from their teens into their 20s, the most common change is from exploring to a commitment that fits, and moving forward is about twice as common as sliding back.

The pieces of youSome you were handed. Some you choose.
  • 🌍 Race and ethnicity
  • 🏠 Family and culture
  • 🗣 Language
  • ⚧️ Gender
  • 💗 Sexuality
  • 🕊 Faith or beliefs
  • 🧭 Values
  • 🎨 Interests and talents
  • 🦽 Body and ability
  • 💼 Class and money
  • 🗳 Politics
  • 📱 Online self

Which pieces feel biggest right now? Often it's the one other people notice most, or the one you're questioning.

Race, ethnicity, and rootsKnowing where you come from helps.

Students of color often start thinking about racial identity early, because the world brings it up for them. White students, especially in mostly white places, may not think about it until later. Everyone has a racial and cultural story, and everyone has some work to do on it.

A 2014 review of 46 studies found that minority youth who felt good about their ethnic or racial group had fewer symptoms of depression, better self-esteem, fewer problems with drugs and alcohol, and did better in school. Exploring your background and feeling proud of it is protective.

If you're mixed, adopted, or the child of immigrants, you may get "pick a side" questions. You don't have to. Holding more than one background is an identity too.

Gender and sexualityYour timeline. Your words.

For some people, gender and sexuality feel settled early. For others, high school is when the questions get loud. Some students come to know they're gay, bi, queer, trans, nonbinary, or something they're still finding words for. Others question for a while and land where they started. All of that is real identity work.

Everyone also deals with gender scripts: guys told to be tough and not too close with friends, girls told to be nice before honest. You can keep the parts that fit and drop the rest.

You don't owe anyone a label or a coming-out. Who you tell, and when, is your call, and safety comes first. A counselor is a good place to think out loud, and The Trevor Project (in Get help below) is there any time for LGBTQ+ young people.

Code switchingDifferent rooms, different versions of you.

Code switching means changing how you talk, dress, or act to fit the room. Everyone does some of it. For many students of color, first-generation students, and kids whose home culture is different from school, it can feel required, and it can be tiring.

It's a real skill. It also has a cost when you never get a break from it.

Worth asking: Where can I be all of me? Who knows more than one version of me? What would I need to bring a little more of myself into this room?

The story you tellYou're writing it now.

Psychologist Dan McAdams studies "narrative identity": the life story each of us builds about where we came from and where we're headed. People start building that story in adolescence. How you tell the hard chapters matters. People who can find growth or meaning in a rough stretch tend to do better than people who tell the same events as a dead end.

You can't pick every chapter. You do get a big say in how you tell it.

You'll keep changingSo will everyone you know.

In a 2013 study of about 19,000 adults ages 18 to 68, people at every age said they'd changed a lot in the past ten years, and they expected to change very little in the next ten. The researchers called this the "end of history illusion." The study compared people of different ages, so it's a rough picture, and the pattern was clear.

Translation: the you at 25 will be different from the you now, probably more than you'd guess. You don't have to decide everything this year.

Who gets to define you?Labels, feeds, and other people's ideas.
Labels other people give you
"The smart one." "The funny one." "The athlete." They can feel like a box. You can outgrow them.
Your feed
Algorithms show you more of whatever you click, so an identity online can harden fast. Try following something new on purpose.
Your family
Families pass down a lot that's worth keeping: language, faith, food, values, stories. In many cultures, who you are is tied closely to your family and community, and that can be a strength. Building your own identity can include choosing a lot of what you were given.
Part 2 · Practice the skills

Building it on your own terms

What actually helps

Explore on purpose. Commit when it fits.

Tap each one.

1 Try things that are easy to undo.

An elective, a club, a job, a new genre, a different group to sit with. Low-stakes experiments are how you find out what fits.

2 Interview your roots.

Ask a grandparent, parent, or elder: What were you like at my age? What did you have to fight for? What do you hope I keep? You'll learn where some of your pieces came from.

3 Name your values.

Pick three that matter most right now: honesty, loyalty, faith, creativity, fairness, family, adventure, kindness, achievement. Values are steadier than labels, and you can steer by them.

4 Find a place to be all of you.

A friend, an affinity group, a team, a youth group, an online community that's safe. Somewhere you don't have to switch.

5 Let people change, including you.

When a friend reinvents themselves, give them room. Ask for the same.

6 Talk it out with an adult.

A counselor, mentor, coach, or teacher can be a sounding board without telling you who to be.

Try itFour moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd actually do.

Your parents have always said you'll be pre-med like your older sister. You're starting to love design.

A classmate asks where you're from. You say Ohio. They say, "No, where are you really from?"

You started theater this year. Your old friends from the team say, "You've changed. You're one of them now."

A friend tells you, nervously, that they're questioning their gender.

More than one answer can work. The best one leaves room for you, and for the other person, to be real.

In a groupPick a corner. Then see if your guess was right.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move to or point at a corner: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. No one has to share.
  3. After each one, the leader taps to show what the research says.
Four corners · 1 of 3"You should know who you are by graduation."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

No. Across 73 studies, identity keeps developing through the 20s. Exploring in high school is right on schedule.

Four corners · 2 of 3"Most adults expect to change a lot in the next ten years."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

They don't. In a study of about 19,000 adults, people at every age expected little future change, even though they reported lots of change in the past ten years.

Four corners · 3 of 3"Feeling proud of your racial or ethnic background makes a real difference."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Yes. Across 46 studies of minority youth, feeling good about your group went with better self-esteem, fewer depression symptoms, and doing better in school.

Talk about itThree questions for the group.
  • What's one thing you were handed that you've decided to keep? Why?
  • Where in this school do people get to be all of themselves? Where don't they?
  • What label would you like to retire?
Tip for the person leading

Let students choose what they share. Gender, sexuality, faith, immigration, and money can be private or unsafe to discuss. Never ask anyone to speak for their whole group, and don't single out the one student who shares a background.

When it's more than figuring things out

  • Someone is harassing or threatening you because of your race, religion, gender, sexuality, or disability.
  • Someone outed you, or is sharing private information or photos.
  • You feel you have to hide who you are everywhere, including at home, and it's wearing you down.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult today. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night. If someone is in danger right now, call 911.

Other ways in

Plenty of stories are about building a self. Some of these you might remember from when you were younger.

📖 Read

American Born Chinese · Gene Luen Yang, 2006

A graphic novel that weaves three stories together, all about the cost of trying to be someone else. Winner of the Printz Award.

The House on Mango Street · Sandra Cisneros, 1984

Short chapters from Esperanza, a Chicana girl in Chicago, working out who she is and who she wants to be, starting with her name.

Heads up: One chapter describes a sexual assault.

🎬 Watch

Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse · Sony Pictures Animation, 2018

Miles has his dad's expectations, a fancy new school, and a mask everyone says he has to wear a certain way. He figures out his own way. Rated PG.

Mulan · Disney, 1998 Throwback

Still the classic story of not fitting the role you were handed, and finding your own way to honor your family. Rated G.

Video · India.Arie, 2001

A warm, confident song about liking who you are without matching what's on screen.

Kehinde Wiley's portraits · 2000s to now

He paints everyday Black and brown people in the poses of old royal portraits. Worth asking: who usually gets to be painted, and who decides?
