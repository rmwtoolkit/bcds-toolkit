Part 1 · Understand it

Substance use: yours and everyone around you

Sound familiar?Your call now. Here's how to stay safe.
  • For a lot of you, college is the first time these decisions are completely yours. No parent down the hall. No curfew.
  • You'll be at parties where you don't know most people, still trying to find your crowd.
  • Some people drink or use a lot. Plenty don't. Both are normal at college.
  • Whatever you decide for yourself, you'll be around people who are using.

This lesson won't tell you what to choose. It's about staying safe, and keeping the people around you safe, whatever you choose.

The real risksAlcohol: most common, most underestimated.
🍺 Alcohol is the big one.
It's the most common substance on campus, and the most underestimated.
⚖️ Same drinks, different results.
Body size, food, sleep, medications, and tolerance all change how alcohol hits. What's fine for one person can be dangerous for another.
🧭 Limits are hard to know when you're new.
If you haven't been drinking much, you don't yet know how you react, and the warning signs come late.
👥 Pressure is strongest early.
When you're still finding your people, saying no or leaving can feel like a social risk.
🌀 Everyone's inhibitions drop.
Substances lower judgment for everyone in the room, including the people around you.
One drink is smaller than you thinkA party cup is rarely one drink.
It keeps rising after you stopThe last drink isn't the peak.
Pills and powders aren't what they seemIf it's not from a pharmacy, you don't know.
Part 2 · Practice the skills

Staying safe, whatever you choose

If you choose to drinkSet your number before you go.
📏 Decide your number before you go.
It's much easier to stick to a limit you set sober.
🍕 Eat first, and alternate with water.
Food slows absorption. Water in between paces you.
⏱ Go slow.
Roughly one drink an hour lets your liver keep up. Remember, the peak comes later.
🥤 Pour your own, keep it with you.
Don't accept drinks you didn't see made, and don't leave yours unattended. If you set it down, get a new one.
🚫 Don't mix.
Alcohol plus pills (benzos, opioids, sleep meds) or plus weed hits much harder. Mixing with energy drinks hides how drunk you are.
Whatever you chooseArrive together. Leave together.
👯 Arrive together, leave together.
Make a plan with your people. Check in with each other during the night. Nobody walks home alone.
🚗 Have a ride plan.
Save the campus safe-ride number. Never get in a car with someone who's been drinking or using.
💬 Practice your "No thanks."
"I'm good." "I've got an early practice." Holding a cup of water ends most offers.
🌿 Edibles: start low, wait two hours.
They take a long time to kick in. Doubling up is the classic mistake.
💊 Carry Narcan. Know about test strips.
Many campuses give out naloxone and fentanyl test strips for free. Narcan can reverse an opioid overdose and can't hurt someone who isn't overdosing.
🆘 Call. Every time.
Most colleges have medical amnesty policies that protect students who call for help. Find yours before you need it.
Try itKnow CUPS. Call every time.

Call 911 or campus safety if you see any of these:

  • Cold, clammy, pale, or bluish skin
  • Unresponsive: can't be woken
  • Puking while passed out
  • Slow or irregular breathing

Then roll them on their side, stay with them, and if opioids might be involved, give Narcan.

👥 Coleen and Malik · 1 of 2Coleen

It's the third Friday of the semester and Coleen is at a party in an upperclass dorm. She's had two drinks, already more than she's used to. A guy from her orientation group keeps refilling her cup without asking. It feels rude to say something. An hour later she feels much more drunk than she expected. She finds a corner and checks her phone, hoping to look busy. She doesn't know anyone well enough to say "I think I need to leave," and her roommate texted that she's already asleep.

  1. What would you do in this situation?
  2. What makes this harder in college than at home?
  3. Who or what resources would help?
Moves that help

Coleen can switch to water, use the safe-ride or safe-walk number, call or video-call someone she trusts, or ask an RA or friendly-looking person for help getting home. Anyone who notices the refills can step in: "Hey, want to grab some water with me?" Politeness can wait. Safety can't.

👥 Coleen and Malik · 2 of 2Sarah and Malik

Sarah's friend Malik has been drinking since before the party. Now he's slurring, stumbling, laughing too loud at nothing. Malik gets annoyed when anyone tries to slow him down, and last week he told Sarah to "relax." She doesn't want to embarrass him or get him in trouble. Maybe she's overreacting; everyone drinks in college, right? Then Malik tries to climb a flight of stairs and has to grab the railing with both hands.

  1. What would you do in this situation?
  2. What makes this harder in college than at home?
  3. Who or what resources would help?
Moves that help

Sarah doesn't need to win an argument. She can get him water, get him sitting down, bring in a friend he listens to, and get him home safely. If he shows any CUPS signs, she calls 911 or campus safety, every time. Being annoyed with her tomorrow beats the alternative.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • What would you do if you saw what was happening to Coleen?
  • What if Malik were a close friend? Someone you barely know?
  • What makes stepping in feel hard? What would make it easier?
Tip for the person leading

Encourage everyone to see themselves as a helper, guys included: stepping in is a strength. If students aren't drinking at a party, their awareness is even more valuable. Know your school's medical amnesty language if students ask.

Try itSave these numbers before move-in.

Look up your college's version of each one now, and put the numbers in your phone before move-in.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually backfire.
🙊 Staying quiet to be polite.

Embarrassment fades. Some mistakes don't.

😴 "Let them sleep it off."

Blood alcohol can keep rising while someone sleeps.

🧃 Pregaming hard.

Drinking a lot fast before you go is how people end up way past their limit before the party starts.

💊 Trusting a friend's pill.

Your friend can't see fentanyl either.

Call 911 or campus safety right away if someone

  • Can't be woken, or has slow or irregular breathing.
  • Has cold, clammy, bluish skin, or vomits while passed out.
  • Has blue lips or tiny pupils after taking a pill or powder (give Narcan).
  • Has a seizure or chest pain.

Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222. See Get help below.
