Part 1 · Understand it

What test anxiety is

Sound familiar?Blanking doesn't mean you didn't study.
  • You knew the material last night, and then you looked at the test and everything was gone.
  • Your heart pounds, your hands sweat, or your stomach hurts before a test.
  • You read the same question four times and it still doesn't make sense.
  • You couldn't sleep the night before a big quiz.
  • You see other people writing and panic that you're falling behind.
  • Once you remembered the answer, it was right after you turned the test in.

Test anxiety is really common, and it's not a sign you're not smart or didn't study. It's your body's alarm system going off at the wrong time.

What's happening in your body

Tap each one.

🚨The alarm goes off

Your brain sees the test as a threat and triggers fight-or-flight: fast heartbeat, shallow breathing, tense muscles.

🧊Thinking goes offline

Stress hormones make it harder for the thinking part of your brain to pull up memories. That's the blank.

🌀Worry takes up space

Thoughts like "I'm going to fail" use up working memory you need for the actual questions.

Why it hits so hardYour alarm system goes off at the wrong time.
The stakes feel huge.
Grades, parents, teachers, getting into the next level. A lot can feel riding on one test.
Anxiety feeds itself.
Noticing your heart pounding can make you more anxious, which makes it pound harder. It's a loop.
Comparison makes it worse.
Seeing others finish early or hearing them say it was easy can convince you that you're the only one struggling. You're not.
Some tests carry extra weight.
Maybe you're testing in your second language, have a learning difference, or feel like you're showing what your whole family can do. That pressure is real, and teachers and counselors can help with it.
💡 A little stress actually helpsNerves can mean you're getting ready.

Some nervousness can sharpen focus. Research suggests that seeing your racing heart as your body getting ready, instead of as a sign of doom, can help people perform better. The goal isn't zero nerves. It's keeping them from taking over.

The worst part: test anxiety can make you avoid studying, because thinking about the test feels bad. Then you feel less prepared, which feeds the anxiety.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

Breathe, move on, circle back.

Tap one to see how.

🌬 Breathe out longer than in.

In for 4, out for 6. A longer exhale tells your nervous system to calm down. Do it before and during the test.

✍️ Write the worry down.

Research has found that spending a few minutes writing about your worries before a test can help free up your thinking.

🔁 Reframe the nerves.

Tell yourself "I'm getting ready" instead of "I'm freaking out." Same feeling, different story.

⏭ Skip and come back.

If you blank on a question, move on. Answering easier ones calms your brain and often unlocks the hard ones.

🧠 Practice under test conditions.

Taking practice tests, timed and without notes, makes the real thing feel familiar.

😴 Protect your sleep.

Sleep helps memory and calms your stress system. An all-nighter makes test anxiety worse.

Try itOne minute. Breathe with the circle.

Use this before a test, or during one if you feel the panic rising. Follow the circle: breathe in as it grows, out as it shrinks.

Then try grounding: name 3 things you can see, 2 you can hear, and 1 you can feel, like your feet on the floor.

Try itGet the worries out so there's room to think.

Before your next test, take two minutes to write your worries here. Getting them out of your head can free up room for thinking. Nothing is saved.

Try itMid-test blank. What's your move?

Pick the most helpful move.

You're halfway through a test and suddenly blank on a question you know you studied.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese usually crank the anxiety up.
🌙 Pulling an all-nighter.

Less sleep means worse memory and more anxiety. It's a double hit.

🙈 Avoiding studying because it's stressful.

Avoiding makes you less prepared, which makes the anxiety worse.

👀 Watching everyone else.

How fast others finish tells you nothing about how you're doing.

☕ Lots of caffeine.

Energy drinks and lots of coffee can make your heart race even more.

When to ask for more help

  • Test anxiety is hurting your grades even when you know the material.
  • You feel sick, panicky, or can't sleep before most tests.
  • Worry about school is taking over your life outside of tests.

A counselor can help, and some students, including students with ADHD or learning differences and students learning English, qualify for testing accommodations like extra time or a quieter room. See Get help below.

Other ways in

Lots of people have frozen up and found a way through. A few of these you might remember from when you were little.

📖 Read

Testing Miss Malarkey · Judy Finchler, illustrated by Kevin O'Malley, 2000 Throwback

The whole school is panicking about one big test, even the teachers and the parents. The kid telling the story notices that the grown-ups are the most nervous of all.

Ruby Finds a Worry · Tom Percival, 2019

Ruby's worry gets bigger every time she ignores it. When she finally talks about it, it shrinks. Like writing the worry down.

🎬 Watch

Belly Breathe · Sesame Street, 2012 Throwback

Common, Colbie Caillat and Elmo sing about slow belly breathing. It's a segment for little kids, and it still works before a test.

🎧 Listen

Surface Pressure · Jessica Darrow, 2021

Luisa in Encanto is the strong one, and she's starting to crack under everything she carries for her family. For when a test feels like it's about more than you.

🖼️ Look

Guts · Raina Telgemeier, 2019

Raina's stomach hurts before school, tests and anything stressful, and she figures out it's worry. A graphic memoir about anxiety in the body and getting help for it.

Heads up: Raina gets scared of throwing up and avoids some foods for a while.

✏️ Make your own

Draw the alarm from the lesson: a siren, a smoke detector, whatever yours looks like. Next to it, write what you'll tell it next time: "I'm getting ready."
