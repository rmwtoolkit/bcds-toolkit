Holding a Friend's Secret

Some things are too big to keep.

What's going on

Being the person someone trusts is a big deal. It can also feel like way too much to hold by yourself. You don't have to decide if it's serious. That's an adult's job. Your job is to tell.

A friend trusts you→"Promise you won't tell"→You want to keep it→You're scared to lose them→You hold it alone

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "They'll be mad at me."
  • "I promised."
  • "Maybe it's not serious."

💬 What you notice

  • a late-night message
  • pulling away from everyone
  • big changes in eating or sleep

🫀 Body

  • your stomach drops
  • it feels too heavy
  • hard to stop thinking about it

Where to step in

Listen first. Then bring in an adult.

  1. Listen first. "Thank you for telling me" is a great start.
  2. Don't promise to keep it secret: "Let's figure out who to tell together."
  3. Tell a trusted adult today, and offer to walk with them to the counselor.

Tell an adult now, call or text 988, or call 911 if they're in danger (988 can also send a crisis team).

Part 1 · Understand it

When a friend shares something serious

Sound familiar?Too big to hold alone? You don't have to.
  • A friend told you they've been hurting themselves.
  • A friend said something like "I don't want to be here anymore" or "Everyone would be better off without me."
  • A friend told you someone is hurting them, at home or somewhere else.
  • A friend has stopped eating lunch, or talks about their body in a way that worries you.
  • A friend posted or messaged something late at night that made your stomach drop.
  • A friend made you promise not to tell anyone.

Being the person someone trusts is a big deal. It can also feel like way too much to hold by yourself. You don't have to.

What "scary" can look like

Tap each one.

💬Something they say

Talking about wanting to die, hurting themselves, or feeling hopeless, even as a joke.

📱Something online

A worrying post, a goodbye-sounding message, a dark story or comment, or a sudden change in what they share.

👀Something you notice

Giving away things they care about, pulling away from everyone, cuts or burns, big changes in eating or sleep.

Why it's so hardYou don't have to decide if it's serious. Just tell.
You're scared of losing the friendship.
It's common to worry your friend will be mad at you for telling. They might be, at first. Most people later say they're grateful someone cared enough.
You want to keep your promise.
Keeping secrets is part of being a good friend, usually. If a friend tells you they're gay, trans, or still figuring out who they are, or shares something private about their family, that's theirs to share. Keep it safe. The secrets that are too big to keep are the ones about someone getting hurt.
You might not know if it's serious.
You don't have to decide that. That's an adult's job. Your job is to tell.
It's not your job to fix it.
You can be a good friend without being their counselor. Getting an adult involved is how you help.
🛟 Safety beats secretsTelling isn't snitching. It's how you help.

If a friend's safety is at risk, telling a trusted adult is the most caring thing you can do. You're not snitching. You're making sure your friend gets help from people who know how to give it.

The worst part: you might feel like you're betraying them. You're actually doing the opposite. You're keeping them safe.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

What to do next

What actually helps

Listen first. Then bring in an adult.

Tap one to see how.

👂 Listen first.

Let them talk. You don't need the perfect words. "Thank you for telling me" and "I'm really glad you told me" are a great start.

❓ Ask directly.

If you're worried about suicide, it's okay to ask, "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Research shows asking does not put the idea in someone's head. It can bring relief.

🙅 Don't promise to keep it secret.

Try: "I care about you too much to keep this to myself. Let's figure out who to tell together."

🧑‍🏫 Tell a trusted adult.

A counselor, teacher, coach, nurse, or parent or caregiver. Tell them today. If it's after school, tell an adult at home or call 988 together.

🤝 Offer to go with them.

"Want me to walk with you to the counselor?" can make it much easier for them to go.

🫶 Take care of yourself too.

Hearing something scary is heavy. Talk to an adult about how you're feeling as well.

Which adult do I tell?There's always someone you can tell.
  • At school: a counselor is the best first choice. Any teacher, coach, advisor, or nurse can help you get there.
  • After school or on the weekend: your parent or guardian, another family adult like a grandparent, aunt, or older cousin, or your friend's parent if it feels safe.
  • Anytime, day or night: call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. You can call for a friend, not just for yourself. If your friend is LGBTQ+, The Trevor Project (1-866-488-7386, or text START to 678-678) and Trans Lifeline (877-565-8860) are there too.
  • If they're in danger right now: call 911. You can also call 988, which can send a crisis team instead of police.
  • If it's an online post: tell an adult, and use the app's report tool. Most apps have a way to flag self-harm posts so the person gets support resources.
Try itPractice the words now, so they're there later.

Practice the words now so they're easier later. Nothing is saved.

Try itTwo hard moments. What's the caring move?

Pick the best move.

Your friend shows you cuts on their arm and says, "Promise you won't tell anyone."

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpThese leave your friend without real help.
🤐 Keeping it to yourself.

You're not trained to handle this alone, and you shouldn't have to.

😬 Freaking out or lecturing.

Stay as calm as you can. Your friend needs to feel heard, not judged.

🦸 Trying to be their only support.

No one person can hold this. Bring in adults.

💬 Spreading it to other friends.

Telling peers isn't the same as getting help, and it can hurt your friend.

Act right away if your friend

  • Talks about wanting to die or has a plan to hurt themselves.
  • Is being hurt or abused by someone.
  • Posts or sends something that sounds like a goodbye.
  • Has taken something or hurt themselves already.

Tell an adult now, call or text 988, or call 911 if they're in danger (988 can also send a crisis team). See Get help below.
