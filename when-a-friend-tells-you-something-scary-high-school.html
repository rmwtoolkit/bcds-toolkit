Holding a Friend's Secret

They chose you. Now you're holding it.

What's going on

When a friend picks you to tell, you are probably the first to know. Keeping confidences is part of friendship, most of the time. A secret that puts someone's life or body at risk is the exception.

A friend picks you→"Swear you won't tell"→Loyalty pulls one way→Safety pulls the other→You carry it alone

How you'll know

💭 Thoughts

  • "I promised."
  • "What if I make it worse?"
  • "A real friend handles it."

💬 What you hear

  • "Can I tell you something?"
  • jokes that keep getting darker
  • "Don't make it worse."

🫀 Carrying it

  • replaying the conversation
  • sleeping badly
  • guilty, or angry at them

Where to step in

Listen first. Then bring in an adult.

  1. Slow down and listen: "Thank you for telling me. Keep going, I'm listening."
  2. Don't promise secrecy. Offer company: "Let's go to the counselor together. I'll stay."
  3. Tell the adult, even if they won't. Then stay their friend afterward.

Get an adult now, call or text 988, or call 911 if they're in danger.

Part 1 · Understand it

When someone picks you to tell

Sound familiar?They chose you. Now you're holding it.
  • It's 1 a.m. and a friend texts, "Can I tell you something if you swear you won't say anything?"
  • A teammate has been skipping meals since preseason and fainted at practice. She says it's "just cutting weight."
  • Your friend's jokes about not waking up have gotten darker, and nobody laughs anymore.
  • Someone tells you their partner checks their phone, controls who they see, and shoved them once.
  • A friend says an adult at home has been hurting them, and begs you not to make it worse.

You learned "tell an adult" years ago. That's still the rule. What's harder now is everything around it: you know more, people trust you with more, and the right adult isn't always obvious.

ResearchYou are probably the first to know. In a survey of 6,020 students aged 15 and 16, teens who had hurt themselves or thought about it were more likely to go to a friend than to anyone else. Many who felt they needed help never asked anyone at all (Evans, Hawton and Rodham, 2005).

First question: how urgent is it?

Now: don't wait until morning

Suicidal crises can move fast. In a study of people aged 13 to 34 who survived a nearly fatal attempt, about 1 in 4 said less than five minutes passed between deciding and acting. Stay with your friend if it's safe, call 988 or 911, and get an adult there in person. Help get pills, weapons, or anything else they might use out of reach.

Today: an adult hears it before you sleep

Self-harm, talk about wanting to die, abuse, an eating problem, or drug use that scares you. These need an adult who can act, today. If school is over, that's a parent, your friend's parent if it's safe, or a call to 988 together.

This week: keep the door open

Your friend seems off, quieter, or more checked out than usual. Keep showing up, ask how they're really doing, and tell a counselor what you've noticed. You can say, "Nothing big happened. I'm just worried."

Why it's hardLoyalty and safety pull in opposite directions.
You promised.
Keeping confidences is part of being a good friend, most of the time. A secret that puts someone's life or body at risk is the exception, and it's the one that matters most.
You're scared you'll make it worse.
Many people worry that asking about suicide plants the idea. The research says it doesn't. More on that in Part 2.
You might know more than any adult does.
That's a lot of weight. It's also why your telling matters: the adults who could help may have no idea.
Telling isn't snitching.
The difference is purpose. Snitching gets someone in trouble. Telling gets someone help. Your friend might be angry for a while. Most people later say they're glad someone cared enough.
What happens after you tellUsually a conversation and a plan, not sirens.

Not knowing what happens next stops a lot of people. Most of the time, a counselor listens to you, checks how safe your friend is, talks with your friend, and helps make a plan. Family usually gets involved when it's safe for them to.

If the danger comes from an adult at home, it's different. In Massachusetts, school staff are mandated reporters: if they believe someone under 18 is being abused or neglected, they must report it to the Department of Children and Families (DCF), a child-protection agency. They're required to act, which is the point. It means you aren't the one who has to fix it.

You can ask the counselor whether your name has to come up. Often it doesn't, though they can't always promise.

Families handle hard things differently. In some, problems stay inside the family, mental health isn't talked about, or contact with any agency feels frightening. If that's true for your friend, tell the counselor. It changes how they help, and they can plan around it.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

The first five minutes, and after

What actually helps

Listen, ask straight, then bring in an adult.

Tap each step.

1 Slow down and listen.

Don't rush to fix it or argue them out of how they feel. "Thank you for telling me. Keep going, I'm listening" does more than advice.

2 Ask the direct question.

"Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Saying the actual words tells them you can handle the answer. In a trial with 2,342 New York high school students, those randomly assigned to be asked about suicide were no more distressed or suicidal afterward than those who weren't, including the students at highest risk (Gould and colleagues, 2005).

3 Ask about a plan.

"Have you thought about how?" A plan, a date, or access to pills or a weapon moves this to Now.

4 Don't promise secrecy. Offer company.

"I care about you too much to keep this to myself. Let's go to the counselor together, and I'll stay while you talk."

5 Tell the adult, even if they won't.

If your friend refuses, you still tell. You can let them know first: "I'm going to talk to Ms. Rivera after lunch. I'd rather you came with me."

6 Stay their friend afterward.

Text, sit together at lunch, act normal. Telling an adult doesn't end your part. It changes it from carrying to supporting.

ResearchFriends who know what to do change outcomes. In a randomized trial in 18 high schools, students trained as peer leaders were about four times more likely than untrained peers to connect a suicidal friend with an adult, and the whole school grew more confident that adults could help (Wyman and colleagues, 2010).
Who to callThere's a right person for every hour.
  • During school: a counselor first. A teacher, coach, athletic trainer, advisor, or nurse can walk you there.
  • After school or weekends: your parent or guardian, another adult relative you trust, or your friend's parent if that's safe.
  • Any hour: call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. You can call about a friend. They'll help you figure out the next step.
  • In Massachusetts: the Behavioral Health Help Line, 833-773-2445, call or text, 24/7, free, in more than 200 languages. It can send a mobile crisis team to a home instead of police.
  • Danger right now: 911 or the nearest emergency room.
  • An online post: screenshot it, tell an adult, and use the app's report tool so the person gets support resources.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths that keep people quietWhat the research says instead.
MYTH Asking about suicide gives them the idea.

Trials and reviews keep finding the opposite. Asking doesn't raise risk, and many people feel relief that someone finally said it out loud.

MYTH People who talk about it won't do it.

Talking about wanting to die is one of the main warning signs crisis lines list, even when it comes out as a joke. Take every mention seriously.

MYTH If they seem fine now, it's over.

They may be better. They may also have stopped talking. Keep checking in, and keep the adult in the loop.

MYTH A real friend handles it themselves.

A real friend gets the right people involved. You're a friend, not a therapist, and nobody should carry this alone.

MYTH Telling other friends is getting help.

It spreads the story without getting your friend care, and it can hurt them. Go to one adult instead.

Carrying itHearing it can stay with you too.

After a disclosure, it's common to replay the conversation, sleep badly, feel guilty, or feel angry at your friend for putting you in this spot. All of that is normal. None of it means you did something wrong.

Tell the counselor how you are doing, not only how your friend is. Keep doing the things that steady you. You can care about someone and still have limits on how much you hold.

Act right away if your friend

  • Talks about wanting to die and has a plan, a date, or a way to do it.
  • Sends or posts something that sounds like a goodbye.
  • Has already hurt themselves or taken something.
  • Is being hurt by someone, or is afraid to go home.

Get an adult now, call or text 988, or call 911 if they're in danger. See Get help below.
