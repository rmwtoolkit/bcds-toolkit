Part 1 · Understand it

Healthy, unhealthy, abusive

Sound familiar?Each thing seems small. Together they add up.
  • They want your location on "so I know you're safe," then ask why you were at that address.
  • Most fights end with you apologizing, even when you're not sure what you did.
  • You've stopped seeing certain friends because it isn't worth the argument.
  • They say nobody else would put up with you.
  • Your friends have gone quiet about the relationship.
  • You're not dating, and that's common. These patterns show up in friendships, teams, and families too.

You know what a red flag is. The harder skill is spotting a pattern from inside it, when each piece seems minor and the same person also makes you feel great.

Control rarely shows up all at once.

Healthy

You both have your own friends, plans, and privacy. You can disagree and not be afraid of what comes next.

Unhealthy

One person makes most of the decisions, jealousy runs the mood, pressure replaces asking. Sometimes this can change, if both people see it and work on it.

Abusive

One person uses control, fear, or harm to get their way. This isn't a communication problem, and trying harder won't fix it. Since 2024, Massachusetts law counts coercive control, a pattern of threatening, isolating, or controlling someone, as abuse when a person seeks a protective order.

The researchTeen relationships count. So does what goes wrong in them.
It's common.
In the CDC's 2021 national survey, about 1 in 12 high school students who dated in the past year experienced physical dating violence, and about 1 in 10 experienced sexual dating violence.
Phones are part of it.
In a Pew Research Center survey of teens with dating experience, 31% said a partner had checked up on them several times a day, 21% had a partner read their texts without permission, and 13% had a partner demand their passwords.
Digital abuse counts as abuse.
A 2013 study of 3,745 teens in relationships found about 1 in 4 had experienced cyber dating abuse in the past year: harassing texts, monitoring, pressure for sexual photos.
It follows people forward.
A national study tracked 5,681 teens for about five years. Those who experienced dating violence were more likely as young adults to have suicidal thoughts and to end up in violent relationships again. That's why noticing early matters.
Location and phonesSharing isn't the problem. How it's used is.

Plenty of families and friends share locations, and in some families that's simply expected. The line isn't the app. It's whether you could turn it off without being punished, and whether it gets used to question you.

A useful test for any request: could I say no to this without being afraid of what happens next?

If turning off your location would start a fight, it isn't about safety.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Reading the pattern, and acting on it

When you can't tell

Three questions that cut through it.

Can I say no without paying for it?Not "are they nice," but what happens after a no: sulking, punishment, fear, or nothing.
Is my world getting bigger or smaller?Friends, family, activities, grades. Healthy relationships add to your life.
Do I feel more like myself?Or am I spending my energy managing their moods?

If the answers worry you. Tap to see how.

1 Write it down.

What happened, and when. Patterns are easy to talk yourself out of, and screenshots matter if it ever turns into threats.

2 Get one outside view.

Isolation is how control works. One friend, relative, or counselor who sees the whole picture breaks it.

3 Plan before you leave, if you're afraid.

Tell someone first. End it in a public place or by phone. Change your passwords and turn off location sharing. love is respect can help you make a plan.

4 Know there are legal protections.

In Massachusetts, people can seek an abuse prevention order against a dating partner. A counselor or advocate can explain how it works for someone your age.

5 Check yourself too.

Jealousy, checking a phone, sulking to win. Anyone can slide into these. Noticing it in yourself is the first step to stopping it.

Myths that keep people stuckWhat people say, and what's true.
MYTH Jealousy means they care.

A flash of jealousy is human. Using it to control where you go and who you see is not love.

MYTH It's only abuse if it's physical.

Monitoring, threats, and isolation do real harm, and Massachusetts law now names coercive control as abuse.

MYTH If I'm a better partner, it'll stop.

Control isn't caused by your mistakes, so being perfect doesn't end it.

MYTH This only happens to girls, or in straight couples.

It happens in every kind of relationship, to people of every gender. Boys and LGBTQ+ teens deserve the same help.

Get help right away if

  • Someone threatens you, hurts you, or makes you afraid.
  • Someone threatens to hurt themselves if you leave. (They can call or text 988. You don't have to stay.)
  • Someone pressures or forces you into anything physical or sexual.
  • Someone threatens to share private photos or messages.

love is respect is free and confidential, 24/7, for young people: text LOVEIS to 22522 or call 1-866-331-9474. A counselor, relative, or other adult you trust can help too.

If you're in danger right now, call 911. See Get help below.

A reminder

Intensity isn't the same as love. The relationship worth having is the one where you can say no, see your people, and still feel like yourself.

Other ways in

Writers have been drawing the line between love and control for a long time. Two of these you probably met as a kid.

📖 Read

The Giving Tree · Shel Silverstein, 1964 Throwback

The boy takes and takes, down to the trunk, and the tree keeps saying she's happy. Read it again with the lesson's question: is her world getting bigger or smaller? People have argued about this book for sixty years.

Coraline · Neil Gaiman, 2002 Throwback

The Other Mother has better food and all the attention Coraline wants. Then she wants to sew buttons over Coraline's eyes so she'll stay forever. Step one of the control ladder, then step five.

Heads up: Scary in places. The 2009 movie is rated PG for thematic elements, scary images, some language and suggestive humor.

🎬 Watch

Pride & Prejudice · Joe Wright, 2005

Wickham is charming and tells a great story about himself. Darcy is awkward and quietly does the right thing. What people do tells you more than how they come across. Rated PG.

🎧 Listen

Since U Been Gone · Kelly Clarkson, 2004

The relief after a bad relationship ends: she can breathe for the first time. Play it after you answer the three questions honestly.

✏️ Make your own

Draw the control ladder from Part 1 using a story you know, from a movie or a book. Put one moment from it on each rung. Then draw the exit: where could someone have stepped off?

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
  • Tell a counselor, teacher, coach, or parent today. You can show them this screen.
  • If you might act on thoughts of hurting yourself, call 911 or go to an emergency room.

The smallest version counts.

Protect yourself the way you'd protect your best friend. You'd want respect for them. You deserve the same.
