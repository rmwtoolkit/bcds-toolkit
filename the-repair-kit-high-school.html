Part 1 · Understand it

Repair is a skill

Sound familiar?Something broke. You're not sure how to fix it.
  • You said something in the group chat at midnight that looks a lot worse in daylight.
  • A friend apologized with "sorry you feel that way," and it made things worse.
  • You shared something you were told in confidence, and now you don't know how to come back from it.
  • You and a teammate haven't really talked since a blowup at practice, and the season isn't over.
  • The fight is with someone you can't avoid: a sibling, a coworker, a lab partner.

You know how to say sorry. This is the next level: which parts of an apology actually matter, why apologizing is harder than it should be, and how to repair things with people you can't just walk away from. That's most of adult life.

What makes an apology work

Roy Lewicki and colleagues at Ohio State had 755 people rate apologies built from six possible parts. Apologies with more parts worked better, but the parts weren't equal.

Taking responsibility comes first

Naming what you did, specifically, with no "if" and no "but." This mattered most.

Then an offer to fix it

Deleting the post, telling the group it was your fault, paying for what broke. It turns words into action.

"I'm sorry" is in the middle

Regret, explanation, and a promise to change all help, roughly equally. None of them carries an apology alone.

Asking for forgiveness matters least

It can come across as pressure. If you leave one part out, the researchers said, leave out this one.

Research

Across four studies, psychologists Karina Schumann and Carol Dweck found that people who believe personality can change were more likely to take responsibility after hurting someone. If you see a mistake as something you can grow from, owning it feels less like a verdict on who you are.

Why it's so hardRefusing to apologize can feel good. That's the trap.
Not apologizing gives a short-term boost.
In a 2013 study, people who wrote a message refusing to apologize for something they'd done felt more powerful and had higher self-esteem afterward than people who didn't. The researchers weren't recommending it. They were explaining why "sorry" can be so hard to say. The boost does nothing for the relationship.
Defense mode.
When you feel attacked, your attention narrows to protecting yourself. It's hard to see the other side while part of you is still in the fight.
Going first feels like losing.
Both people wait for the other one. Meanwhile, the silence becomes its own problem, and the first message gets harder to send every day.
Solomon's paradoxYou give better advice to friends than to yourself.

Psychologists Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross found that people reason more wisely about a friend's relationship conflict than their own: they consider other viewpoints, admit what they don't know, and look for compromise. When people thought about their own conflict from a distance, for example in the third person, the gap disappeared.

Try it: "Why is Jae so angry at Sam?" works better than "Why is Sam so angry at me?" Same facts, clearer head.

OnlineOnline fights come with an audience and a record.
  • An argument in the group chat that everyone watched.
  • A post that felt aimed at you, or one of yours that was.
  • Screenshots of the fight getting passed around.
  • Being left on read after you apologized.

People pick sides from screenshots without the whole story. And no tone, no faces, means everything reads harsher than it was meant.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

How to fix it

The repair sequence

Cool down, own it, make it right, listen.

Tap each step.

1 Cool down first.

An hour, a night, sometimes a few days. You're ready when you can think about it without your heart racing, you're curious about their side, and you want the friendship back more than you want to win.

2 Step back from it.

Think about the fight in the third person, or ask what you'd tell a friend in your position. It's the fastest way past Solomon's paradox.

3 Lead with responsibility.

Specific, not vague. "I shouldn't have posted your message. That was private, and I made it public." Not "sorry for whatever happened."

4 Offer to repair, and do it.

"I deleted it, and I told the group it was on me." Action is the second most important part, and it's the one people skip.

5 Drop the "but."

"I'm sorry, but you started it" cancels the apology. Your side matters. Save it for a later conversation.

6 Listen to understand.

"What was it like from your side?" Then listen without planning your comeback. You can disagree about what happened and both still feel heard.

If you were the one who got hurt
  • Say it once, simply: "When you ___, I felt ___." One thing, not a list.
  • Let them answer. They may not have known how it landed.
  • Forgiving isn't forgetting. You can accept an apology and still need time to trust again.
  • You don't have to accept every apology. "Sorry" without change, again and again, isn't repair.
Repair looks different in different families

In some families and cultures, a direct "I was wrong" is expected. In others, repair mostly happens through action: showing up, doing a favor, cooking for someone, acting normal again, or having an older relative step in. Some families rarely say sorry out loud at all. Both can be real repair. If a friend's way is different from yours, their apology may be there in a form you weren't looking for. How does your family usually make things right?

Tap each one to see why.

A good apology is specific, has no "but," and shows what you'll do about it.

Try itDraft it messy. Then check it against the research.

Write a first draft. It stays on this page. Read it back before you decide what to actually say.

Check it: Does it start with what you did? Does it offer repair? Is there a "but," an "if," or a request for forgiveness you could drop? Then say it in person or on a call if you can.

What makes it biggerThese usually grow the fight.
Fighting over text.

No tone, no faces, and a permanent record. For a real conversation, meet in person or call.

Recruiting a team.

Getting people on your side makes it harder for anyone to back down without losing face.

Bringing up everything ever.

Stick to what happened this time. A list makes the other person defend instead of listen.

Acting like it never happened.

Small things can pass on their own. Big things you skip repairing tend to come back later, bigger.

Online, right nowTake it offline. Repair works better face to face.
Move it offline.
Text "can we talk tomorrow?" and stop there.
Clean up what you posted.
If you posted something in the heat of it, delete it. That's part of the repair.
Don't screenshot and share.
Even if you're right. Sharing the fight makes it bigger and harder to fix.
Give "read" some grace.
No reply to an apology can mean they're still thinking. Give it a day or two before deciding it means no.

When it's not something to repair alone

Some situations need an adult, not just an apology:

  • Someone is threatening you or anyone else.
  • The fight is turning into a pile-on, online or at school.
  • Someone keeps hurting you and apologizing, over and over.
  • Something happened that made you feel unsafe.

Want to write more?

A reminder

Owning a mistake isn't a verdict on who you are. It's a skill, and people who see themselves as able to grow find it easier. Most fights between people who care about each other can be repaired.

Other ways in

Some of the best apologies in fiction are mostly action. These get it.

📖 Read

Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen, 1813

Darcy's letter explains himself but doesn't fix much. What changes things is what he does next, quietly paying to clean up the Lydia mess. Offer to repair, then do it.

🎬 Watch

Avatar: The Last Airbender · Nickelodeon, 2005 Throwback

In "The Southern Raiders," Zuko tries to earn back Katara's trust by helping her find the man who killed her mother. She doesn't forgive him because he says sorry. She forgives him after he shows up. Rated TV-Y7.

Heads up: The episode centers on Katara's grief and anger over her mother's death, years before the story.

Little Women · Greta Gerwig, 2019

Amy burns Jo's manuscript, a real hurt between sisters who share a house and can't avoid each other. Rated PG for thematic elements and brief smoking.

🎧 Listen

Back to December · Taylor Swift, 2010

Swift called it her first apology song. It names exactly what she did that night, with no "if" and no "but." Acknowledging responsibility, which the Lewicki study ranked first.

🖼️ Look

American Born Chinese · Gene Luen Yang, 2006

Jin hurts his best friend Wei-Chen badly and lets the friendship go. The way back takes Jin owning who he is first.

Heads up: Uses a deliberately offensive Chinese caricature to make a point about racism, and includes a fight between friends.

✏️ Make your own

Write the fight you're stuck on in the third person, using both names, the way the lesson suggests. Then write the apology, putting the six parts in the order the research ranked them. Leave off asking for forgiveness.

What this might mean

You're not ready to repair yet, and that's okay. Trying while you're still hot usually makes it worse.

What might help right now

  • Give it more time. Sleep on it.
  • Write out everything you're mad about, then put it away.
  • Move: a walk, practice, anything that gets the energy out.
  • Don't text them yet.

What this might mean

Nervous means you care about the friendship. Going first is brave, and it usually works better than people expect.

What might help right now

  • Use the apology draft in Part 2, and check it against the ranking.
  • Practice saying it out loud, or to someone you trust.
  • Pick a calm, private moment. "Hey, can we talk for a sec?" is enough to start.
  • Keep it short. You don't have to fix everything in one conversation.

What this might mean

Waiting for an apology can feel endless. It's okay to tell them how you feel, even if they haven't made the first move.

What might help right now

  • Say it simply: "When you ___, I felt ___."
  • Talk to someone outside the fight about it.
  • Decide how long you're willing to wait before you reach out or let it go.

What this might mean

Some friendships come back stronger after a fight. Some don't. You don't have to know yet.

What might help right now

  • Ask yourself: is this a one-time thing or a pattern?
  • Is This a Good Friend? can help you think it through.
  • If it is over, Friendship Breakups can help with what comes next.

What this might mean

Repair is working, or time is helping. Better days don't have to be perfect days to count.

What might help right now

  • Notice what helped, so you can use it next time.
  • Do something normal together, to rebuild the easy feeling.
  • Give yourself credit for working through something hard.
