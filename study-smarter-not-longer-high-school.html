Study Smarter

Familiar feels like knowing. It isn't.

What's going on

Rereading makes material feel familiar, and your brain reads that as knowing. The methods that work, like quizzing yourself and spreading study out, feel slower and clumsier. That effort is what builds a memory you can use.

Part 1 · Understand it

Why studying that feels good often isn't

Sound familiar?You felt ready. The test disagreed.
  • You reread the chapter twice, everything looked familiar, and you still couldn't write the free response.
  • You learned the unit for the unit test, and by the cumulative final it was gone.
  • You did 20 problems of the same type perfectly, then froze when the test mixed them up.
  • Your study group spent two hours "going over it." Nobody quizzed anyone.

If you've heard "quiz yourself and spread it out," that's right. Here's the next layer: why your brain tells you the wrong methods are working, and how to plan for tests that cover months of material.

The experiment that changed how researchers think about studying

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had college students learn short nonfiction passages. Some reread the passage. Others read it once, then wrote down everything they could remember, with no feedback. Then everyone took a final test after five minutes, two days, or a week.

In a second experiment, students who had only reread predicted they would remember the most. They remembered the least a week later. That's the trap: rereading makes material feel familiar, and familiar feels like knowing.

The researchMost students use the methods that work least.
The ratings are in.
In 2013, psychologist John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed hundreds of studies on ten common study techniques. Only two earned a "high utility" rating: practice testing and spreading study out over time. Rereading, highlighting, and summarizing were rated low.
And yet.
In a 2009 survey of college students, about 84% said they reread notes or textbooks to study. About 11% said they practiced recalling information, and only 1% called it their main method.
Why the good methods feel bad.
Psychologist Robert Bjork calls these "desirable difficulties." Pulling information out of memory, waiting a few days, and mixing problem types all feel slower and clumsier in the moment. That effort is what builds a memory you can use later.
Sleep is part of the method.
During sleep, the brain replays and strengthens what you learned that day. A review by Susanne Diekelmann and Jan Born describes sleep as an active part of memory consolidation. Cutting sleep to study more trades away part of what you studied.
Mixing it upPractice that feels worse, results that are better.

In a 2007 study by Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, college students practiced math problems either in blocks (all of one type, then the next) or mixed. During practice, the block group did better: 89% correct versus 60%. On a test a week later, the mixed group scored 63% and the block group 20%.

It was a small study, but mixed practice has held up in classrooms since. Blocking tells you which method to use before you start. Mixing makes you figure that out, which is exactly what the test asks.

If practice feels smooth, check whether it's practice or a performance for yourself.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

A study system for high-stakes tests

Retrieval, upgraded

Make every session a test.

Tap one to see how.

1 Brain dump first, notes second.

Before you open anything, write everything you know about the topic on a blank page. Then check your notes and fill gaps in a different color. The colored parts are tomorrow's study list.

2 Ask why and how.

Don't stop at "what is it." Ask "why does this happen?" and "how does it connect to last unit?" Explaining forces you to rebuild the idea.

3 Practice in test format.

If the test is free response, write free responses. If it's timed, time yourself. Released AP and SAT questions are free online.

4 Mix old with new.

Every session, include a few questions from earlier units. Cumulative finals reward the people who never stopped reviewing.

5 Quiz each other, don't review together.

Study groups work when people take turns asking questions with books closed. Reading notes to each other is rereading with company.

Try itBrain dump. Ten minutes, no notes.

Pick a topic from a class with a test coming. Write everything you can remember without looking. Then open your notes and list what you missed. That gap is your study plan. Nothing here is saved.

Spacing, by the numbersHow far apart should study sessions be?

In a 2008 study of more than 1,350 people, Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues varied the gap between study sessions and the time until the test. The best gap grew with the test delay. For a test a week away, the best gap was a day or two. For a test a year away, it was closer to a month.

A plan for a unit test ten days out:

  1. Day 1: Learn it. End with a brain dump.
  2. Day 3: Practice questions from memory, then check.
  3. Day 6: Mixed practice test, including last unit. Mark what you missed.
  4. Day 8: Only the missed material, from memory.
  5. Day 9: Short final quiz. Then sleep.

For finals, AP exams, and the SAT or ACT, the same rule means starting weeks out, with a little each week, not a big push at the end.

Try itThree study plans. Which one would you bet on?

Kenji has an AP Bio unit exam in ten days. His plan: make a detailed color-coded study guide over the weekend, then reread it every night.

Rosa's precalc final covers six units. She plans to review one unit per day, in order, doing all the problems of each type together.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths that waste study timeWhat the research says instead.
MYTH I'm a visual learner.

You may prefer visuals, and that's fine. But a 2008 review by Harold Pashler and colleagues found no good evidence that teaching to a person's "learning style" improves learning. Retrieval and spacing help everyone.

MYTH If it feels easy, I know it.

Ease while studying often means familiarity, not recall. The rereaders in Roediger and Karpicke's study were the most confident and remembered the least.

MYTH More hours means more learning.

Two hours of rereading can teach less than 45 minutes of quizzing yourself. Count questions answered from memory, not minutes spent.

MYTH An all-nighter is worth it for a big test.

Sleep is when memories get strengthened. Skipping it costs you some of what you studied, plus focus on test day.

When to ask for more help

  • You're using good methods and still struggling in a class.
  • Reading, writing, or focusing takes you much longer than it seems to take others.
  • School stress is taking over your evenings, your sleep, or your mood.

Teachers, learning specialists, and counselors can help. If a learning difference is part of it, an evaluation can open up supports, now and in college. See Get help below.

A reminder

The methods that work will feel harder at first. That feeling is the learning happening. Trust the results on the test, not the comfort of the study session.

Other ways in

Memory research shows up in more places than textbooks. A few of these go deeper, and one you might remember from first grade.

📖 Read

Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning · Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III & Mark A. McDaniel, 2014

Written partly by Roediger, from the rereading-versus-testing experiment in this lesson. It's the long version of why retrieval and spacing beat rereading.

Moonwalking with Einstein · Joshua Foer, 2011

A journalist with an ordinary memory trains for one year and wins the USA Memory Championship. Memory turns out to be something you practice, not something you're born with.

Thank You, Mr. Falker · Patricia Polacco, 1998 Throwback

Trisha can't read in fifth grade and thinks she's dumb until a teacher sees what's going on and helps. Polacco based it on her own dyslexia. A reminder that struggling isn't the same as not being smart.

Heads up: Classmates tease Trisha a lot before things get better.

🎬 Watch

Spellbound · Jeffrey Blitz, 2002

A documentary about eight kids headed to the National Spelling Bee. Watch how differently they prepare, from one father's tutors and constant drills to a dictionary nearly worn to pieces. Rated G.

🎧 Listen

The Climb · Miley Cyrus, 2009 Throwback

From Hannah Montana: The Movie. It's about the climb being the part that matters. Bjork's desirable difficulties, set to music.
