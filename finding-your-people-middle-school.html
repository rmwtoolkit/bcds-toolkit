Part 1 · Understand it

How friendships actually start

Sound familiar?Still looking for your people? So is half the school.
  • You just started middle school and none of your elementary friends are in your classes.
  • You moved, maybe from another town or another country, and you're starting over from zero.
  • Your old friend group changed, and you don't really fit there anymore.
  • You have people to sit with, but nobody you'd call a close friend.
  • Everyone else seems to have a best friend.
  • You're not sure how to go from "we talk in class" to actually hanging out.

Feeling like you haven't found your people yet is one of the most common feelings in school. It's usually a timing problem, not a you problem.

Where friendships actually come from

Tap each one.

🔁Showing up again and again

Most friendships grow from seeing the same people over and over: a class, a team, the bus, a club. Repetition does a lot of the work.

🎯Shared stuff

Liking the same game, show, music, sport, or cause, or sharing a language, faith, or culture, gives you something to talk about besides "so... how's it going?"

🪜Small steps up

Hi in the hall, then a question in class, then sitting together, then a text, then a plan. Friendships build in steps.

Why it can feel so hardReal friends take time. Weeks isn't enough yet.
It takes longer than you'd think.
Research suggests it takes many hours of time together for an acquaintance to become a real friend. If it hasn't happened in a few weeks, that's normal.
Everyone's a little nervous.
The person you're scared to talk to is probably worried about what you think of them too.
Groups look closed from the outside.
Established groups can look like walls. Most have more openings than you'd guess, especially one person at a time.
New places make your brain cautious.
In unfamiliar situations, your brain scans for danger and rejection. That makes small talk feel high-stakes even when it isn't.
🌱 The liking gapThey probably liked you more than you think.

Studies have found that after a conversation with someone new, people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them. That awkward chat you keep replaying? They probably thought it went better than you did.

The worst part: waiting for someone to come to you. It can happen, but you'll find your people faster if you take a few small risks.

📱 Finding people online, and offlineEveryone's feed looks settled. Most people aren't.

Social media can help you find your people, and it can also make it look like everyone has already found theirs:

  • Everyone's stories look like their friend group is perfect.
  • Following people from school helps you know what they're into before you talk.
  • Finding friends through a game or a fan community.
  • Waiting for someone to follow you back, and wondering what it means.

The worst part: feeds show other people's best moments, so it's easy to believe you're the only one still looking.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

You don't need a crew. Start with one person.

Tap one to see how.

🔁 Go where the same people go.

Join one club, team, youth group, or affinity group and keep going, even when it feels awkward at first. The third or fourth time is usually when it gets easier.

❓ Ask questions.

People like talking about what they're into. "How did you get into that?" goes a long way.

🪜 Take the next small step.

If you've talked a few times, try one step up: sit together, text about a class, suggest a plan.

🙋 Be the inviter.

Most people are waiting to be invited. If you do the inviting, you get to start the friendships you want.

🧩 Look for one person, not a whole group.

One good friend can change how school feels. You don't need a big crew.

Try itFind your rung. Then take one step up.

Pick one person you'd like to know better. Check the rungs you've already done. The next one up is your step.

Conversation starters that actually workIt doesn't have to be clever. Just curious.

Comment on something you share, then ask a question:

"That math quiz was rough. Did you get number 6?"
"Is that a Minecraft sticker? What do you build?"
"Do you want to be partners for this?"

It doesn't have to be clever. Friendly and a little curious is enough.

Try itYou've said hi. What's your next move?

Pick what you'd try.

There's a kid in your science class who always draws the same anime you watch. You've never talked.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't actually helpWaiting to be chosen usually doesn't work.
⏳ Waiting to be chosen.

Some people do get swept into friendships. Most people have to reach out a little.

🎭 Trying to be someone else.

Changing yourself to fit a group can work for a while, but it's exhausting. The people worth finding will like the real version: your culture, your accent, your faith, who you like, and how your brain works.

📱 Hiding in your phone.

It's a safe-feeling shield in awkward moments. It also signals "don't talk to me." Try putting it away for a few minutes at lunch.

🧮 Taking one no as the final answer.

If someone's busy or doesn't click, it's one data point. Try again, with them later or with someone else.

What to do about social media right nowUse your phone as a bridge, not a wall.
🔗 Use it as a bridge.
Comment on a post about something you share, then talk about it in person. Online to offline is a great path.
🧭 Look for the real people behind the posts.
Most of your classmates are less sure of their friendships than their stories suggest.
🛡 Stay safe with online-only friends.
Online friends can be real friends. Don't share personal info, and never meet someone from online without a trusted adult knowing.
⏸ Take a scroll break.
If your feed makes you feel more alone, put it down and do one thing in the real world instead.

When to talk to someone

  • You've felt alone at school most days for a long time.
  • You're avoiding school because of it.
  • Loneliness is making you feel hopeless or down most of the time.

Counselors help students find their people all the time, and they know about groups and activities you might not. See Get help below.
