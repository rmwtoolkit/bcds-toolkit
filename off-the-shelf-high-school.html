Part 1 · Understand it

What's behind the counter, and what it does

Sound familiar?Cheap, legal-looking, and badly understood.
  • Smoke shops sell big, flavored "Galaxy Gas" tanks, and people fill balloons at parties.
  • Someone stood up after a few balloons, dropped, and hit their head.
  • Kratom shots are sold for "focus" or "energy," and people use them to get through a double shift or an all-nighter.
  • 7-OH tablets or tianeptine ("gas station heroin") sit by the register.
  • Someone in your family uses kratom for pain or to get off pain pills, and it doesn't feel like a drug to them.

You probably already know these aren't harmless. This goes into the how: what nitrous does to your nerves, why a computer duster can stop a heart, and what the opioid-like products actually are.

8×more emergency room visits for nitrous oxide misuse in Michigan in 2023 than in 2019 (60, up from 7). EMS calls rose from 15 to 78. Most patients were in their 20s and 30s.Vohra and colleagues, CDC MMWR, 2025

How nitrous damages nerves

Dentists use nitrous mixed with oxygen, for minutes, with someone watching. Balloons are pure gas, often dozens in a night. The high is only part of the problem. Nitrous oxide inactivates vitamin B12, and your nerves need B12 to maintain myelin, the insulating coat that lets signals travel.

Dorsal columns: where you are in space

These fibers tell your brain where your feet are without looking. When their myelin breaks down, people feel numbness and tingling in their hands and feet, then walk unsteadily, especially in the dark.

Myelin: the insulation

Without enough working B12, the body can't keep myelin in repair. Signals slow, scramble, or stop. Doctors call this nitrous oxide myeloneuropathy.

SWART AND COLLEAGUES, 2021

Doctors in Sydney described 20 patients with nerve damage from nitrous, average age 24. Spinal MRI showed dorsal column damage in all 20, and seven could not walk when they arrived. Their average B12 blood test looked normal, which is one reason the damage gets missed. Most patients who were followed up still had symptoms.

Other inhalantsDusters can stop a heart on the first try.
BASS, 1970

A forensic pathologist described 110 sudden deaths in young people who had been sniffing solvents and aerosols, and coined the term sudden sniffing death. The most likely cause: the chemicals make the heart oversensitive to adrenaline, so a scare, a chase, or exercise can trigger a fatal rhythm.

Computer dusters, spray paint, and lighter fluid also cut oxygen to the brain with every hit. Judgment and balance drop within seconds, which is why falls and head injuries are so common.

Gas from a nitrous tank or duster comes out freezing cold and can cause frostbite to the lips, mouth, and throat.

Kratom, 7-OH, and tianeptineOpioid effects, gas-station packaging.
Kratom
A plant from Southeast Asia. Small amounts act like a stimulant; larger amounts act on opioid receptors. Regular use can lead to dependence, with flu-like withdrawal.
7-OH
A kratom chemical concentrated in a lab. It binds the same mu-opioid receptors as morphine and may be up to 13 times as potent. In July 2025, the FDA recommended making it a controlled substance.
Tianeptine
Not approved for any use in the U.S. It acts on the opioid system, and the FDA has warned against it repeatedly.
COUNTS AND COLLEAGUES, CDC MMWR, 2024

Between June and November 2023, the New Jersey poison center tracked 17 people with severe illness after taking tianeptine products, mostly the brand Neptune's Fix. Thirteen went to intensive care and seven needed a breathing tube. Lab tests found synthetic cannabinoids in the product that were not on the label.

Some adults use kratom to manage pain or to cut back on opioids, and the research on that is still limited. If someone in your family does, the safest step is talking it through with a doctor, not judging them.

Legal vs. testedSold in a store. Rarely tested.
Reviewed?A medicine from a pharmacy is FDA-reviewed for safety and dose. A product from the counter usually isn't.
LabelPharmacy: an accurate dose. Counter: strength can vary a lot, and ingredients may be missing from the label.
WarningsPharmacy: clear instructions. Counter: often just "not for human consumption."
Who's sellingA pharmacist who can answer questions, or whoever is working the register.

In March 2025 the FDA warned people not to inhale flavored nitrous products like Galaxy Gas after a rise in reports of injuries. Being on a shelf only means nobody has stopped it yet.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Reading past the marketing

What actually helps

Ask what it does, not where it's sold.

Tap each one.

1 Decode the label.

"Natural," "botanical," "legal," and "for culinary use" are sales words. "Not for human consumption" usually means "we don't want to be responsible."

2 Know the early warning signs.

After nitrous: numbness, tingling, weak or clumsy legs. After kratom, 7-OH, or tianeptine: needing more, feeling sick without it. Either one means see a doctor soon, and tell them what you've been using.

3 If someone is using near you, keep them sitting.

Most inhalant injuries happen when people stand up, faint, and fall. Don't chase or startle someone who's been sniffing.

4 Know when to call 911.

Won't wake up, slow or no breathing, blue lips, a seizure, chest pain, or a head injury. With kratom, 7-OH, or tianeptine, give naloxone if you have it.

5 Have your line.

"That stuff wrecks your nerves." Short and true.

6 Talk to someone.

Counselors and nurses help with this, including when it's a family member who uses.

Try itThree real moments. What's your move?

Pick a move. There may be more than one good one.

At a party, people are passing balloons from a Galaxy Gas tank. Andre has done six and is getting up to walk to the kitchen.

Your coworker Lin takes 7-OH tablets every day "for stress" and feels awful when she skips a day.

Your friend Jordan has been doing whippets most weekends and mentions his toes have been tingling for a couple of weeks.

The best answer is the one you'll actually do.

In a groupTake apart the package.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. Imagine a bright package: "All natural. Focus and calm. Lab tested. Not for human consumption."
  2. In small groups, list what the package wants you to believe, what it leaves out, and who benefits.
  3. Then rewrite the label so it's honest, using what you learned on this page.
  4. Share a few. Which honest label would make people stop and think?
Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Why would a product say "not for human consumption" when it's obviously meant to be used?
  • Why do you think these products are sold at gas stations and smoke shops instead of pharmacies?
  • How should someone talk to a family member who uses kratom for pain?
Tip for the person leading

Treat this as a media literacy activity. Keep it about products and claims, not personal use. Some students have family members who use kratom for pain or recovery, so keep it non-judgmental.

Call 911 right away if someone

  • Passes out and won't wake up, or isn't breathing normally.
  • Has blue lips, a seizure, chest pain, or an irregular heartbeat.
  • Hits their head when they fall.

Give naloxone if opioid-like products were involved. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222. See Get help below.
