Part 1 · Understand it

Pressure, scams, and what's really at stake

Sound familiar?You never owe anyone a picture.
  • Someone you're dating said "if you trusted me, you would."
  • They sent one first, and now it feels like you owe them.
  • Someone you'd never met followed you, flirted hard, and asked for a photo within the hour.
  • A photo of someone in your grade is going around, and people keep asking if you've seen it.
  • You already sent something, and now you can't stop thinking about where it is.

There are two different situations here. One is pressure from someone you know. The other is a scam run by strangers, often from overseas, built to get one image and then money. Both are someone else doing something wrong. Both have a way out.

How a sextortion scam runs

Steps 1 to 3: the setup

The account often looks like someone your age, often a girl if the target is a guy. The photo they send first is usually stolen. Speed is a red flag: real people rarely go from hello to nudes in an hour.

Steps 4 and 5: the trap

Once they have an image, the tone flips. They show screenshots of your followers, family, or school and demand payment. Paying almost never ends it. It tells them you'll pay again.

The exit: stop, save, block, tell

Stop responding. Save the messages and the account name. Block and report. Tell an adult you trust. The FBI's guidance is the same: get help before you send money or anything else.

The researchWhat the numbers show.
Sextortion is mostly aimed at teen boys.
Between October 2021 and March 2023, the FBI and Homeland Security Investigations received more than 13,000 reports of financial sextortion of minors, involving at least 12,600 victims, mostly boys. From August 2022 to August 2023, NCMEC received an average of 812 sextortion reports a week, most of them financial and mostly involving boys 14 to 17. By 2025 it was getting about 137 financial sextortion reports a day.
Sending is less common than people think.
A 2018 review of 39 studies with more than 110,000 young people found about 15% had sent a sext and about 27% had received one. "Everyone does it" is not true.
Trust breaks a lot.
Elizabeth Englander and Meghan McCoy at Bridgewater State surveyed 1,320 Massachusetts students. In 2015, almost half of those who had sent a sexual image said it had been passed on without their consent.
Pressure is common.
In Englander's 2015 survey of college students, most who had sexted reported at least some pressure or coercion. Pressure doesn't make it your fault. It makes it the other person's responsibility.
The law, plainlyWhat's illegal, and who's protected.

Under 18. Sexual images of anyone under 18 are illegal under federal and Massachusetts law to create, ask for, keep, or share, even when everyone agreed. That includes a photo of yourself.

Massachusetts since 2024. A state law signed in June 2024 made it a crime to share someone's intimate images without consent, including AI-generated fakes, and named coercive control as a form of abuse. For teens caught sexting, it created an education-based diversion program instead of prosecution under child pornography laws.

Federal, since 2025. The TAKE IT DOWN Act made publishing intimate images without consent a federal crime. Since May 2026, covered platforms must remove reported images within 48 hours.

If you're being threatened, the law treats you as the victim. The person threatening you is the one committing a crime.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Before you send, and if you already did

Read the pressure

How someone takes no tells you who they are.

Someone who respects you hears no once. Pressure usually shows up in one of four forms.

Trust as a test"If you trusted me, you would." Trust doesn't require handing someone something they could use against you.
Owed"I sent one, your turn." A photo you didn't ask for creates no debt.
Wearing you downAsking again and again until yes feels easier than no. That's pressure, not interest.
Promises"I'll delete it." "It disappears." Neither is something they can guarantee.
Try itPick the no you'd actually use.

Pick the ones that sound like you, or write your own. Nothing is saved.

If something you sent is being used against youDon't pay. Don't send more. Tell someone.
  1. Stop responding. Don't negotiate, don't send more, and don't pay. Paying usually leads to more demands.
  2. Save evidence. Screenshot the messages, the account name, and any payment details before you block.
  3. Block and report the account on the app.
  4. Tell an adult you trust, today. A parent or relative, counselor, school nurse, or coach. If home isn't the right place for this conversation, school staff can help. You are not in trouble.
  5. Report it. NCMEC's CyberTipline takes reports at report.cybertip.org or 1-800-843-5678.
  6. Get it taken down. NCMEC's free Take It Down service helps remove images of people under 18 from participating sites, without you having to send the image to anyone.
Try itFour real situations. What's your move?

Your girlfriend or boyfriend of two months says, "If you trusted me, you'd send one."

An account you don't know follows you, flirts hard, sends a photo, and asks for one back. It's been 40 minutes.

You sent a photo. Now the account is threatening to send it to your followers unless you pay $300 in gift cards.

A friend shows you a nude of a girl in your grade that's been going around.

There's usually more than one decent answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths that keep people stuckWhat actually happens.
MYTH If I pay, it ends.

Payment usually leads to a bigger demand. The FBI's advice is to stop and get help before paying anything.

MYTH If I tell, I'll get in trouble.

Being pressured or tricked isn't a crime. Adults, schools, NCMEC and police treat the person being threatened as the victim.

MYTH This only happens to girls.

Financial sextortion mostly targets teen boys. Anyone can be targeted.

MYTH A disappearing app keeps it safe.

Screenshots and screen recordings exist. Once it's sent, you can't control where it goes.

Get help right away if

  • Someone is threatening to share a photo of you.
  • Someone is asking for money, more photos, or anything else in exchange for not sharing.
  • An adult is asking you for photos.
  • You feel hopeless or like there's no way out.

This is not your fault, and it can be handled. Tell a trusted adult. NCMEC CyberTipline: 1-800-843-5678.

If you're thinking about hurting yourself, call or text 988 now. See Get help below.
