Part 1 · Understand it

What's going on in the chat

Group chats are where a lot of friendship happens now. They're also where a lot of friendship drama starts. If a text has ever ruined your night, you are in very good company.

Sound familiar?A text ruined your night? You're in good company.
  • You sent something and got "k" or "ok." back, and spent an hour wondering what you did.
  • You posted in the chat and nobody answered.
  • You found out there's a second chat for the sleepover, and you're not in it.
  • Someone made a joke about you, and everyone added laughing emojis.
  • Somebody took a screenshot of what you said and showed it to people at lunch.
  • You watched it happen to someone else and didn't know what to do.

All of these count. You don't have to call it bullying for it to hurt.

Misread, left out, piled on.

Tap each one.

📱Misread

A short reply, a period, a slow answer, or no answer at all. Your brain decides it means something bad, before you have any real information.

🚪Left out

The side chat, the plans you hear about later, the inside joke from a night you weren't there. Sometimes it's on purpose. Often it isn't.

🧱Piled on

One person becomes the target and everyone else joins in with comments, emojis, or screenshots. It can go from zero to the whole grade in minutes.

Why it hits so hardTexts have no tone. Your brain fills in the worst.
Texts have no tone, so your brain fills it in.
In person you get a face, a voice, timing. In a text you get a few letters. When you're tired, stressed, or already worried about a friendship, your brain tends to fill in the worst version. "ok." might mean annoyed. It might also mean typing with one thumb in the car.
Being left out really does hurt.
Research suggests being left out activates some of the same brain systems as physical pain. That's why finding a chat you're not in can feel like getting hit. Your brain is treating belonging as something that matters, because it does.
It's public, and it lasts.
An awkward moment in the hallway fades. A message can be reread, screenshotted, and forwarded. Knowing other people can see it makes everything feel bigger.
🧱Why pile-ons spread so fastOne person stepping out can turn it around.
  • Joining feels safer than standing out. People laugh along so they aren't next.
  • Everyone assumes someone else will say something. So nobody does.
  • Screens make it feel less real. It's easy to type things you would never say to someone's face.

The worst part: the person being targeted is reading every single message, usually alone, usually at night.

The hopeful part: most people in a pile-on aren't cruel, they're going along. One person stepping out can change the direction.

📱It's bigger than the group chatApps show you exactly what you're missing.

The chat is only one place this happens. The same misreads, left-out moments, and pile-ons show up everywhere online:

  • Seeing everyone in one spot on Snap Map when you thought nobody was doing anything.
  • Watching stories from a hangout you didn't know about.
  • A friend letting a streak die, or leaving your snap on "opened" for hours.
  • A game party or Discord call everyone is in except you.
  • Comments under a post that start as jokes and turn into a pile-on.

The worst part: apps show you exactly what you're missing, in real time, with photos. It's like being left out over and over.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Finding your way through it

What actually helps

Read it three times before you react.

Tap one to see how.

📖 The Three Reads.

When a text makes your stomach drop, read it three times before reacting. First, just the words, nothing added. Second, in a flat robot voice. Third, as if your best friend sent it. A lot of "mean" texts stop sounding mean by the third read.

❓ Ask instead of assuming.

If you still can't tell, check it out. Short and light works best:

"hey, couldn't tell your tone there. we good?"
"wait, joking or actually annoyed? genuinely can't tell lol"

🗣 Take it out of the group.

Real conversations go better one on one, and better in person or on a call than over text. A group chat is the worst place to work something out, because everyone's watching.

🔕 Mute is allowed.

You can mute a chat for an evening, turn off notifications, or leave. Catching up tomorrow is fine. You don't have to be on call for every message.

What doesn't actually helpThese keep the drama going.
🔥 Replying while you're heated.

Your thumbs move faster than your judgment. Run the Screenshot Test below first.

🔍 Investigating.

Scrolling back through old messages, checking who's online, asking three other people what they think it meant. It feels like getting answers. Mostly it keeps the worry going.

📣 Vague posts.

Posting "some people are so fake" so the right person sees it. Everyone guesses who it's about, and now there are two problems.

😐 Pretending it doesn't bother you.

You don't have to make a scene. You also don't have to act fine. Telling one person the real version helps a lot.

Try itWould you be okay if this got screenshotted?

Type the reply you want to send. It stays on this page and goes nowhere.

Not replying is a reply option. "Let me think about it" counts too.

🚪When you're left outSort the facts from the guesses.
  1. Name the feeling. Hurt, embarrassed, jealous, lonely. Naming it, even just in your head, turns the volume down a little.
  2. Sort facts from guesses. Fact: there's a chat I'm not in. Guess: they all secretly hate me. Guesses feel like facts when you're hurt.
  3. Pick a move. Talk to the one friend you trust most, one on one. Make your own plan and invite someone. Spend time with your other people. Or let it go, if it's a one-time thing.

If you want to bring it up, keep it short, say how it felt, and ask a real question:

"hey, I saw there was a chat for the sleepover. I felt kinda left out. was that on purpose or just a mistake?"
🧱When a pile-on startsTarget or watching, you have moves.

If you're the target

  • Don't reply while it's happening. Replies keep it going.
  • Screenshot it so there's a record.
  • Mute or leave the chat. You're allowed.
  • Tell an adult you trust, even if you worry it'll make it worse.
  • Reach out to one person who's on your side.

If you're watching

  • Don't add anything, not even an emoji.
  • DM the person: "that wasn't okay. you good?"
  • Say "ok can we not," or change the subject.
  • Leave the chat if it won't stop.
  • Tell an adult. That's being a good friend, not snitching.

If you were part of it: stop adding, delete what you can, and apologize to the person directly. Owning it is hard, and it matters.

What to do about social media right nowStreaks aren't friendship scores.
📍 Turn on Ghost Mode, or stop checking the map.
You don't need a live feed of where everyone is. Not knowing is often easier than knowing.
⏱ Streaks aren't friendship scores.
Apps make streaks feel important so you keep opening them. A dropped streak usually means someone was busy.
🔕 Mute stories for a while.
Muting is private. Nobody gets notified, and you get a break.
🧘 Notice the after-feeling.
If you feel worse every time you open an app, that's information. Try a set time to check instead of all day.

Get an adult involved right away if a chat includes:

  • Threats to hurt someone, or talk about a weapon
  • Someone saying they want to hurt themselves or die, even as a "joke"
  • Private, embarrassing, or undressed photos of anyone
  • The same person getting targeted again and again

A counselor, teacher, coach, or parent can help. See Get help below.

What this might mean

Your brain is trying to solve a puzzle with missing pieces, so it keeps going back for more clues. Checking feels like it will help. It usually makes the worry louder.

What might help right now

  • 📵Put the phone in another room for 30 minutes. Set a timer if that helps.
  • 📖Do the Three Reads on the message one time, then stop rereading.
  • ❓If you really need to know, ask: "couldn't tell your tone, we good?"
  • 🌬Breathe in for 4, out for 6, five times. A longer exhale tells your body to settle.

What this might mean

Belonging matters to everyone, and your brain treats being left out as a real hurt. It doesn't mean you're unlikable. It means you care about these people.

What might help right now

  • ✍️Write down what you know for sure, and separately, what you're guessing.
  • 💬Text someone who isn't involved, just to talk about anything.
  • 🙈Mute the stories for tonight. You don't have to watch it.
  • 🗣Tell someone the real version of how you feel, not the "it's fine" version.

What this might mean

Anger usually shows up when something crossed a line. That's useful information. It's also the feeling most likely to make you send something you regret.

What might help right now

  • 📝Type the message you want to send in the Screenshot Test above. Don't send it.
  • 🏃Move your body: a walk, a run, shooting hoops, loud music.
  • ⏳Wait until tomorrow before you respond to anyone involved.
  • 🧑‍🏫If someone was targeted, tell an adult. Anger at unfairness is a good reason to speak up.

What this might mean

When something private gets shared, it can feel like everyone is looking at you. It feels huge right now. Most people move on much faster than it seems from the inside.

What might help right now

  • 📸Screenshot what was shared, so there's a record, then stop looking at it.
  • 🧑‍🏫Tell a trusted adult. If a photo of you was shared, they can help get it taken down.
  • 🤝Ask one friend to stick close to you at school tomorrow.
  • 🫶Remind yourself: one moment is not who you are.

What this might mean

Some distance is starting to help. Better days don't have to be perfect days to count.

What might help right now

  • 🔎Notice what helped today, so you can do it again.
  • 💛Spend some time with the people who feel easy to be around.
  • 🤲If someone else is going through it, you know what helps now. You could check on them.
