Part 1 · Understand it

What's really going on

Sound familiar?You didn't go looking. It showed up anyway.
  • Something popped up on a game, a link, or a video site that you never searched for.
  • An older kid held out a phone and said, "Want to see something?"
  • Kids at school joke about porn, and you laughed even though you weren't sure what they meant.
  • You saw something that felt confusing, gross, or scary, and it keeps replaying in your head.
  • You got curious and looked something up.
  • You're worried you'll get in trouble if anyone finds out.

If any of this happened to you, you're far from alone, and you're not a bad kid. Lots of kids run into this before anyone talks to them about it.

How common it is

Porn is videos or pictures of people without clothes doing sexual things. It's made for adults, and it's made to make money. In a big 2022 survey, more than half of teens had seen it by age 13, often by accident.

It's like pro wrestling

Ever watched pro wrestling? The wrestlers are real athletes, but the match is a show. The ending is planned, the moves are practiced, and the wrestlers work together so nobody gets hurt. That's why they tell kids, "Don't try this at home." Porn works the same way.

Tap each one.

🎬It's scripted

What happens is planned before the camera turns on, the same way a wrestling match is.

🎭They're performers

They're acting for a camera. What they seem to feel may not be what they feel.

✂️It's edited

Lights, makeup, camera angles, and a lot of cutting. Real bodies and real people don't look or act like that.

🚫No "don't try this"

Wrestling warns you. Porn doesn't, so some people think it shows how things are supposed to go.

What got left out:

  • talking
  • asking
  • being kind
  • being nervous
  • laughing
  • caring how the other person feels
  • taking your time
🧠 Why it sticks in your headYour brain holds on to anything shocking.

Brains pay extra attention to things that are new or surprising, so something you saw for a few seconds can replay for days. That's how brains work, and it fades.

Being curious about bodies and sex is a normal part of growing up. Having seen something doesn't make you gross or bad.

A lot of it shows people treated badlyOn screen it looks fine. In real life it would hurt.

A lot of porn shows people being rough or mean and acting like it's fine. Teens who had seen porn were more likely to have seen people getting hurt than to have seen anyone ask, "Is this okay?"

In real life, being rough with someone is hurting them. Respect means asking, listening, and stopping when someone says no or seems unsure.

What real relationships look likeKind, honest, and nobody's in a rush.
🤝 Kind
You treat each other the way you'd treat a good friend.
🗣 Talking
You can say what you like, what you don't, and what makes you nervous.
✋ Asking
Even for small things, like holding hands. Either person can say no, any time, and that's respected.
🔒 Private stays private
No screenshots, no spreading stories.
🐢 No rush
There's no deadline for dating or anything else. Plenty of people your age aren't interested yet, and that's normal.
💛 Anyone you like
Whether you like boys, girls, both, or you're not sure yet, the same rules apply.
For guys especiallyNobody your age knows everything. Asking is smart.

Guys hear a lot of "real guys watch this" and "you should already know." Boys are more likely to go looking for porn, so its ideas reach them more often.

A good guy asks before he acts, listens when someone says no, and doesn't treat anyone like an object. Having questions is normal, and asking an adult is a strong move.

Your family, your cultureFamilies talk about this in different ways.

Some families talk about bodies and sex openly. Some never do. Some have rules from their faith or culture. All of that is real, and you don't have to figure this out alone.

  • Who in your family, or outside it, could you ask a question?
  • What do the adults who raise you hope you'll know about relationships?

If talking at home doesn't feel safe or possible, a school counselor can help you find someone who is.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

If you see it, or someone shows you

What actually helps

You can always close it and tell someone.

Tap one to see how.

📵 Close it.

Close the page or put the phone down. You don't have to finish watching anything.

🤼 Remember it's a show.

Like wrestling, it's staged. It doesn't show how real people treat each other.

🙅 "Nah, I'm good."

If someone tries to show you, you can say no, look away, and walk off. No speech needed.

🗣 Tell an adult you trust.

A parent or caregiver, a counselor, a relative. Seeing something you didn't choose isn't your fault, and most adults are glad you told them.

🛡 Adults never get a pass.

If an adult or much older kid shows you porn, sends you sexual pictures, or asks for pictures of you, tell a trusted adult right away. That's never okay, and it's never your fault.

🔒 Ask for a filter.

If stuff keeps popping up, you can ask a parent or caregiver to help set up filters. That's smart, and you're not in trouble for asking.

No. Teens were much more likely to say they learned a lot from a parent, caregiver, or trusted adult than from porn. The grown-ups in your life matter.

👥 Talk about itTalk it over.
  • How is porn like pro wrestling?
  • Where do kids your age learn about crushes and relationships? Which of those can you trust?
  • What makes a good friend or a good partner?
Tip for the person leading

Keep it calm and general: nobody says what they've seen. Expect some giggles and let them pass. If a student tells you an adult or older kid showed them porn or asked for pictures, follow your school's reporting steps; you're a mandated reporter.

When to tell an adult right away

  • An adult or much older kid shows you porn, sends you sexual pictures, or asks for pictures of you.
  • Someone asks you to keep a secret about anything like this.
  • Someone touches you in a way that feels wrong, or asks you to touch them.
  • Something you saw keeps scaring you or making you feel sick.

Tell a parent or caregiver, a counselor, or another adult you trust today. You can call or text 988 any time, or call Childhelp at 1-800-422-4453. If someone is in danger right now, call 911.
