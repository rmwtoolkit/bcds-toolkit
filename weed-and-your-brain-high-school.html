Part 1 · Understand it

THC and a brain still under construction

Sound familiar?You've heard both extremes. The science is more specific.
  • It's legal for adults in Massachusetts, so the stores, the ads, and the smell are just part of the landscape.
  • Someone you know uses a cart every night to fall asleep, or to take the edge off before a test.
  • A friend is high most days and still gets good grades, so how bad can it be?
  • Someone in your family uses it, maybe with a medical card, and talks about it very differently than your school does.
  • A summer job, a team, or a future employer drug tests, and you're not sure how long THC stays in your system.

This lesson skips the scare stories. It covers how THC works, what the biggest studies found, and where the evidence is honestly mixed.

74%of U.S. high school seniors did not use cannabis in the past year. For 10th graders, it was 84%.Monitoring the Future, 2025

Your brain already has a cannabis system

Your brain makes its own cannabinoids, like anandamide, on demand. They act on CB1 receptors to fine-tune signals between neurons, then get broken down quickly. THC fits the same receptors, but it lasts for hours and hits everywhere at once. CB1 receptors are densest in the areas below.

Hippocampus: memory

Saving new memories depends on precise timing between neurons. THC blurs it. In regular users, memory and attention problems can last for days after the last use, and usually improve after several weeks without it.

Prefrontal cortex: planning and focus

Still maturing into your mid-20s. During the teen years, your own cannabinoid system helps decide which connections get strengthened and which get trimmed. That's the main reason researchers worry more about teen use than adult use.

Striatum: reward and habit

Part of the brain's reward circuit. Repeated THC trains the habit and builds tolerance, so the same amount does less over time.

Amygdala: fear and anxiety

THC works in two directions here. Low doses can feel calming. High doses are more likely to trigger anxiety, a racing heart, and paranoia.

Cerebellum: coordination and timing

Fine motor control and timing. This is part of why reaction time and lane control suffer when someone drives high.

StrengthAverage THC roughly doubled in a decade.
CHANDRA, ELSOHLY, AND COLLEAGUES, 2019

Average THC in cannabis seized in the U.S. nearly doubled in ten years, from 8.9% in 2008 to 17.1% in 2017. Concentrates, the kind in carts and dabs, averaged 55.7% THC.

DI FORTI AND COLLEAGUES, 2019

Across 11 cities in Europe and Brazil, researchers compared 901 people with a first episode of psychosis to 1,237 people without. Daily cannabis users had about 3 times the odds of psychosis. Daily users of high-potency cannabis (over 10% THC) had nearly 5 times the odds.

McDONALD AND COLLEAGUES, 2024

Following 11,363 young people in Ontario, teens aged 12 to 19 who used cannabis were about 11 times as likely to be diagnosed with a psychotic disorder over the next several years. The study found no clear link for young adults aged 20 to 33, which points to the teen years as the sensitive window.

Most teens who use won't develop psychosis, and these studies show strong links, not proof of cause. The risk rises with higher potency, daily use, a younger start, and a family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder. Nobody can tell in advance whose brain will react this way.

Thinking and memoryWhere the research agrees, and where it doesn't.
WADE AND COLLEAGUES, 2026 (ABCD STUDY)

The largest U.S. study so far followed 11,036 kids from age 9 or 10 to 16 or 17. Before they started using, teens who later used cannabis kept pace with their peers. After they started, gains in memory, attention, language, and processing speed slowed while non-users kept improving. In a subgroup tested with hair samples, THC exposure was tied to worse memory; CBD exposure was not. The differences were modest, and the design shows a link, not cause.

MEIER AND COLLEAGUES, 2012 (DUNEDIN STUDY)

Following 1,037 New Zealanders from birth to age 38, people who used heavily and persistently starting in adolescence lost about 8 IQ points between 13 and 38.

JACKSON AND COLLEAGUES, 2016 (TWIN STUDIES)

Comparing twins in two U.S. samples (789 and 2,277 people), teens who used cannabis did score lower over time, but so did their twins who did not use. That suggests shared family or genetic factors explain part of the IQ difference.

The honest summary: frequent teen use is linked to worse thinking and memory, and the signal is strongest with heavy use and an early start. How much is caused by the cannabis itself is still debated. That's a reason for caution, not a reason to panic.

Getting hookedAbout 3 in 10 past-year users had a use disorder.
HASIN AND COLLEAGUES, 2015

In a national survey of more than 36,000 U.S. adults, 30.6% of people who had used cannabis in the past year met criteria for a cannabis use disorder: wanting to cut back and not being able to, needing more for the same effect, or letting it crowd out other things.

People who start before 18 are several times more likely to develop a use disorder than people who start as adults (CDC). Withdrawal is real, too: irritability, poor sleep, vivid dreams, and low appetite, usually worst in the first week and fading over two to three.

Heavy, frequent use can also cause cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: repeated bouts of severe vomiting and stomach pain. Hot showers relieve it for a while, which is one way doctors spot it. The only fix is stopping.

Edibles and cartsStrength and timing are where most bad nights start.

Edibles can take up to two hours to kick in, and then last six hours or more. The classic mistake is "it's not working," then a second dose, then both hit together. Carts and dabs deliver concentrate fast, at several times the strength of flower.

Delta-8, THC-O, and "hemp-derived" gummies and vapes from gas stations and online shops still get you high. They're loosely regulated, and the FDA has warned about poisonings and mislabeled products.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Making your own call

What actually helps

Know what it's doing for you. Know what it costs.

Tap each one.

1 Know your why.

If you use, or are thinking about it, name the job you want it to do: sleep, calm, fun, fitting in. Then ask what else does that job. If the answer is "calm," note that regular use is linked with more anxiety over time, and withdrawal makes anxiety worse in the short run.

2 Know the law where you live.

In Massachusetts, buying and having cannabis is legal only at 21. Under 21 it's against the law, and schools have their own rules on top of that. Driving high is OUI. Many employers and teams test, and THC can show up for days to weeks in regular users.

3 Cutting back? Plan for week one.

Expect irritability, rough sleep, vivid dreams, and less appetite, usually peaking in the first week. Plan for it: exercise, a sleep routine, fewer triggers, and someone who knows you're trying. A counselor can help you build the plan.

4 Never drive or ride high.

THC slows reaction time and makes it harder to stay in your lane. Call your exit-plan person, a sober friend, or a rideshare.

5 Look after someone who's too high.

Quiet spot, water, slow breathing, and the reminder that it will pass. Stay with them. Call 911 for chest pain, fainting, a seizure, or if they can't stay awake. If a young child eats an edible, call Poison Control right away.

6 Talk to someone.

Counselors and nurses help students with this all the time. If you're worried about what stays private, ask that first.

Gray area · 1 of 3If it's legal at 21, it should be legal at 18.

Agree, disagree, or it depends? Vote first.

What the research says

The Ontario study found a strong link between cannabis and psychosis for 12 to 19 year olds, and no clear link for people 20 and up. Brain maturation continues into the mid-20s. Where to draw the line is a values question, but the biology isn't the same at 18 and 25.

Gray area · 2 of 3A friend who uses daily but gets good grades is fine.

Agree, disagree, or it depends? Vote first.

What the research says

Grades are one measure. Effects on memory, motivation, sleep, and mood can be hard to see from outside. Daily use is also the pattern most linked to dependence and psychosis. "Fine" and "unaffected" aren't the same thing.

Gray area · 3 of 3Weed is safer than alcohol.

Agree, disagree, or it depends? Vote first.

What the research says

It depends on the risk. A cannabis overdose doesn't stop breathing the way an alcohol overdose can. Cannabis carries other risks: psychosis, dependence, impaired driving, and, for teens, possible effects on memory and attention. "Safer" depends on which harm you're asking about, and mixing the two makes both worse.

Talk about itTalk it over.
  • Which statement was hardest to vote on? Why?
  • How do the messages you've heard about weed differ between school, family, online, and friends?
  • What would you want a younger sibling or cousin to know?
Tip for the person leading

Keep questions about "people our age," never about "you." Nobody should have to share whether they use. Families and cultures view cannabis very differently, including for medical use, so leave room for that.

Get help right away if

  • Someone has chest pain, or their heart won't slow down.
  • Someone faints, has a seizure, or can't stay awake.
  • Someone keeps vomiting and can't stop.
  • Someone is seeing or hearing things, or is so paranoid they could hurt themselves or someone else.
  • A young child ate an edible.

Call 911 for an emergency. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222. See Get help below.
