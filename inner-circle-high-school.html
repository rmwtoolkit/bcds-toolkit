Part 1 · Understand it

How power works in a friend group

Sound familiar?Nobody said anything. Everybody knew.
  • The group chat got a new name, and you weren't added to the new one.
  • One person quietly decides who's in the prom group, the formal table, or the car.
  • A friend is warm when it's just you two and cold the minute the group shows up.
  • Something you said in confidence came back to you as a screenshot.
  • "You've changed since you started hanging out with them."
  • The guys roast someone every day, and everyone knows who's at the bottom this week.
  • You're trans or nonbinary, and you can feel the group deciding which "side" you belong on.
  • You got called "fake," "soft," or "doing too much" for running for something or speaking up in class.
  • You've done some of this yourself, and part of you knows it.

You've probably seen this since middle school. By now you may know the patterns but still feel stuck in them. This page goes further: what researchers have actually measured, how it plays out for girls, guys, and nonbinary students, where the "girls are mean" idea gets it wrong, and what to do.

Popular and liked are different things

When researchers ask students "Who's popular?" and then "Who do you like?", the lists overlap but don't match. One team followed about 300 students every year from 5th to 12th grade. Leaving people out and spreading rumors raised how popular a student seemed, and lowered how much people actually liked them. That trade-off was strongest for girls, and the popularity boost peaked during the move from middle school to high school.

Leaving out on purposePlans, chats, and seats arranged so one person knows they weren't included.
Rumors and screenshotsPrivate things made public, or stories started, to change how people see someone.
The freeze-outSilence, eye rolls, being left on read. Nobody has to say a word.
Friendship as a threat"If you hang out with her, we're done." Loyalty used to control.

Researchers call these moves relational aggression. Nicki Crick and Jennifer Grotpeter named it in 1995: hurting someone through their relationships instead of with fists.

Across the gender spectrumSame power game. Different moves.
In a lot of girls' groups
Friendships tend to be close and intense, so the moves are quieter: exclusion, secrets, the freeze-out. Lisa Damour notes that many girls are taught to be nice before honest, so conflict goes underground.
In a lot of guys' groups
Groups are often bigger with a clearer pecking order, so power is louder: roasting that always lands on the same person, ranking, and getting quietly dropped from the game, the lift, or the chat. Sociologist C.J. Pascoe spent 18 months in a California high school and found that guys used anti-gay insults mostly to police who counted as a "real guy," whatever anyone's orientation. Calling someone "soft" or "gay" is a power move.
In mixed groups
Dating gets pulled in. A couple can run the group, a breakup can split it, and a crush can be used as leverage.
For trans and nonbinary students
Many friend groups are quietly sorted by gender, so a trans or nonbinary student can end up outside both. Their identity can become gossip. Outing someone, or sharing their old name or photos, is one of the most harmful forms of relational aggression.

These are patterns counselors and researchers see often, not rules. Any of them shows up in any group, and most people of every gender don't run their friendships this way.

The claims vs. the research"Girls are just mean," and other things that aren't quite true.
"Girls are meaner than boys."
A review of 148 studies and about 74,000 kids found girls use this kind of aggression only slightly more than boys, a difference the authors called trivial. Boys do it too, and they also fight physically more. What's different is that for many girls, relational moves are a bigger share of how conflict shows up, and girls tend to report more distress when friendships go wrong.
"The popular kids have it made."
A study that followed students from 13 to 23 found that kids who chased status early by acting older than their age were seen as cool at 13. By their early 20s, they had more trouble in close relationships and more problems with alcohol, drugs, and the law than their peers.
"Venting every night with your best friend helps."
Partly. Psychologist Amanda Rose calls it co-rumination: going over the same problem again and again. Friends who do it feel closer, and they also report more anxiety and low mood, and girls do it more. Talk it through, then go do something.
Why it hurts this muchBeing left out isn't a small thing to your brain.

In a 2003 study, people played an online game of catch, and partway through the other players stopped throwing to them. Brain scans showed activity in an area that also responds to physical pain, and it tracked how upset people said they felt. Scientists still argue about how close the overlap really is, but the basic point holds up: exclusion registers as a threat.

That was a game with strangers. When it's your friends, and it's in front of the whole grade, it makes sense that it takes over your week.

Why nobody says anythingEverybody's waiting to see who goes first.

In a Canadian study, researchers recorded kids at recess. Other kids were around for 88% of bullying episodes and stepped in during only 19%. When someone did step in, it stopped the bullying 57% of the time.

In a lot of groups, several people privately think it's wrong, and each assumes they're the only one. So the group looks united when it isn't.

Where the gender rules come fromGirls hear "be nice." Guys hear "be tough." Both skip "be honest."

This part comes from clinicians and writers rather than one big study. Psychologist Lisa Damour, writing from years of work with teen girls in Untangled, argues that girls often hear only two options: be a pushover or be cruel. Direct, respectful disagreement gets taught less, so conflict goes underground, into chats, seating, and silence. Rachel Simmons made a similar case in Odd Girl Out.

Guys often get the opposite script: closeness is weak, and toughness wins. Hurt comes out as roasting and ranking instead of a direct "that bothered me." Students who don't fit either script, or who are outside the binary, catch pressure from both sets of rules.

It shows up as double standards too. An ambitious boy is a leader; an ambitious girl is "doing too much." A boy who cries is "soft." Girls can be angry, competitive, and loud. Guys can be gentle and close with their friends. What hurts people is using friendship as the weapon.

What a strong friendship looks likeYou can relax in it.
Safe
You don't check the chat to see if you're still in.
Open
Other friendships aren't treated as betrayal.
Direct
Problems get raised with the person, not with everyone else.
Fair
Power moves around. No one person always runs the plans or the mood.
Room for everyone
Trans and nonbinary friends, guys who'd rather draw than play ball, girls who are loud, students of color in a mostly white school, someone who just moved here, the only one of their faith in the group. Some people get extra pressure to fit the group's idea of how a girl or a guy should act. A strong group doesn't make them pay for being themselves.

What loyalty and closeness mean can depend on your family and culture. In some families, friends become family. In others, private things stay private. Worth asking: what were you taught about friendship, and what do you want to keep?

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Keeping your place without taking someone else's

What actually helps

Not a doormat. Not a bully. Assertive.

Tap each one.

1 Use the third option.

Assertive means standing up for yourself while respecting the other person. "I'm not okay with that" works without being mean back.

2 Go to the person, not the group.

"I noticed I wasn't in the new chat. Is something going on with us?" One on one, calm, short. It takes the audience out of it.

3 Let it stop with you.

Rumors and screenshots need people to pass them on. You can be the dead end.

4 Look where you want to go.

Mountain bikers learn that if you stare at the rock, you ride straight into it. If all your energy goes into watching the group that shut you out, that's where you'll keep ending up. Put your eyes on the people who make you feel like yourself.

5 Keep more than one circle.

Team, job, club, youth group, neighborhood, cousins, online friends. If one group goes cold, you still have somewhere to stand.

6 Pull someone in.

"Want to come with us?" If you have some social power, this is the best use of it.

Try itFour moments. What's your move?

Pick what you'd actually do.

The prom group has one spot too many. In a side chat, someone suggests "just not telling Maya" the plan changed.

A friend screenshots your private message about another girl and sends it to her.

You're running for class president. Your group starts calling you "try-hard" and stops inviting you.

The guys' chat has a running bit about who's the most "soft." This week it's Dev, and he wasn't told about the pickup game.

You won't always get it right. Doing it once is more than most people do.

In a groupPick a corner. Then see if your guess was right.

For advisory or class · about 15 minutes · no materials needed

  1. The leader reads a statement. Students move to or point at a corner: agree, disagree, not sure, or it depends.
  2. Ask one or two people from different corners to explain why. No one has to share.
  3. After each one, the leader taps to show what the research says.
Four corners · 1 of 4"Girls are meaner than boys."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Not really. Across 148 studies, girls used relational aggression only slightly more than boys, and boys used more physical aggression. Both do both.

Four corners · 2 of 4"The most popular kids are the most liked."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Often not. In a study that followed students from 5th to 12th grade, students who used exclusion and rumors were seen as more popular and liked less. The gap was widest for girls, and it showed up for boys too.

Four corners · 3 of 4"Talking it over every night with your best friend helps."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

It depends. Rehashing the same problem builds closeness and also predicts more anxiety and low mood. Venting helps most when it leads somewhere: a plan, a laugh, or a change of subject.

Four corners · 4 of 4"If you stand up for someone, you'll lose your spot."

Agree, disagree, or not sure? Vote first.

What the research says

Sometimes there's a cost. But in recorded recess observations, peer interventions stopped bullying 57% of the time, and kind, trustworthy students are the ones peers like most.

Talk about itThree questions for the group.
  • Why might someone trade being liked for being powerful?
  • What's the difference between a close group and a closed group? Which is ours, as a grade?
  • What did your family or community teach you about loyalty, and about disagreeing with a friend?
Tip for the person leading

Keep it about situations, not named students or groups. Someone in the room may be living this right now. If the talk turns to "girls are like this," bring in the research: everyone does this, and most people don't like it.

If you're on the outsideThis year is not the whole story.
Groups keep shifting.
Friend groups reshuffle a lot in high school, and again after it. Where you sit this semester isn't a ranking of who you are.
One good friend counts.
You don't need a crew. One person who's glad to see you makes a real difference.
Mute the highlight reel.
Watching their stories at midnight is staring at the rock.
Say it to an adult.
A counselor, coach, parent or caregiver, or another adult you trust. They can help quietly.
If you've been the one doing itPower you didn't ask for is still power.

Plenty of people end up in charge of a group without planning to. Often it starts with being scared of being the one left out. Noticing it is the hard part.

Change one thing.
Add them back. Tell them about the plan. End the side chat.
Keep the apology short.
"I left you out, and that wasn't okay. I'm sorry." No explaining it away.
Use the power differently.
The person who decides who's in can also decide everyone's in.

When it crosses a line

  • Someone is sharing photos of a person, real or AI-made, or private information about them.
  • A group is threatening someone, or it's been going on for weeks.
  • Being left out has you dreading school, not eating, or not sleeping.
  • You have thoughts of hurting yourself.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult today. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night. If someone is in danger right now, call 911.

What this might mean

Being left out hurts, and your brain is built to notice it. It says more about how the group is working right now than about what you're worth.

What might help right now

  • 🧍Reach out to one person today, inside or outside the group.
  • 📵Mute the stories and the chat for tonight.
  • 🧑‍🏫Tell a counselor or trusted adult, especially if it keeps happening.

What this might mean

Being pulled between friends is exhausting. You're allowed to like more than one person.

What might help right now

  • 🧭Try: "I like you both. I'm not picking."
  • 🤐Don't carry messages back and forth.
  • 🗣Talk it over with someone outside the group.

What this might mean

Most people go along the first few times, usually because they're scared of being next. It doesn't lock you in.

What might help right now

  • 🛡Check on the person it was about.
  • 💬Find one friend who felt the same.
  • 🎯Pick your line for next time, so it's ready.

What this might mean

What might help right now

  • 🔄Change one thing: add them back, save a seat, stop the joke.
  • 💬Keep the apology short: "I left you out, and that wasn't okay."
  • 🤔Ask yourself what you were afraid of. A counselor can help you sort it out.
