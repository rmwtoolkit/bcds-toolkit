Part 1 · Understand it

Why new takes longer than it should

Sound familiar?You know where to go now. You don't know who to sit with.
  • You transferred in, and everyone else has had years to form their groups.
  • You moved from another state or another country, and the unwritten rules here are different.
  • Your old friends are at a different school now, and the group chat is getting quieter.
  • You're one of very few students here who share your background, your faith, or your home language.
  • You've had good conversations with people in class, but nobody's turned into an actual friend yet.
  • Everyone's stories make it look like they found their people on day two.

Being new is hard at any age. College, a first job, and a new city all bring it back. What follows is what the research says about the part nobody explains: how long it really takes, and what speeds it up.

Friendship runs on hours

Communication researcher Jeffrey Hall surveyed adults who had just moved to a new city and first-year college students making friends from scratch. His estimate: about 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 to become friends, and more than 200 for a close friend.

Counts a lotHanging out, joking around, gaming, real conversations about each other's lives.
Counts lessSitting near each other in class or working side by side. Small talk alone was linked to drifting apart.

So if it's October and you don't have a group yet, that's arithmetic, not a verdict on you.

The researchPeople like you more than you think.
The liking gap.
In a 2018 set of studies, people who had just talked with someone new consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them. It showed up in first-year college students getting to know their roommates, and it lasted for months. After that awkward conversation, you probably came across better than you think.
Strangers want to talk more than you'd guess.
When commuters were asked to talk to a stranger on a train or bus, they reported a more pleasant ride than people who sat alone, even though others predicted the opposite. The researchers traced the mistake to underestimating how interested other people are in connecting.
Time isn't enough on its own.
In Hall's study, closeness grew with real conversation, joking, and hanging out, not just with being around each other. A whole semester next to someone in class can stay at "hey."
"Do I belong here?"The question is normal, and it fades.

In a 2011 study, first-year college students read short stories from older students: worrying about whether you belong is common at first, and it fades. Over the next three years, the Black students who got that message earned higher grades, cutting the racial achievement gap in half. It was a small study, so researchers repeated it in 2023 with almost 27,000 students at 22 colleges. A version under 30 minutes helped more students finish their first year full time, especially in groups that had historically struggled more, but only at schools that actually offered those students ways to belong.

If you're one of few students here from your background, the "do I belong" question can be louder. That isn't proof you're in the wrong place. It's also fair to expect the school to make room for you. You don't have to leave your home culture at the door, and finding even one club, affinity group, or adult who shares part of your background can help.

OnlineGroups look locked in. They aren't.

Group chats form fast. Stories show people who seem to have a crew by the first Friday. Location sharing shows clusters of classmates you weren't with.

The worst part: online, the first weeks look settled. They're not. At 50 hours a friendship, almost nobody has a real group yet, and groups keep shifting all year.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Speed up the math

What actually helps

More hours, better hours.

Tap each one.

1 Join something that meets every week.

A team, a club, a show, a job, a faith group. Repeated time with the same people is the hours problem solved for you.

2 Turn class time into hangout time.

Make a specific, easy ask: "want to study for the chem test at the library Thursday?" Specific is easier to say yes to than "we should hang out sometime."

3 Go one question past small talk.

"What lunch do you have?" is a fine start. Then ask a real follow-up: what they did at their old school, what they're into, what they think of the new coach.

4 Assume they liked talking to you.

The liking gap says you're probably underestimating it. Act on the better guess: say hi again tomorrow.

5 Find one adult.

An advisor, coach, teacher, or counselor you feel okay with. One adult who knows your name makes a building feel smaller, and they often know which club or person would be a good fit.

6 Keep your old friends, not as a hiding place.

Old friends are a real support. Just keep some evenings open for the new hours to happen.

Try itBuild your first-month plan.

Pick a few. The point is hours, so choose things that repeat. Nothing is saved.

Try itTwo moments. What would you try?

Pick the move you'd try.

You transferred in as a junior. For three weeks you and Priya, your lab partner, have had good conversations in class. You've never seen her outside it.

Your old friends invite you out Friday. The robotics club you just joined has a team dinner the same night.

More than one answer works. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

What doesn't helpThese make the first months longer.
Comparing your month one to everyone's stories.

Stories show the highlights, not the awkward lunch tables.

Waiting to be invited.

Sometimes it happens. More often, the new person has to make the first small move.

Judging the whole school by October.

At 50 hours for a casual friend, it's too early to know.

Hiding who you are to fit in.

Adjusting to a new place is normal. Erasing your background, accent, or faith to blend in tends to cost more than it buys, and the friends you make that way only know part of you.

Not telling anyone it's hard.

Counselors and advisors expect this. Telling one of them is normal, not dramatic.

When to get more help

  • You dread school most mornings or feel sick before going.
  • After a couple of months, you still feel alone most of the time.
  • Someone is targeting or excluding you on purpose, including because of your race, religion, accent, or where you're from.
  • Your sleep, eating, or mood has changed a lot, or you feel hopeless. You can call or text 988 any time.

A counselor helps new students all the time. See Get help below.

Other ways in

New-school stories are everywhere because nearly everyone lives one. One of these might take you back to your first day of kindergarten.

📖 Read

Brown Girl Dreaming · Jacqueline Woodson, 2014

A memoir in poems about growing up between South Carolina and Brooklyn, and figuring out where she belongs in each place. National Book Award winner.

Heads up: Woodson's grandfather gets sick and dies.

The Kissing Hand · Audrey Penn, 1993 Throwback

Chester the raccoon is scared to start school, so his mom kisses his palm for him to carry. It's a way to bring old people with you while you meet new ones.

🎬 Watch

Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse · Sony Pictures Animation, 2018

Miles gets sent to an elite new school and would rather be with his Brooklyn friends. He spends much of the movie asking if he belongs. Rated PG.

Heads up: Two important characters die, including one early in the movie.

🎧 Listen

New Soul · Yael Naïm, 2008

A new soul in a strange world, learning how things work. The MacBook Air ad song.

🖼️ Look

American Born Chinese · Gene Luen Yang, 2006

Jin moves to a school where he's one of only two Asian students, and then Wei-Chen arrives. A Printz Award graphic novel about fitting in and what it costs.

Heads up: Jin faces racist bullying, and the book uses a deliberately offensive stereotype character to expose racism.

Use Hall's hours. Draw a bar for each person you've met here, and shade it in by how many real hangout hours you think you've had. Pick one bar to add to this week.

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
