Part 1 · Understand it

What the college process really is

Sound familiar?Everyone has an opinion about your future.
  • A relative asks where you're applying, then tells you where you should be applying.
  • Your feed in December is all early acceptances, and you're still waiting.
  • You signed up for a fourth AP because it "looks good," and you're exhausted.
  • You love a school you're not sure your family can afford, and nobody has said the number out loud.
  • Part of you isn't sure a four-year college right away is the right move, and you haven't said that out loud either.

You've heard "there's no single right school." This lesson gives you the evidence behind that, including the places where the honest answer is more complicated.

What you see online vs. the whole picture

Decision-day posts come mostly from a small group of very selective schools. There are far more four-year colleges than that. A 2019 Pew Research Center analysis of federal data looked at 1,364 of them.

Most colleges admit most of the people who apply. The stress you feel is real, but it's shaped by a narrow slice of schools that get most of the attention.

The researchDoes a more selective school pay off?
For most students, less than people think.
Economists Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger compared students who got into similar schools but chose differently. Once they accounted for where students applied and were admitted, the average earnings boost from attending the more selective school mostly disappeared.
But not for everyone equally.
The same research found a real benefit for Black and Hispanic students and for students whose parents had less formal education. Selective schools can open networks that some families already have and others don't.
At the very top, there's an effect.
A 2023 study by Raj Chetty, David Deming, and John Friedman found that attending an Ivy-Plus college, compared with a selective public flagship, raised the chances of reaching the top 1% of earners and of landing jobs at elite firms. Effects on average earnings were much smaller. Both things can be true: a few schools offer specific advantages, and most people do well at many kinds of schools.
What you do there matters a lot.
A 2014 Gallup and Purdue survey of about 30,000 graduates found that the type of college mattered little for later well-being and work engagement. What mattered was experience: graduates who had a professor who cared about them, made them excited to learn, and encouraged their goals had more than double the odds of thriving.
The pressure itselfPushing harder has a cost.

Psychologist Suniya Luthar has spent years studying students at high-achieving schools. Her research finds higher rates of anxiety, depression, and substance use than many people expect, tied partly to constant pressure to excel. Her work also points to what protects students: close, accepting relationships with adults and friends who value them apart from their achievements.

The goal isn't to stop caring. It's to keep the process from becoming the only thing you're allowed to care about.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Make the decision yours

Build a list on purpose

Fit has four parts. Prestige is not one of them.

Academic fitDo they teach what you want to learn, in a way you learn well? Class size, advising, research or co-op options.
Financial fitUse each school's net price calculator. The sticker price and what families actually pay can be very different. Talk numbers early.
Life fitDistance from home, size, setting, whether you'll find your people, how students like you describe it.
OddsA balanced list includes schools where you're likely to be admitted and would be glad to go. Those aren't backups. They're choices.
More than one road

Paths that work, and that people rarely mention.

Tap one to see more.

1 Community college, then transfer.

As of 2024, Massachusetts residents without a bachelor's degree can attend community college tuition-free through MassEducate. MassTransfer then offers credit transfer, and for qualifying grades, guaranteed admission and tuition credit at UMass and state universities in approved programs. Check current terms with a transfer counselor.

2 A gap year with a plan.

Work, service, or caring for family, with a clear next step. Many colleges let admitted students defer.

3 Trades, apprenticeships, the military.

Skilled trades and registered apprenticeships pay while you train. These are real careers, not consolation prizes.

4 Staying close to home.

For some students, commuting saves money or keeps them connected to family responsibilities they value. Going far away isn't the only version of growing up.

Try itSort what's yours from what isn't.

Check the ones you can actually influence. Notice where most of your worry has been going.

Not yours: admission rates, what a committee decides in a given year, who else applies, what other people post. Worry about those doesn't change them.

Try itThree real moments. What would help most?

Camila was deferred from her early decision school the same night her close friend got in. Her friend wants to celebrate together this weekend.

Darnell wants to go to school three states away. His parents want him to commute to a state university to save money and stay close. Every conversation turns into an argument.

Hana is a junior with four APs, two varsity sports, and a job. She's sleeping five hours a night and dropped the art class she loved because it "doesn't count."

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths about admissionsWhat the evidence says instead.
MYTH It's nearly impossible to get in anywhere.

More than half of four-year colleges admit at least two-thirds of applicants. It's hard to get into a small number of schools.

MYTH The name decides your future.

For most students, what they do in college predicts more than where they went. The exceptions are real, and worth knowing, but they're narrower than the hype.

MYTH More activities always look better.

A few commitments you care about and can talk about honestly usually say more than a long list you did for the list.

MYTH Talking about money is rude.

Money is one of the biggest parts of fit. Families handle these talks differently, but having the conversation early protects everyone from heartbreak in April.

When to get more help

  • Worry about the future is affecting your sleep, eating, or mood most days.
  • You feel like your life is over because of one grade, score, or decision.
  • Pressure at home about college is causing serious conflict, or you feel you can't be honest about what you want.

Your counselor can help with both sides: the logistics and the stress. If you'd be the first in your family to apply, ask about programs and fee waivers built for that. See Get help below.

A reminder

The research is clear on this much: for most people, what you do once you get somewhere shapes your life more than the name of the place. Choose a place where you can do that.

Other ways in

Is any of this making you feel hopeless, unsafe, or like you might hurt yourself?

Thank you for being honest. Please don't carry this alone. Feeling hopeless or unsafe is a reason to talk to someone today.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time.
  • Text HOME to 741741, the Crisis Text Line.
  • Tell a counselor, teacher, coach, or parent today. You can show them this screen.
