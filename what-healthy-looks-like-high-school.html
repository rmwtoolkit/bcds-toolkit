What Healthy Looks Like

What's going on

Healthy isn't the absence of problems. Symptoms and well-being are two separate scales. You can have no diagnosis and still feel flat, or have real symptoms and still be connected and growing.

Part 1 · Understand it

What healthy actually is

Sound familiar?You know what to stop. What are you aiming for?
  • You can list what's wrong with you in detail. Asked what's going well, you go blank.
  • You figure that if you're not depressed or anxious, you're fine. But fine feels flat.
  • Your friend group runs on drama, and a quiet week makes you restless.
  • You assume healthy people don't fight, don't cry, and don't need help.
  • Nobody has really shown you, up close, what handling hard things well looks like.

Most advice is about what to stop. This lesson is about what to aim for, and what research says healthy looks like when you get close enough to see it.

Two separate scales, not one

Flourishing

Not happy all the time. Mostly engaged: you feel a sense of purpose, you belong somewhere, and you're growing. In a national sample of 1,234 people ages 12 to 18, flourishing was the most common category for 12 to 14 year olds.

Languishing

No diagnosis, but life feels hollow. It matters: in Keyes's study of 3,032 adults, people who were languishing were nearly six times as likely as flourishing people to have a major depressive episode.

Coping and growing

You can have anxiety, depression, or ADHD and still build real well-being. Treatment and a good life aren't either-or.

Struggling

Symptoms are high and life feels low. This is the clearest signal to get support. See Get help.

By ages 15 to 18 in Keyes's youth study, "moderately healthy" had overtaken flourishing as the most common result. As well-being went up, depression symptoms and problems like skipping school went down, and closeness to others went up. High school is when this starts to need attention on purpose.

Look where you want to goYou steer toward what you look at.

Mountain bikers learn it the hard way: stare at the rock and you hit it. Look at the line you want, and the bike follows your eyes. Driving research backs this up. In a 1994 study, drivers looked at a specific point on the inside of each curve one to two seconds before they turned into it. Where they looked came first. Steering followed.

If all you can picture is what you're trying to avoid, it's hard to steer anywhere else. So picture the trail.

What healthy minds tend to doIt looks more ordinary than you'd think.
They bend.
Psychologists Todd Kashdan and Jonathan Rottenberg argue that flexibility, shifting strategies when one stops working and acting on your values even when it's uncomfortable, is central to health. Rigid is the risk, not sad.
They name feelings precisely.
People who can tell "disappointed" from "embarrassed" from "resentful" are less likely to turn to binge drinking, aggression, or self-harm when they're upset, according to a 2015 research review. Precise words point to precise fixes.
They talk to themselves like a friend would.
A 2018 analysis of 19 studies with 7,049 people ages 10 to 19 found a strong link between self-compassion and lower anxiety, depression, and stress. The studies show a link, not proof of cause, but it's one of the more consistent findings in teen research.
They repair.
Healthy friendships still have fights. Someone goes back, names what happened, and fixes what's fixable.
The window of tolerance

Healthy is a range, not a spot.

Psychiatrist Dan Siegel calls it the window of tolerance: the zone where you can feel something strongly and still think.

Above the windowRacing, flooded, snapping at people. Your body is on high alert.
In the windowYou feel it, and you can still choose what to do next.
Below the windowNumb, flat, checked out. Your body hit the brakes.
Boundaries

Build a gate, not a wall.

A wallNobody gets in. It feels safe, and it gets lonely.
A gateYou decide who comes in, and when. "Yes" and "not right now" both feel okay to say.
No fenceAnyone walks through, any time. It's exhausting.
Healthy across culturesHealthy isn't one family's version.

Families and cultures differ in how much they share, how loud disagreements get, how much privacy is normal, and how much you're expected to put family first. None of those is automatically healthier than another. A close family that eats dinner together every night can be just as healthy as one where everyone does their own thing.

A more useful test works anywhere: can people disagree and stay connected? Can you say what you need and be heard, in whatever way your family does that?

If calm feels strange because you're used to yelling or silence, strange isn't the same as unsafe. Often it means you're somewhere new.

Part 2 · Practice the skills

Steering toward it

Measure the right thing

Track what you want more of, not only what you want less of.

Keyes measures well-being in three parts. Ask yourself one question for each.

EmotionalHow often this week did I feel interested, calm, or glad to be where I was?
PsychologicalAm I getting better at something, and does what I do feel like it means something?
SocialDo I belong somewhere, and do I add something to it?

Ways to steer. Tap to see how.

1 Name the rock, then the trail.

Write what you want to stop. Then turn it into something you can do instead.

2 Make your picture boring.

Skip "I'd be happy." Try "I'd eat breakfast before I check my phone." Ordinary details are easier to steer toward.

3 Take small turns.

Change is a lot of one-degree turns over a long distance. A big swerve tends to put you in the ditch.

4 Borrow a picture.

Think of someone who handles hard moments in a way you respect: a coach, a teacher, a grandparent, a friend. Watch how they disagree and make up.

5 Missed a turn? Turn again.

You don't have to start over. Look up, find the trail, and turn again.

Try itTurn "stop doing that" into "try this."

Most goals start as a rock. Turn one into a trail you can actually do this week.

"Stop procrastinating."
Start each assignment for five minutes the day I get it.
"Stop worrying so much."
Do one thing today that worry tells me not to do.
"Stop hanging out with people who drain me."
Notice who feels steady, and spend more time there.
Try itPicture an ordinary good day. Then steer there.

Imagine a normal school day three months from now, when things are going well. Pick one of the three parts above (emotional, psychological, social) and make it specific.

Try itFour situations. What's the healthy move?

Pick what you think, and see why.

Lena and her friend argued yesterday. Today the friend texts, "we good?" Lena is still annoyed.

Omar's family expects him home for dinner most nights. His friends tease him about it.

Chloe isn't sad and isn't anxious. Nothing is wrong, exactly. Nothing feels interesting either.

A friend keeps asking to copy your homework. You said no, and now you feel selfish.

There's usually more than one good answer. The best one is the one you'll actually do.

Myths about healthyWhat people assume, and what's true.
MYTH Healthy means happy all the time.

Healthy people feel the full range, including sad, mad, and worried. They come back to the window sooner.

MYTH No diagnosis means I'm fine.

That's only one of the two scales. Languishing is real and worth fixing.

MYTH Healthy friends don't fight.

They fight about the issue, not each other's character, and then they repair.

MYTH Needing help means I'm not healthy.

Knowing when to ask is part of the definition.

MYTH My feed shows what normal looks like.

Drama and perfection get clicks, so feeds show more rocks than trails. Research on social media and well-being is mixed, so the useful question is personal: how do you feel after?

If it gets heavy

  • You feel hopeless, or like nothing will ever get better.
  • Things at home or with a friend or partner feel unsafe.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be here.

Tell a counselor or a trusted adult right away. You can also call or text 988, any time, day or night.

Other ways in

Flourishing looks pretty ordinary up close. These get it.

📖 Read

There's a Name for the Blah You're Feeling: It's Called Languishing · Adam Grant, The New York Times, 2021

Grant borrowed Corey Keyes's word for the space between depressed and flourishing, and it became one of the most-read Times pieces that year. A short read on the two-scale idea.

Where the Wild Things Are · Maurice Sendak, 1963 Throwback

Max gets furious, sails off, has his rumpus, and comes home to find his supper still hot. Out of the window and back in. Read it again with that in mind.

🎬 Watch

The Secret Life of Walter Mitty · Ben Stiller, 2013

Walter isn't sick. He's careful, stuck, and zoning out into daydreams, which looks a lot like languishing. Then a missing photo sends him out into the world. Rated PG for some crude comments, language and action violence.

🎧 Listen

The Middle · Jimmy Eat World, 2001

Written after a fan wrote in about being shut out by a clique. "It just takes some time." Healthy isn't a place you arrive at.

🖼️ Look

Humans of New York · Brandon Stanton, 2013

Street portraits of ordinary people with a few lines in their own words. Healthy looks different across families and cultures, and here it is, face after face.

✏️ Make your own

Draw Keyes's two scales as a grid, like the one in the lesson. Put a dot where you'd be this week. Then draw an arrow toward flourishing and write one small thing on it you could do this week.
